## Class 1 – Understanding the True Ownership and Responsibilities of a Product Manager (25 July 2026)

The first class was quite different from what I had initially anticipated. I expected the discussion to begin with Artificial Intelligence and its applications, but instead, most of the session focused on understanding the actual role and responsibilities of a Product Manager.

Initially, many of us described a Product Manager as someone who connects business and technical teams, gathers requirements, or coordinates different stakeholders. However, our professor challenged these conventional definitions and introduced a much deeper concept: **ownership**. He encouraged us to look at a Product Manager as the CEO of the product. This perspective significantly changed my understanding of the role.

The cricket analogies used during the session made the concept of ownership particularly easy to understand. A captain receives recognition when the team wins, even if he does not score any runs. Similarly, even if the captain performs exceptionally well individually, a team defeat is still a defeat. I understood that a Product Manager must take responsibility for the overall product outcome rather than simply blaming engineering, marketing, or other departments when things go wrong. Although this level of accountability can be challenging, it is an essential part of the role.

Another interesting moment was when one of my classmates questioned whether Product Management is essentially a thankless responsibility. I could relate to this concern because similar situations can arise in professional environments. Our professor explained that dissatisfaction and challenges are part of almost every responsibility, whether in an organisation or in everyday life. However, that does not necessarily mean that the effort is meaningless. If someone consistently feels that their contribution is unappreciated, they should reconsider whether the role is right for them rather than continue working with frustration.

**My Key Learnings:**

- **Accountability and Responsibility:** Although tasks and responsibilities can be distributed among team members, the Product Manager cannot transfer accountability for the overall product outcome.
- **Influencing Without Authority:** A Product Manager frequently needs to collaborate with and influence people who do not directly report to them. I realised that this is a fundamental requirement of the profession.
- **Domain Understanding:** Product Management is much more than passing information between departments. Without a proper understanding of the product, its domain and its users, meaningful product decisions are difficult to make.

**My Personal Perspective:** I agree with the importance of ownership, although I feel that implementing it can be challenging in large organisations. Product Managers may be accountable for outcomes without having direct control over engineering resources, budgets or organisational decisions. Therefore, while taking ownership is essential, organisations should also provide Product Managers with sufficient authority and decision-making flexibility.

**How I Plan to Apply This Learning at Work:** In my current professional responsibilities, I have sometimes approached platform-related activities primarily from a coordination perspective. After this session, I want to become more conscious of the decisions and outcomes I am responsible for. I plan to distinguish between decisions that I can independently own and those that I tend to escalate unnecessarily. This will help me gradually develop a stronger sense of ownership and a more product-oriented mindset.


## Class 2 – Understanding the Product Life Cycle and Essential Product Management Artifacts (1 August 2026)

This session helped me understand the complete journey of a product, starting from discovery and definition, followed by strategy, planning, development, launch and growth, and eventually renewal or retirement. One important learning was that product development is not a one-way, linear process. It is iterative, involving continuous feedback, evaluation and improvements. A product rarely gets everything right in its first attempt.

Two concepts from this session particularly caught my attention.

**First, the concept of being a trustee.** A Product Manager is responsible for managing resources that belong to others, including the organisation's money, employees' time and people's professional efforts. This makes documentation and maintaining evidence important, not merely as administrative activities but as a way of demonstrating responsible ownership. During a discussion about decision gates and misaligned expectations, our professor explained that decisions which are inexpensive to postpone can be deferred, whereas fundamental decisions with long-term consequences should be taken early. For example, choosing the colour of a room can wait, but deciding whether to construct a house or purchase an apartment cannot. I realised that I have sometimes followed the opposite approach in my professional work by finalising technical choices early while postponing important discussions and alignment with stakeholders.

**Second, the concept of 'Good Enough'.** I learned that a product does not need to be absolutely perfect before it can be launched. Product Managers must understand when the solution has reached an acceptable level of readiness and make a timely launch decision. Our professor shared an interesting experience involving a card-processing product from the 1990s. The original plan was to complete development by April, conduct beta testing in May and launch in June. However, the CEO was not convinced by this timeline, and the actual launch eventually took place the following February. His key learning was that completing beta testing does not mean the entire journey is over; it is only halfway through the process. Teams must manage their energy and avoid exhausting themselves before reaching the final stages.

Another important distinction discussed was between a **project and a product**. A project is generally developed to fulfil the requirements of a particular customer, whereas a product is designed to serve a larger and potentially unknown group of customers. I found this distinction particularly relevant because it will influence how I approach our team assignment and evaluate whether we are solving a specific requirement or building something that can serve multiple users.

The session also introduced several important Product Management artifacts, including PRDs, discovery documents, roadmaps, launch briefs and PR/FAQs. My understanding is that these documents should be treated as adaptable guidelines rather than rigid formats. They should evolve according to the product's requirements. However, one point I found especially important was **traceability**. A decision is meaningful only when it is properly reflected throughout the subsequent stages of design, development, testing and user documentation. Merely recording a decision without ensuring its implementation does not create much value.

**My Personal Perspective:** I appreciate the discipline of maintaining a central repository of decisions and evidence, particularly the suggestion of using GitHub. However, in large corporate environments, important discussions and approvals are often distributed across emails, Microsoft Teams and other communication channels. I feel the challenge is to establish proper traceability without creating an additional documentation system that duplicates existing organisational processes.

**How I Plan to Apply This Learning at Work:** I want to become more systematic in documenting important technical and business decisions, identifying which decisions need immediate attention and which can be deferred. I also want to maintain better alignment between requirements, implementation and testing so that decisions are traceable throughout the product development life cycle.


## Class 3 – Using Product Management Frameworks as Decision-Making Tools (18 August 2026)

The third class began with two of my classmates revisiting the key learnings from the previous sessions before moving into an extensive discussion of different Product Management frameworks. We covered several approaches, including Jobs To Be Done, Double Diamond, Opportunity Solution Trees, Lean Canvas, Amazon's Working Backwards approach, PR/FAQ, Porter's Five Forces, PESTLE, SWOT, Ansoff Matrix, QFD, RICE, Kano Model, MoSCoW, RACI/RAPID, one-way and two-way door decisions, and OODA.

Although the number of frameworks initially seemed overwhelming, one message made the entire discussion easier to understand: **frameworks should act as instruments that support decision-making, not as substitutes for judgment.** Our professor explained that frameworks are like dials, not the steering wheel or the brakes. I understood this to mean that using more frameworks does not automatically result in better product decisions. If a second framework does not introduce new insights, improve the available evidence or influence the final decision, using it simply becomes an unnecessary exercise.

A few concepts from this session particularly stood out to me:

- **Be Specific About the Customer:** Instead of repeatedly using the generic term "user", we should identify the actual customer segment, such as a rider, driver, farmer or parent. This makes it easier to understand their needs, develop meaningful personas and identify the specific problems they want to solve.
- **Transformation vs. Improvement:** The example of making a caterpillar crawl faster versus enabling it to become a butterfly helped me understand the difference between incremental improvement and genuine transformation. I realised that organisations sometimes describe small operational improvements as major transformations. What matters is recognising the actual scale of change rather than relying on impressive terminology.
- **Products as Moving Targets:** Our professor compared product development to launching a missile towards a target that is constantly moving, while competitors are also trying to reach the same target. This analogy helped me understand why continuous evaluation and course correction are essential. Changing direction based on new information should not automatically be considered a failure.
- **A New Perspective on Scrum:** Having worked with Scrum practices for several years, I found this explanation particularly interesting. Our professor described a sprint as a commitment between the Product Manager and the development team. During the sprint, the team should receive stability and freedom from unnecessary changes. Once the sprint is completed, priorities can be reconsidered. He also explained that although a sprint can be cancelled, its scope should not be continuously changed midway. In highly dynamic environments, shorter sprints may be more appropriate, whereas relatively stable environments can accommodate longer sprint cycles. This gave me a different perspective on how sprint duration and team commitments should be managed.

Another interesting discussion towards the end of the session focused on **value capture and product economics**. We discussed the idea that a product builder may capture only around 15–25% of the total value created, with approximately 20% serving as a reasonable planning assumption. The remaining value is retained by customers and other participants in the ecosystem. This made me realise that creating significant customer value does not necessarily mean capturing an equivalent amount of revenue. Product economics must account for how value is distributed rather than assuming that the organisation can monetise the entire benefit it creates.

**My Personal Reflection:** One of the most memorable moments of the class was not related to any framework. Our professor shared his personal experience of returning from leave only to discover that his company had shut down. Instead of focusing entirely on his own situation, he prioritised helping his team members find suitable opportunities. I found this experience particularly meaningful because it highlighted the human side of leadership. Technical knowledge, frameworks and business decisions are important, but how a leader supports people during difficult circumstances is equally valuable.

**How I Plan to Apply This Learning at Work:** I want to become more selective when applying frameworks to professional problems. Rather than using multiple models simply because they are available, I will focus on identifying the actual problem, selecting the most relevant framework and using its insights to support a decision. I also want to pay greater attention to customer-specific requirements, changing priorities and the economic value generated by the solutions I work on.


## Class 4 – Understanding Where and When AI Can Create Meaningful Value (22 August 2026)

This was the session where our discussions became specifically focused on AI. The professor structured the discussion around a series of decision-making gates, helping me understand how to evaluate whether an AI solution is genuinely worth pursuing.

The first concept was **Gate 0 – Social Legitimacy**, which comes before evaluating technical feasibility. Before asking whether we can build something, we should question whether we have the right reasons and justification to build it. This includes examining consent, the involvement of vulnerable groups and whether the proposed solution depends on surveillance, manipulation or hidden human labour. Only after addressing these concerns should we evaluate its business value, technical feasibility and learning potential.

The next concept, which I found particularly relevant to my day-to-day work, was the **AI Appropriateness Ladder**. It presented four levels of solutions, arranged from the simplest to the most advanced:

1. **Deterministic Rules:** Simple if-then-else conditions.
2. **Retrieval, Analytics and Search:** Finding information and extracting insights from available data.
3. **Predictive Machine Learning:** Using historical data to predict outcomes.
4. **Generative or Agentic AI:** Generating content, reasoning and carrying out tasks through advanced AI capabilities.

The main takeaway was to always start from the bottom of the ladder. If a straightforward rule can solve a problem, there is no reason to introduce a complex AI model. We should not choose a foundation model simply because AI is currently popular. The professor's example of a "nuclear-powered pencil sharpener" made this point very effectively. Just because something can be built does not mean it should be built. If sharpening a pencil costs ₹5,000, the solution is clearly disproportionate to the value it provides.

Another concept that stood out to me was the **Actionable Time Window**. The usefulness of information depends not only on its accuracy but also on whether it reaches someone early enough to take meaningful action. For example, a rain prediction delivered one minute before rainfall provides very little practical benefit. However, informing a farmer two days in advance gives enough time to arrange labour and plan the harvest.

The autonomous driving example reinforced this idea. A vehicle's safety depends on several factors working together: obstacle detection time, alert delivery time, human recognition, decision-making time, braking distance and an adequate safety margin. I realised that claiming a model is accurate does not necessarily prove that the overall product is reliable. The complete system must perform effectively in real-world conditions.

Two additional lessons from this session are particularly important to me:

- **Consider Tail Risk Rather Than Relying Only on Average Performance:** Even if a system performs well in most situations, a rare event with extremely serious or generational consequences cannot be ignored. A good average result does not automatically make such risks acceptable.
- **Human-in-the-Loop Must Involve Meaningful Human Judgment:** Human involvement can easily become a rubber-stamping exercise. The professor's lending example demonstrated a better approach: the human and AI evaluate applications independently, and only disagreements are escalated. This is more effective than presenting the AI's recommendation first and expecting the human to simply approve it.

The discussion about technology adoption was equally interesting. I understood that people do not necessarily resist change because they lack intelligence or awareness. Adopting a new solution involves genuine switching costs, such as employee retraining, disruption to existing workflows and uncertainty about future support. The example of a car delivering 100 kilometres per litre illustrated this perfectly. Although I would be attracted to such a vehicle, I would still want to know where its service stations are located. A product's value depends not only on its technical capabilities but also on the supporting ecosystem required to use it.

**My Personal Reflection:** The biggest learning from this session was that successful AI implementation begins with asking the right questions, not selecting the most advanced technology. I found the distinction between model accuracy and actual product effectiveness particularly valuable. It reminded me that AI solutions must be evaluated in the context of the decisions people need to make, the time available to act and the consequences of failure.

I also realised that technology adoption requires more than demonstrating superior performance. Unless a solution fits into existing workflows and addresses the practical concerns of its intended users, even an impressive technology may struggle to gain acceptance.

## Class 5 – Moving from AI Opportunities to Designing for Uncertainty (29 August 2026)

The session was divided into two parts. The first focused on identifying business opportunities where AI could create value, while the second explored how products should be designed to handle the uncertainty that comes with AI.

During the discussion on identifying opportunities, I learned to look for signals such as operational friction, gaps in trust, repetitive activities, decision-intensive processes and workflows that generate substantial amounts of data. However, identifying these signals is only the starting point. The most important question is: **Where is the evidence?** Our professor emphasised that we should not simply claim that an AI solution will improve a process. We must be able to demonstrate its value through experiments, measure the outcomes and document the results. I realised that validating assumptions with evidence is an essential part of product management.

The example of the intranet application was particularly interesting. A team wanted to develop a complete user interface for managing group, division and company structures. When the professor asked how frequently these structures changed, the answer was once every two or three years. He pointed out that a simple back-end update would be sufficient instead of building an entire interface with its associated testing and access-management requirements. This made me realise that automation should be driven by the actual business need. Not every manual activity deserves a technology solution, especially when the frequency and impact of the activity are low. **Materiality matters.**

The discussion around **Build vs Buy vs API/Subscribe** was especially relevant to my professional experience. Using an external API can accelerate development, but it also creates dependence on the provider and reduces our control over the underlying technology. Similarly, running an open-weight model within our own infrastructure may appear to be a build decision, but it is effectively a form of buying because the model may be freely available while operating and maintaining it still involves costs. Choosing between different AI platforms, such as OpenAI-style and Anthropic-style APIs, is also a strategic decision because it can influence future flexibility and vendor lock-in.

The second half of the session focused on designing AI products around uncertainty. These were my key takeaways:

- **Decide Whether AI Should Be Visible:** Showing users that AI is involved should be a deliberate product decision rather than an automatic design choice.
- **Handle Low Confidence Honestly:** When the system is uncertain, it should be able to flag the result, ask the user for clarification or clearly communicate that it does not have a reliable answer.
- **Plan for Fallbacks and Errors:** Alternative workflows and error communication should be considered during product design instead of being added only after problems occur.
- **Avoid False Confidence:** An interface that presents uncertain information as though it were completely reliable can create excessive user trust. Once users experience incorrect outputs, they may lose confidence in the entire product.

One observation from the professor particularly stayed with me. Traditional software development has established practices around writing test cases and measuring test coverage. However, similar discipline is not yet consistently followed in AI development. When developers or data scientists are asked to provide test cases for a generative AI feature, the response may simply be that they experimented with two prompts. I understood from this discussion that informal experimentation is not sufficient for a product that must deliver consistent results to real customers.

I also found the recommended prompt structure very useful: **Role, Context, Task and Constraints**. It provides a systematic way to define what an AI system is expected to do and the boundaries within which it should operate. The professor also questioned why chat interfaces do not routinely provide separate fields for these four elements, which would encourage users to structure their instructions more effectively.

An important distinction I took away is that a prompt embedded within a product is not merely a one-time instruction. It influences the product's behaviour and therefore forms part of the overall product experience and promise. It should be version-controlled and maintained with the same discipline as other product components.

**My Personal Reflection:** This session helped me understand that identifying an AI opportunity and successfully implementing it are two different challenges. A promising use case must first be supported by evidence, and the resulting solution must be designed to handle uncertainty rather than assume that every AI response will be correct. I particularly liked the discussion about vendor dependency and prompt versioning because both have practical implications for building and maintaining AI-based applications.

I also realised that reliability is not achieved simply by selecting a powerful model. It requires proper testing, clear fallback mechanisms, honest communication and continuous validation of the system's behaviour.

## Class 6 – Reimagining Possibilities and Understanding Customers Better (5 September 2026)

The class began with some administrative discussions around GitHub and topic submissions, but soon moved into concepts that I found much more relevant to product thinking and customer understanding.

One of the main themes was **the art of the possible**. Our professor explained that sometimes the biggest limitation is not the availability of technology but our awareness of what technology can achieve. For example, if I am unaware that computer vision can identify incorrectly labelled products, I may never consider building a product around that capability. Similarly, audiobooks were once economically impractical, but advancements in text-to-speech technology have made their production much easier and more affordable.

This made me realise that product innovation often begins with expanding our understanding of what is possible. The professor also shared an example of an organisation where one team would print, sign, scan and email a document, only for the receiving team to repeat the same process. It highlighted how organisations continue following processes that were originally introduced because of older technological limitations, even when those limitations no longer exist. We should periodically question whether existing workflows are still necessary or simply continuing out of habit.

I also found the professor's advice on writing a **Product Requirements Document (PRD)** very practical. Requirements should be expressed from the stakeholder's perspective, using statements such as "I, as a rider, want..." The language should reflect what the stakeholder needs rather than describe technical implementation. For instance, stating that the system must support 10,000 users is a technical specification, not a requirement expressed by a rider.

Another important point was the need for consistent terminology across the organisation. Once a team decides to use a particular term, such as rider, driver, right-giver or right-taker, that terminology should remain consistent across engineering, customer support, documentation and user manuals. I understood that even small differences in terminology can create confusion when multiple teams are working on the same product.

The concept of **Black Box Reframing** was another interesting learning. The idea is to retain the same inputs and outputs of an existing process while reconsidering how the internal steps can be redesigned. With newer technology, we may no longer need all the inputs that were previously required. For example, a process that earlier depended on 20 inputs might now work with only 12. Alternatively, we may combine three existing inputs with two additional inputs that were previously too expensive or difficult to collect. This approach encourages us to rethink established processes instead of simply automating them as they currently exist.

The second half of the session focused on **sentiment analysis**, which gave me a different perspective on understanding customer feedback. A simple thumbs-up or thumbs-down response, or even a general positive or negative sentiment score, may not provide enough information for a product manager to make meaningful decisions.

Our professor explained that an opinion can be understood through five elements:

1. **Object:** The product being discussed.
2. **Feature:** The specific aspect of the product being evaluated.
3. **Sentiment Value:** Whether the opinion is positive, negative or neutral.
4. **Opinion Holder:** The person expressing the opinion.
5. **Time:** When the opinion was expressed.

The context in which the opinion is expressed is also important. For example, a customer may say that a phone's camera is excellent in daylight but performs poorly at night. A single review therefore contains different opinions about the same feature under different conditions. It may also include feedback about several features from multiple people.

One example from the discussion particularly caught my attention. A review included the statement, "My mother was mad with me because I didn't tell her before I bought the phone." Although this statement does not evaluate any technical feature of the phone, it reveals something about the emotional significance of the purchase and the customer's personal circumstances. The professor pointed out that people generally do not have this kind of emotional reaction when purchasing something ordinary, such as ice cream.

I realised that analysing customer feedback only at the feature level can cause us to miss important human insights. Understanding the emotions and circumstances behind a purchase can sometimes reveal more about customer behaviour than a conventional sentiment score.

Finally, the session reinforced the importance of **data consent and privacy**. Our professor reminded us that organisations are custodians of customer data, not its owners. Regulations such as India's DPDP framework and the GDPR must be considered when designing products that collect or process personal information. Consent and responsible data usage cannot be treated as issues to address after development. An AI product that ignores these considerations risks losing customer trust and may not be viable from the beginning.

**My Personal Reflection:** This session helped me understand that innovation is not always about creating something entirely new. Sometimes it involves questioning existing processes, recognising capabilities that were previously unavailable and redesigning workflows around new possibilities. I particularly liked the discussion on sentiment analysis because it showed me why understanding the context behind customer feedback is just as important as identifying whether the feedback is positive or negative.

I also took away an important lesson about product documentation: requirements should reflect the stakeholder's actual needs, while technical specifications should describe how the system will meet them. This distinction can help reduce misunderstandings between business and technical teams.

This was our final session before the midterm examination, and almost the entire class was conducted through a live case study. I found this approach particularly effective because it allowed us to apply product management concepts to a realistic business situation rather than simply discussing them through presentation slides.

The case involved a company developing a customer-service product for electricity distribution companies (DISCOMs). Our role was that of a product company selling a solution to these organisations, not that of the electricity distribution company itself. As students proposed different approaches, the professor repeatedly challenged us to think beyond conventional textbook answers. Suggestions such as premium pricing and brand positioning were not necessarily relevant in this situation. A DISCOM does not purchase a customer-service platform for prestige or status; it expects a solution that addresses a genuine operational or financial problem.

The discussion became much more interesting when a classmate introduced the concept of **System Thinking**. Electricity distribution companies purchase power in advance through forward contracts. If a particular locality experiences a power outage, the company has already paid for electricity that it is temporarily unable to distribute and sell to customers. Therefore, identifying an outage and resolving it quickly is not merely about improving customer service. It can directly help protect the company's financial margins.

This changed how I understood the product's value proposition. Instead of presenting it as another call-centre improvement tool, we could connect its benefits to reduced financial losses and faster outage resolution. The business value became much easier to quantify.

However, the most interesting question raised during the case was: **Why should customers have to complain in the first place?** If the system can identify an unusual drop in electricity consumption across a locality, it may already have enough information to detect a possible outage. Rather than waiting for customers to call, the company could proactively notify them.

Even when a customer does contact the organisation, the system may already have access to useful information such as the customer's phone number, address and meter details. If the customer is away from home, their GPS location could also help identify the relevant issue. With these capabilities, many customer calls could potentially be avoided altogether.

I found this to be a powerful example of the difference between **optimising an existing process and reimagining the process itself**. Improving call-centre efficiency is one approach, but eliminating the need for certain calls is a much more fundamental transformation.

The pricing discussion also introduced an important business consideration. An outcome-based model, such as charging for every successfully resolved ticket, aligns payment with the value delivered. However, it also transfers some business risk to the product provider. The number of tickets may vary significantly between quiet periods and days with a large number of outages.

We also discussed per-seat pricing. This model can create an unusual incentive problem: as the customer improves its operations and requires fewer support agents, the provider's revenue may decrease. I realised that pricing models should not only generate revenue but also encourage the right behaviour and align the interests of both parties.

Another important framework from this class was **TFIR – Truth, Facts, Inferences and Recommendations**. It helped me understand how different types of information should be handled in an AI-based product.

- **Truth and Facts:** These can be made highly reliable through database lookups and tool-based retrieval rather than depending entirely on generated responses.
- **Inferences:** These are conclusions drawn from available information and should be supported by relevant data.
- **Recommendations:** This is the most sensitive layer because the system moves from presenting information to suggesting what someone should do. To make recommendations trustworthy, the product should expose the supporting evidence and reasoning so that a human can identify an incorrect assumption or faulty foundation.

I also noted several additional lessons that are relevant both for examinations and practical product development:

- **Third-Party Dependency and Supply-Chain Risk:** Build, buy and subscribe decisions involve risks beyond development cost. For example, if an OTP service provider experiences an outage, internet banking may stop functioning. Similarly, an AI vendor changing or discontinuing a model can affect an already launched product, even when our own application code remains unchanged.
- **AI Products Require Continuous Attention After Launch:** In traditional software, uncertainty generally reduces considerably after General Availability (GA). With AI products, it may reduce only gradually because external models, APIs and other dependencies can continue changing.
- **Product Managers Should Be Able to Demonstrate Their Own Products:** If a product manager cannot independently demonstrate the product and always needs someone else's assistance, it may indicate insufficient understanding of the solution.
- **Stay Close to Customer Support:** Spending time with the support team after launch helps product managers understand actual customer problems, recurring issues and gaps that may not be visible during development.

## Class 8 – Moving from Prototypes to Scale Through Validated Progress (3 October 2026)

This was our first session after the midterm examination. The class began with a topic that I had already encountered in my own work but had not previously understood in a structured way: **Provider Instability**.

Our professor distinguished between two different types of uncertainty. The first is the normal probabilistic behaviour of an AI model. For example, if a model produces incorrect results approximately 5% of the time, we would generally expect its performance to remain around that level under similar conditions. The second is provider-driven instability, where a vendor makes changes behind the scenes and the same prompt, testing setup and application suddenly start producing noticeably different results.

The professor shared an example of instructing an AI agent to continuously work towards a goal, yet the agent stopped after completing only one step. He connected this to a Kannada saying about a donkey moving only one step after each beating. Although the example was humorous, the underlying concern was serious. During development, we may be able to identify a problem and roll back changes. In production, however, the situation is more complicated because we may not even know which customers have already received unreliable responses.

Another concept that I found particularly relevant was **Assumption Drift**. Our professor explained how he had asked an AI system to test a process involving three banks. The system silently interpreted this instruction as meaning that there would always be exactly three banks. When the scenario was later changed to involve only one bank, the system failed.

This example showed me how easily AI can make assumptions that are not explicitly stated in the instructions. Such assumptions can remain unnoticed when product managers review outputs quickly and approve them without examining the underlying logic. The professor also warned that product managers can become bottlenecks when every decision requires their approval. Under pressure, they may simply respond with "fine, go ahead", allowing incorrect assumptions to become part of the product.

The central topic of the session was the progression from **Concept → Prototype → MVP → Pilot → Scale**. The important principle was that a team should earn the opportunity to move to the next stage by demonstrating that the current stage has achieved its objectives.

A prototype is primarily intended for learning, validating an idea and demonstrating how a solution might work. An MVP, on the other hand, should be usable by real customers so that their feedback can guide further development. We also discussed different types of prototypes, including concept, UI, data and workflow prototypes.

The discussion about **Wizard of Oz MVPs** was particularly interesting. In this approach, a human may secretly perform the work that customers believe is being handled by an automated system. Our professor made an important distinction: having a human in the loop is acceptable, but deliberately hiding the human involvement from customers is not. I understood that early-stage products can use manual assistance to validate demand and workflows, but they should remain transparent about how the service operates.

There were several practical lessons from this session that I want to apply:

- **Keep Vendor Dependencies Flexible:** AI products should be designed so that switching between providers is manageable. Since providers may offer different API structures and capabilities, such as OpenAI-style and Anthropic-style APIs, the architecture should avoid unnecessary dependence on a single vendor.
- **Define Success Criteria Before Starting:** A concise prototype or MVP brief should clearly establish the objective and expected outcomes. This prevents confusion later if the team believes the evaluation criteria have changed midway through development.
- **Validate Before Automating Completely:** Automation should be introduced in stages. When confidence in the system is still limited, manual fallback processes should remain available until the solution demonstrates sufficient reliability.
- **Keep Governance Proportionate:** The discussion included Jaydeep's observation about excessive ISO/CMM documentation. I understood that governance should improve quality, accountability and consistency rather than become a paperwork exercise with little practical value.
- **Build a Product, Not a Customer-Specific Project:** Our professor challenged the suggestion that a vendor should be prevented from selling the same solution to other customers. His recommendation was to develop a reusable core product and handle individual customer requirements through separate add-ons or configurations. This allows the solution to serve multiple organisations rather than becoming a one-customer project.

**How I Plan to Apply This Learning at Work:** I have already noticed situations in our chatbot-related work where the same prompt produces different responses under different conditions. This session made me realise that relying on informal observation or simply judging whether an answer looks correct is not enough. I want to maintain a small, fixed set of test cases and execute them again whenever the underlying model or service provider changes. Comparing the results will help identify behavioural changes more systematically and provide evidence before accepting a new version in production.

**My Personal Reflection:** The most important takeaway for me was that progress in AI product development should be based on validated learning rather than assumptions or pressure to launch quickly. Each stage, from prototype to scale, should demonstrate enough evidence to justify the next investment. I also realised that flexibility, transparency and proper testing are essential when building products that depend on external AI providers.

## Class 9 – Building Business-Trustworthy Metrics and Evaluation Methods (10 October 2026)

This session focused on evaluating AI products and selecting metrics that genuinely reflect business performance. The most memorable part for me was the live Excel exercise in which our professor created a loan payoff matrix to demonstrate why model accuracy alone cannot determine whether a solution is commercially successful.

The example involved a bank issuing a loan of ₹1 lakh with a 2.5% spread. If the borrower defaulted, the bank would lose the entire principal amount. By changing the values across the four possible outcomes in the matrix, the professor demonstrated that a model with more than 90% accuracy could still generate a financial loss, whereas a model with lower accuracy could actually be profitable.

The reason was simple but important: the financial impact of different prediction errors is not equal. A single bad loan could wipe out the profit earned from approximately 40 successful loans. This made me realise that a model's accuracy percentage does not tell us whether it is creating business value. We must understand the financial consequences of its correct and incorrect decisions.

During the discussion, Jaydeep suggested that recall would be the appropriate metric for this situation. However, the professor deliberately avoided recommending precision or recall as universally correct measures. Instead, he encouraged us to assign a monetary value to each cell in the matrix, understand who benefits and who bears the loss, and then evaluate the model from a business perspective.

I found both viewpoints valid, but I personally connected more with the economic approach as an MBA student. Converting model outcomes into financial terms makes the evaluation more practical and less dependent on selecting a metric that presents the model favourably. It also helps an organisation understand how much it should be willing to invest in improving model performance.

Several other lessons from the session were equally valuable:

- **Tail Risk Can Matter More Than Average Performance:** The professor discussed how severe wildfires in California contributed to major financial losses for insurance companies. The casino example also helped explain this concept: probability calculations may assume that a game can continue indefinitely, but in reality, a player or business can run out of money. A business must therefore consider whether it can survive extreme losses, not just whether its average outcomes look favourable.
- **Metrics Should Change as the Product Evolves:** A product may continue generating higher revenue even while new customer registrations are declining. If the same metrics are being monitored from launch without reconsideration, important changes in customer behaviour may go unnoticed. I understood that measurement should evolve with the product and its business priorities.
- **The Allocation of Error Costs Influences Behaviour:** The party responsible for the financial consequences of false positives and false negatives has a strong influence on how seriously those errors are addressed. The banking example demonstrated how fraud detection became a much stronger priority when regulatory requirements made banks accountable for the resulting losses.
- **Measure Behaviour Without Creating the Wrong Incentives:** If testers are rewarded only for the number of bugs they identify, they may be encouraged to withhold defects rather than report them immediately. This showed me that even a well-defined metric can produce undesirable behaviour if its incentives are not considered carefully.
- **AI A/B Testing Requires Additional Caution:** Evaluating AI products through A/B tests can be complicated because the external vendor may also be conducting experiments or changing model behaviour simultaneously. Therefore, an improvement in customer satisfaction scores, such as an increase from 4.5 to 4.8, should not automatically be treated as proof of success. We must examine sample size and statistical significance before drawing conclusions.

**One Area Where I Have a Slightly Different View:** Our professor recommended keeping the number of metrics small and initially focusing on three. I agree that too many metrics can make decision-making complicated. However, I believe that at least one of the three metrics for an AI product should explicitly measure quality or safety. Otherwise, teams may naturally prioritise reducing costs and improving speed while overlooking the reliability of the product and the consequences of incorrect outputs.

**How I Plan to Apply This Learning at Work:** In the context of finance chatbot development, I want to evaluate incorrect responses in terms of their actual business impact. Instead of reporting only an accuracy percentage, I would like to understand how a wrong answer could affect a customer's time, money or decision-making. This would make the evaluation more meaningful to business stakeholders and help justify investments in improving the system.

**My Personal Reflection:** This session changed my understanding of how AI performance should be measured. A model can look impressive from a technical perspective but still fail to deliver financial value. I realised that product managers must connect technical evaluation with business outcomes, understand the cost of different types of errors and ensure that the chosen metrics encourage the right behaviour.

## Class 10 – Scaling AI Products While Consistently Delivering on the Promise (17 October 2026)

The session began with an important reminder about the evaluation process: the grading would follow a strict bell curve, the average score would be around 70%, and late submissions would not be accepted. The discussion then moved to scaling AI products, where one statement from our professor particularly caught my attention: **If teams are discussing servers instead of the promises made to customers, they are missing an important part of their responsibility.**

Our professor explained that every product makes a promise to its customers. However, customer expectations become much higher when a product or feature becomes critical to their daily activities. For example, WhatsApp is available without a subscription fee, but users still become frustrated when the service is unavailable even for an hour. This demonstrates that customers judge a product based on the experience and reliability they have come to expect, not merely on what the terms and conditions formally guarantee.

To manage these expectations, the professor introduced the idea of a **Customer Promise Matrix**. The organisation should clearly define what it promises to each customer segment and monitor whether it is consistently delivering that promise. For example, Uber may set an expectation that a cab will arrive within three minutes. Monitoring only server uptime would not be enough; the actual customer-facing commitment must also be measured.

The central discussion of the session was the **Latency–Cost–Quality Trade-off**. Our professor emphasised that defining the acceptable balance between these three factors is a product management responsibility. For instance, a product manager might specify that an answer should be delivered within 45 seconds, meet a defined minimum quality level and remain within an agreed cost range.

Without clearly communicating these expectations, different teams may optimise for conflicting objectives. One team member may prioritise response speed and select a smaller model, while another may focus on answer quality and recommend a more expensive, advanced model. I understood that the product manager must establish acceptable boundaries so that technical decisions remain aligned with the product's overall promise.

I also found the professor's example of choosing **predictability over speed** interesting. Instead of returning an answer as quickly as possible, a few additional seconds could be used for a final clean-up process to ensure that response times remain consistent. This reinforced my understanding that the fastest possible response is not always the best customer experience.

Other important points from the session included:

- **Version Everything That Influences Product Behaviour:** Version control should cover not only application code but also data, models, prompts and customer segments. Rolling back an AI product may not always be straightforward because an earlier model version might have already been discontinued by the provider.
- **Plan for Rate Limits and Queues:** These challenges often become visible when a product gains popularity and usage increases. Organisations may need service tiers, fair-use policies and segment-wise monitoring. The professor used the example of Vande Bharat and passenger trains operating on the same railway track to explain how different service levels can be managed.
- **Manage Noisy Neighbours:** In multi-tenant systems, one customer's unusually high resource consumption can affect the performance experienced by other customers. This needs to be considered while designing shared infrastructure.
- **Optimise Model Selection and Operating Costs:** Smaller models should be used wherever they can deliver sufficient quality. Techniques such as model routing and caching can also help protect profit margins. However, caching must be designed carefully because reusing one customer's response for another could create privacy concerns.
- **Avoid Depending Entirely on Heroics:** Building and scaling AI products is a continuous effort rather than a one-time challenge. Teams should have the right people for different responsibilities instead of depending on emergency efforts or a few individuals to solve every problem. The professor used the distinction between "commandos" and "army" personnel to explain the importance of assigning people according to the nature of the work.

**My Personal Connection:** The discussion about rate limits reminded me of an issue I had encountered recently. A system reached its connection limit, and instead of placing new sessions in a queue, it rejected them immediately. This helped me understand the difference between two customer experiences: facing an immediate failure and waiting approximately 30 seconds for the service to become available. From a technical perspective, both situations may be related to resource constraints, but from a product perspective, they represent very different promises to the customer.

**Application of the Class Exercise:** Our professor asked us to identify situations where smaller models would be sufficient and where more advanced frontier models would be necessary. My understanding is that smaller models can handle relatively structured tasks such as classification, request routing, field extraction and fixed-format summaries. However, open-ended reasoning, multi-step planning and customer-facing tasks involving significant legal or financial consequences may require more capable models, supported by appropriate human review.

**My Personal Reflection:** The biggest takeaway from this session was that scaling is not simply about increasing infrastructure capacity or handling more requests. It is about maintaining the experience and reliability that customers expect as usage grows. I also realised that product managers must make deliberate decisions about speed, quality and cost instead of allowing individual teams to optimise these factors independently. Ultimately, successful scaling means delivering the same product promise consistently, even as the number of customers and the complexity of operations increase.

## Class 11 – Building Responsible AI Through Ethics, Privacy and Practical Governance (24 October 2026)

The session began with an introduction to the new lab, Prayogshala, along with some guidance from our professor on its intended use. He explained that it would be particularly useful for traditional machine learning model development, rather than agentic product development or embedding generative AI into products. The discussion then moved towards ethics, bias, privacy and responsible AI practices.

One concept I found especially easy to understand was the professor's explanation of **Bias**. He gave the example of a child who has only encountered Gurkha security guards and therefore assumes that every security guard must be a Gurkha. The child is not deliberately making a biased judgment; the conclusion comes from limited exposure. AI systems can develop similar patterns when they are trained on incomplete or unrepresentative datasets.

I understood that bias is not necessarily introduced intentionally. It can originate from the data collected, the way information is labelled, organisational policies or the workflows through which decisions are made. Recognising these sources is an important first step towards reducing unfair outcomes.

When Gautam asked whether there was one universally accepted framework for managing bias, our professor explained that no single framework could be treated as a complete solution. Organisations should at least comply with applicable laws and industry guidelines while continuously trying to improve their practices. This is important because guidelines themselves may sometimes reflect outdated assumptions or existing biases.

The next topic was **Privacy and Responsible Data Collection**. The professor's recommendation was straightforward: collect only the minimum information that can be justified if a regulator questions why it was needed. For example, if a product does not require a customer's age, gender or even name, there may be no reason to collect those details. Information that is not collected cannot later be used by an AI system for making decisions.

He also emphasised that organisations must clearly communicate the purpose of data collection and obtain appropriate consent. Health-related information and children's data require additional care because of their sensitive nature. Under India's DPDP framework, and under the stricter requirements of GDPR, organisations must treat personal data responsibly. The professor reinforced the idea that we are custodians of customer data, not its owners, and that deletion requests must be handled in accordance with applicable requirements.

His statement that **"data is a drug"** was particularly memorable. It reflects how organisations and development teams often develop a tendency to collect more and more information, even when they cannot clearly justify its purpose. I realised that responsible product development requires discipline in deciding what information is genuinely necessary.

We also discussed practical ways to control **AI Hallucinations**. Some of the approaches included:

- **RAG Grounding:** Connecting the model's responses to relevant and reliable information sources.
- **Restricting Available Choices:** Limiting the possible outputs or options the model can select from when the task requires controlled responses.
- **Answer, Abstain or Escalate:** Designing the product so that it can provide an answer when sufficiently confident, decline to answer when necessary or refer the matter to a human for further review.

I found the third approach particularly useful because it changes how we think about AI reliability. A system should not be forced to produce an answer in every situation. Abstaining can be a responsible outcome. It can also support fair pricing, since a business may decide not to charge customers when the system cannot provide a useful answer.

The discussion on **Governance** gave me two principles that I consider especially important:

- Governance mechanisms must develop and scale faster than the number of AI agents being deployed.
- Committees may provide recommendations, but the product owner remains accountable for the final outcome.

Our professor also warned against turning governance into a formal exercise that exists only for documentation. If the people involved cannot explain the practical value of a review process, it risks becoming governance theatre. Reviewers must also receive sufficient time and recognition for their responsibilities. If reviews are treated as unpaid additional work, people may simply approve submissions without examining them properly.

For **Risk Assessment**, I learned that evaluating probability and severity alone is not enough. We should also consider whether a potential problem can be detected and whether its consequences can be reversed. The professor further highlighted the importance of preparing an incident communication plan. His example of informing people that they were safe if they had not received an email by 7 a.m. demonstrated how carefully designed communication can prevent unnecessary panic and confusion.

**Application to Our Project:** We were asked to include a dedicated section explaining the data our product collects, how consent is obtained, how long information is retained and which regulator or regulatory requirements apply to the relevant industry. We also needed to prepare a launch-gate checklist that distinguishes minimum requirements from conditions that would prevent the product from being launched. The emphasis was on making these points specific to our own product rather than writing generic statements about responsible AI.

**My Personal Reflection:** One small but useful observation from this session was the professor's repeated instruction to avoid using the word *user* without identifying the actual person or role. I have started noticing how frequently I use expressions such as "business users" in my own documents. In reality, I should be more precise about whether I am referring to a finance executive, an administrator, a customer-support representative or another specific stakeholder.

Overall, this session helped me understand that responsible AI is not limited to avoiding bias or complying with privacy regulations. It requires deliberate decisions about data collection, transparency, error handling, accountability and risk throughout the product lifecycle. Governance should protect customers and the organisation while remaining practical enough to support innovation.

## Class 12 – Strengthening Project Documentation, Technical Diagrams and Monetization Strategy (31 October 2026)

The first part of the session involved a live review of one team's project submission. Although the feedback was directed at a particular team, I found it useful because the same principles could be applied to every project.

The most important message was that a **Product Requirements Document (PRD) should be written with the seriousness of a contract** being handed to an external vendor, especially when our own money is at stake. Vague expressions such as "accurate", "user-friendly", "reduction in time", "unsafe output" or "high risk" leave too much room for interpretation. A vendor may interpret these terms differently from what the customer actually expects.

Our professor recommended replacing vague descriptions with measurable expectations. For example, instead of simply asking for a reduction in processing time, we should specify a target such as 30%. He also suggested maintaining a separate column for target values so that the numbers can be updated later without changing the underlying requirement.

Another important point was to avoid hard-coded assumptions that could restrict future product development. For example, designing a system around a fixed list of only 12 medicines would make it difficult to accommodate additional medicines later. Similarly, workflows should be documented clearly from the beginning, even if some details are refined after the pilot.

The PRD should also identify exactly where AI is involved in the workflow. For each AI-based step, we should consider its expected reliability, operating cost, how uncertainty will be communicated and whether human intervention is required. I understood that merely mentioning the use of AI is not sufficient; its role and responsibilities within the overall process must be clearly defined.

One practical suggestion from the professor that I intend to follow is to **submit the PRD to an AI system and ask whether it has enough information to build the proposed product.** We should also ask it to identify the questions or clarifications it would need before development could begin. If the document leaves the AI with several unanswered questions, an actual vendor is likely to face the same difficulties. This can help identify documentation gaps before the development process starts.

The session also introduced me to **PlantUML and Mermaid**, which allow diagrams to be created using text-based descriptions. We discussed swimlane diagrams, where each lane represents a specific participant or component, such as a patient, doctor, system or AI. This makes it easier to understand responsibilities, interactions and hand-offs across a workflow.

I found the ability to maintain diagrams as text particularly useful because they can be tracked through Git and compared using version-control differences. Our professor also introduced the **C4 Model** for visualising software architecture. He explained that product managers generally need a good understanding of the system context and container levels rather than getting involved in every lower-level technical detail.

Another useful recommendation was to prepare project submissions in Markdown for easier AI processing while also providing PDF versions for human readers. This highlighted the importance of presenting the same information in formats that are suitable for both automated tools and people.

The second half of the session focused on **Monetization and Product Economics**. These were my key takeaways:

- **Target Costing:** The Japanese approach to target costing starts with the price a customer is willing to pay. After deducting the desired profit margin, the remaining amount becomes the maximum allowable cost of developing and delivering the product. This is different from simply calculating the cost first and adding a profit margin afterwards.
- **Customers Pay for Outcomes, While Vendors Manage Inputs:** Customers are generally interested in the results they receive, whereas vendors incur costs through resources and inputs. Customers may prefer outcome-based pricing, while vendors often favour input-based arrangements such as Time and Material (T&M). A provider that can effectively manage the risks associated with delivering outcomes may have an advantage in winning business.
- **Account for Inference Costs at Higher Usage:** A successful AI product may attract heavy usage, but increased usage can also increase inference expenses significantly. Therefore, pricing should be stress-tested against high-volume customers rather than calculated only using average usage.
- **Retain Low-Usage Customers Through Flexible Pricing:** Organisations should not automatically treat customers with low usage as unprofitable relationships. Offering a lower-priced plan or downgrade option may help retain them instead of losing them completely.
- **Architecture Must Consider Business Economics:** The professor shared Oracle-related examples to demonstrate how licensing costs influence technical architecture. In one case, database calls were routed through a single module because licensing was charged per connection. In another, databases were consolidated because the licensing cost depended on CPU cores. These examples reinforced his view that architects should understand financial implications, not just technical requirements.
- **Understand Contribution Margin and Unit Economics:** Product profitability should be evaluated across individual workflow paths and customer segments. A product manager should be able to explain the unit economics clearly and concisely, even in a short discussion with a potential investor.
- **Platforms and Ecosystems Create Network Value:** Products that operate as platforms can become more valuable as additional participants join the ecosystem. Their competitive advantage may therefore come from the combined network rather than from the product alone.

**My Personal Connection:** The discussion around Oracle licensing was particularly familiar to me. In my experience with data platforms, licensing costs and connection limits can directly influence how database access and integrations are designed. I have seen situations where financial constraints become a stronger factor in architecture decisions than technical preference. This helped me connect the professor's explanation of architecture and economics with practical technology environments.

**My Personal Reflection:** This session helped me understand that product management requires equal attention to documentation, technical structure and financial sustainability. A clearly written PRD reduces ambiguity during development, diagrams make workflows easier to communicate, and monetization decisions determine whether the product can remain commercially viable. The biggest lesson for me was that a product should not only solve a customer problem effectively; its delivery model, architecture and pricing must also make business sense.

## Class 13 – Learning to Present an AI Product as a Viable Business (14 November 2026)

This was the first day of project presentations, and sir approached each presentation as though the teams were pitching to venture capitalists rather than simply demonstrating a product. One important instruction was that feedback given to one team should be treated as relevant to everyone. We were expected to create a GitHub issue for each applicable point in our own project repository and close it with a comment if it did not apply.

The key areas of feedback that I identified, and which I also need to consider for my team's project, were:

- **Clear swimlane diagrams:** The workflow should identify each role separately, mark AI activities as AI1, AI2, and so on, and clearly indicate where human intervention is involved. It is equally important to show the checkpoints at which AI outputs are reviewed or approved before reaching the final recipient.
- **AI usage across three pillars:** We should explicitly explain whether AI is being used to prepare product management artefacts, support the development process, and operate within the actual product.
- **Ownership of AI costs:** The business model must clarify who pays for API consumption. Does the customer provide and pay for their own API key, or does the vendor include and absorb these expenses?
- **Separate business and customer-value calculations:** I understood that two different financial perspectives must be presented. The first is the startup's business plan, covering development costs, expected customer numbers, revenue, and profitability year by year to establish the investment opportunity for a VC. The second is the customer's return on investment, comparing the price paid with the savings or value received. Several teams, including those with promising products, were combining these two calculations instead of explaining them independently.
- **Establishing a reasonable value-sharing model:** A customer generally shares around 18–25% of the value generated with the product provider. This means the customer would typically expect savings worth approximately three to four times the product's price. Where reliable industry benchmarks are unavailable, this can serve as an initial assumption.
- **Testing the financial assumptions:** We should challenge our calculations with alternative scenarios. For example, if the same work could be outsourced to a country with lower labour costs, would purchasing our product still make financial sense for the customer?
- **Conditional decisions after pilots:** The outcome of a pilot need not be restricted to complete approval or outright rejection. We should also consider a conditional go-ahead, where the product can proceed after specific improvements or conditions are addressed.
- **Distinguishing a product from a project:** Sir strongly emphasised that something developed exclusively for one organisation should not automatically be called a product. A genuine product must be capable of serving a broader set of customers rather than remaining a customised solution for a single organisation.
- **Evaluating all four pillars:** A product's viability must be examined through domain understanding, technological feasibility, regulatory requirements, and consumer behaviour, including both the positive and negative motivations that influence adoption.

One particularly interesting part of the session was sir's decision to use the Idea Hub team's product to evaluate all the GitHub submissions, describing the approach as "dog eat dog food". The team could make adjustments to suit the course requirements, but they were not allowed to customise the product separately for individual teams. Doing so would turn the solution into a project rather than a reusable product. I found this to be a practical demonstration of what product generalisation actually means.

**My personal reflection:** The pricing and financial discussions made me recognise a gap in my own understanding. I am reasonably comfortable estimating technical expenses such as token consumption and infrastructure costs, but I have less experience explaining the customer's return on investment in a convincing manner. I need to improve my ability to connect the product's price with measurable customer savings and business benefits. This is an important area I want to strengthen as I develop my product management perspective.

## Class 14 – Strengthening Product Positioning, Economics and Customer Value (21 November 2026)

The second day of project presentations was another extended session, conducted partly while sir was travelling. I felt that this reflected his commitment to reviewing our work. Compared with the previous session, the feedback was more direct and specific, which made it particularly valuable as a learning experience.

Several important themes emerged repeatedly during the presentations:

- **Aim for transformation rather than minor improvements:** For the school admissions product, sir questioned why people should still need to answer routine queries about fees or academic dates. AI should handle such questions reliably, allowing human staff to focus only on cases that genuinely require their involvement.
- **Validate AI costs before deciding the price:** One team estimated that each call would cost less than ₹1, but sir challenged whether the calculation included all the components. RAG, re-ranking, reasoning tokens and retries can increase the actual cost considerably. I understood that we should first build a working prototype and measure its cost, latency and accuracy before making pricing commitments.
- **Look beyond the immediate user to understand the larger opportunity:** In the wardrobe assistant example, sir encouraged the team to consider the value created for clothing brands and exporters rather than focusing only on the convenience offered to an individual family. This highlighted the importance of identifying where the greatest business impact can be generated.
- **Recognise self-development as a major competitive threat:** A potential customer may question why they should purchase our product instead of creating something similar using a Claude skill or an existing subscription. If our solution is only a thin layer built around a prompt, the model provider could introduce the same functionality and make our product irrelevant. We therefore need a stronger source of differentiation.
- **Move from identifying problems to preventing them:** Simply detecting errors may no longer be sufficient to create a compelling product. Sir compared this with the red underline in an IDE, where developers benefit more when mistakes are prevented before they occur rather than merely being highlighted afterwards.
- **Start with a clearly defined customer segment:** The digital twin team was advised not to target everyone immediately. Instead, they should make the solution work exceptionally well for one specific role, course or organisational function before expanding. I also found the discussion about undocumented organisational knowledge important. Local terminology, abbreviations and informal practices are often understood by employees but are not captured in formal documents.
- **Consider the strategy of offering less at a lower price:** A product does not always need to match the full feature set of a large competitor. Removing unnecessary capabilities can help serve a specific segment that is currently over-served by expensive or complex solutions. This is the idea behind offering "less for a lot less".
- **Explore partnerships instead of direct competition:** For the screen-security product, sir suggested positioning it as an additional capability within existing VDI solutions rather than competing directly with those platforms. However, this approach also carries a risk: the partner could eventually develop the same feature independently.
- **Understand third-party licensing before finalising economics:** Open-source models and libraries may be available without charge for non-commercial use but may require a paid licence when incorporated into commercial products. Unless these obligations are understood early, the product's actual development and operating costs remain uncertain, making reliable pricing difficult.
- **Prove value through actual customer behaviour:** In the sales document generator example, sir suggested finding salespeople who would be willing to accept higher sales targets in exchange for access to the tool. I found this particularly meaningful because such willingness would provide stronger evidence of value than simply asking potential users whether they liked the idea.
- **Present the product with confidence and conviction:** We should not introduce our ideas by saying they are merely something we have just thought of. Every team member should also understand the complete product well enough to explain any section. In a VC discussion, there is no guarantee that every team member will be invited into the room.

**My final reflection:** Looking across both presentation sessions, I realised that the biggest weakness in many teams' projects, including my own, was not necessarily technical capability. Most teams were able to develop a working solution, but the more difficult questions concerned product positioning, unit economics and convincing evidence of customer value. This connects directly with the central learning of the course: AI itself is only the means of delivering value. The real product is the promise made to a specific customer, the problem being solved and the economic value that makes the solution worth paying for.

## Overall Reflection – The Responsibility Behind Product Management

The most important lesson I am taking away from all fourteen classes is that product management involves making a continuous series of decisions without ever having complete information. The objective is not to make every decision perfectly, but to consistently make more sound decisions than poor ones.

Frameworks provide structure, evidence helps reduce uncertainty, and metrics make outcomes easier to evaluate. However, metrics become truly meaningful when they reflect actual financial impact in rupees rather than relying only on percentages. These tools can guide my thinking, but they cannot take responsibility for the final choice.

Ultimately, I have to make the decision, stand by it and accept its consequences. That, for me, captures the real responsibility of being a product manager.
