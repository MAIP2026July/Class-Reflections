# **Reflection: Dynamic Product Navigation, Framework Pragmatism, and Human Team Realities** 

**Session Topic:** Framework Adaptation, The Moving Target Dilemma, Scrum as Stability Negotiation, and Pricing Surplus Dynamics 

# **Core Takeaways & Concepts** 

This session pushed well beyond static frameworks, focusing heavily on how a product manager operates under extreme volatility, shifting user baselines, and complex team dynamics. Key conceptual anchors included: 

- **Frameworks as Dials, Not the Steering Wheel:** Highlighted that frameworks — including JTBD, Double Diamond, Opportunity Solution Trees, Lean Canvas, RICE, and MoSCoW — serve only as diagnostic aids and decision-support instruments, not as management in itself. Stacking redundant frameworks creates administrative ceremony without improving decision clarity or shifting evidence. The PM retains complete ownership of the decision, its expiration date, and its consequences. 

- **The "Moving Target" Analogy & The Course Correction Dilemma:** A product is not launched at a static objective; it functions like a missile fired at an evolving, moving target. User expectations, competitor offerings, and underlying technology stacks drift continuously while development is underway. Course correction—accelerating, reprioritizing, or outright killing features—is a mandatory dynamic reality. In AI-driven environments, democratization of tech stacks means even customers can choose to build an in-house alternative if vendor roadmaps stall or pricing becomes disproportionate. 

- **Scrum as a "Labor Negotiation" for Stability:** Rather than treating Scrum as an infallible dogma, the faculty framed sprints through an organizational lens: dynamic PM pivots inevitably create whiplash for engineering teams. A sprint represents a negotiated contract—the team accepts radical flexibility between cycles in exchange for bounded stability during the active sprint. Sprints must be shorter in fast-moving, uncertain environments (e.g., cutting-edge AI research) to cap exposure, whereas stable roadmaps can afford longer cycles. 

- **Generalizing Products vs. Bespoke Client Projects:** A product solves an unmet need for **_N_** unnamed, unknown customers, not a single client. The lecture cautioned against building bespoke features into the core platform for vocal beta accounts. If a client demands non-reusable customization, the PM must have the discipline to say no, split out a separately funded implementation initiative, or risk diluting the core product into an unmaintainable custom project. 

- **The 80/20 Value Surplus Principle in Pricing:** When pricing an AI product, the developer cannot capture 100% of the economic surplus created. If a product 

generates 100 units of efficiency or cost savings, claiming all of it destroys user incentive to adopt. A pragmatic baseline heuristic is capping the product's captured share around 20%, leaving at least 80% of the surplus with the customer to drive organic adoption and retention. 

- **One-Way vs. Two-Way Doors and Blast Radius:** Decisions must be categorized by reversibility. Reversible (two-way) choices should be decentralized rapidly to empower teams and preserve velocity. Irreversible (one-way) choices—such as structural architectural commitments or entering heavily regulated compliance territory—require rigorous pre-mortems, red-team skepticism, and deliberate executive scrutiny. 

# **Workplace Applicability & Contextual Translation** 

Applying these frameworks to my operational setting in US IT talent acquisition, recruitment workflows, and HRM automation: 

- **The Bespoke Trap in Candidate Screening Systems:** In talent acquisition, hiring managers from specific business units constantly demand custom screening rules, bespoke application stages, or hyper-tailored knockout questions. The faculty's guidance on "layering" (core platform > industry/domain layer >  bespoke edge customizer) maps directly here. If we hardcode unique recruitment rules for a single vertical into our primary applicant evaluation pipeline, we create technical debt that slows down the whole organization. Keeping the core evaluation engine generic while handling edge exceptions through modular scripts or configurable templates protects system scalability. 

- **The 80/20 Rule for Recruiter Tooling Adoption:** Internal productivity tools often face friction because recruiters feel the software adds compliance tracking overhead without giving them back their time. If we introduce an automated sourcing assistant, the recruiter must experience the overwhelming majority of the time savings (the 80% surplus) in faster shortlist turnaround. If the system captures too much process overhead (e.g., requiring mandatory tagging, manual prompt logs, complex data cleanup), adoption collapses. 

- **Managing Sprint Whiplash in Automation Initiatives:** When deploying internal automation (such as resume parsing scripts or candidate engagement bots), operational priorities change weekly based on active hiring requisitions. Slicing automation milestones into tight, predictable two-week increments protects technical contributors from constant context-switching while allowing leadership to reset direction at clear sprint boundaries.
