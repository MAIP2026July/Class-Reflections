# **Session 2 Reflection**

Date: 1 August 2026

Topic: Product vs. Project, Product Lifecycle, Frameworks & Traceability

Today's class helped me understand that a product and a project are not exactly the same, even though both involve planning, development and delivery. I found the explanation that a project usually starts with a specific customer and requirement, whereas a product is built keeping a larger group of users in mind, quite interesting. It made me think about how we develop applications in our day-to-day work. Sometimes we build something to fulfil a particular business requirement, but making it scalable and useful for other users is what makes it more of a product.

Another thing I could relate to was the idea of not making every decision in advance. The house construction example explained this really well. Decisions related to the foundation and structure need to be taken early, but things like colours and finishing can be decided later. I feel this approach is also useful in software development. We don't always have complete information at the beginning, so keeping some decisions open can help avoid unnecessary changes and rework later. However, it is equally important to communicate clearly with the team about what is finalised and what is still under discussion.

The discussion around beta releases also gave me a different perspective. I used to think of a beta release as a stage where most of the major work was already completed. But the credit card product example showed that even when the product seems ready, some important operational requirements may still be missing. It made me realise that completing development is not the same as making a product ready for actual use.

We also discussed several frameworks like JTBD, RICE, Kano, QFD and Traceability Matrix. There were quite a few concepts, but what I understood is that these frameworks are meant to support decision-making rather than just follow a particular process. I found traceability especially important because the original business requirement should remain connected to the technical design, development tasks and testing. Otherwise, there is a possibility that the team may complete the development but miss the actual objective.

The difference between AI-enabled and AI-native products was another interesting part of the session. I understood that simply adding AI features to an existing application is different from building a product where AI is the main foundation. Since I have a technical background, I found the discussion around balancing model performance, cost, response time and risks quite relevant. An AI Product Manager may not need to build the models themselves, but should understand enough to make the right decisions.

Overall, this session made me realise that product management involves much more than getting features developed and delivered. It requires understanding the users, knowing which decisions to take and when, and making sure that the final product actually serves its purpose. I could connect many of these discussions with my own experience in software development, which made the session more interesting and relatable.
