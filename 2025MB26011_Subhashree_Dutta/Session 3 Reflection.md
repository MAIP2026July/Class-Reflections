# **Session 3 Reflection**

Date: 18 August 2026

Topic: Qualifying Users, Progress vs. Features, Incremental vs. Transformation & AI Moats

One thing that really stood out to me in today's session was the idea that adding more features doesn't necessarily mean that a product is improving. We often measure development progress by looking at how many features have been delivered or how quickly a team completes its tasks. But what matters more is whether those changes are actually helping users solve their problems. I found this particularly relevant to my software development experience because sometimes we focus so much on completing requirements that we forget to look at the actual value they bring to the end user.

The discussion about identifying users and customers was also interesting. I realised that we cannot treat everyone interacting with a product in the same way. For example, the person who uses an application daily, the person who makes the purchase decision and the administrator managing the system may all have completely different expectations. Understanding these differences is important before deciding what needs to be developed.

I also liked the comparison between a caterpillar and a butterfly while discussing incremental improvements and digital transformation. Improving an existing process by 5% is definitely useful, but it doesn't necessarily mean that the entire process has been transformed. This made me think about how organisations sometimes use the term digital transformation even when they are only making small improvements to existing systems. The discussion about teams holding back improvements to have something to deliver in future appraisal cycles was also an interesting observation.

Another concept I found useful was the importance of maintaining different layers in a product. A product should have a common core that works for everyone, followed by industry-specific requirements and then limited customer-specific configurations. I could relate this to enterprise applications, where different customers often request changes based on their individual business processes. Accepting every customisation can make the application difficult to maintain and affect its scalability. So, knowing when to say no is also an important part of product management.

The discussion about AI and competitive advantage was probably one of the most relevant topics for me. With tools like LLMs and AI APIs becoming easily accessible, developing a basic feature or prototype is no longer very difficult. Even someone with limited development experience can build something similar in a short time. This made me realise that just having the ability to build an AI-based feature may not give a company a long-term advantage. The real value comes from things like proper workflow integration, unique data, governance and the ability to provide something that competitors cannot easily replicate.

We also discussed sprint duration and how it affects team stability and flexibility. I understood that sprint planning is not just about following an Agile process. The duration needs to be decided based on the nature of the product, the team's working style and how frequently requirements or priorities change.

Overall, this session made me think beyond the development side of a product. My biggest learning was that product success should be measured by the progress and value delivered to users rather than simply the number of features released. It also helped me understand that in the AI space, building something is becoming easier, but building something that has lasting value is the real challenge.
