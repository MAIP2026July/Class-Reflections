# **Session 4 Reflection**

Date: 22 August 2026

Topic: AI Appropriateness, Tail Risk, Actionable Windows & Human-in-the-Loop

Today's session made me think about an important question: just because we can use AI to solve a problem, does it mean we should? Earlier, whenever I came across an AI-based solution, I mostly focused on what it could do and how it could be implemented. But I understood that choosing AI should depend on whether it actually adds value to the product. Sometimes a simple solution can be more effective than introducing a complex AI model.

One concept I found useful was the four levels of choosing an AI solution. We discussed starting with basic rules and checklists, then moving towards analytics and search, predictive machine learning, and finally generative or agentic AI. I liked this approach because it makes us think about the actual requirement before selecting a technology. If a problem can be handled through simple conditions or existing business logic, using an expensive AI model may not be necessary.

The discussion about accuracy and timing was another interesting part. The agricultural advisory example helped me understand that even if an AI model gives accurate predictions, the information is not useful if it reaches the user too late. For example, informing a farmer about heavy rainfall after the crops have already been damaged doesn't really solve the problem. So, along with accuracy, we also need to consider response time and whether the user has enough time to take action based on the information.

I also learnt that looking only at the average accuracy of an AI model can be misleading. Even if a model performs well in most situations, the few cases where it fails can have serious consequences, especially in areas involving financial or operational decisions. This made me realise why proper validations, backup mechanisms and escalation options are important while building AI-based solutions. We cannot assume that a model will always give the correct output.

Another topic that caught my attention was Human-in-the-Loop. I always thought that keeping a human involved would automatically make an AI system safer. However, the discussion about automation bias gave me a different perspective. If people get used to accepting whatever the AI recommends without checking it, then human involvement becomes just a formality. So, the system should be designed in a way that encourages people to actually review and question AI-generated recommendations whenever required.

Towards the end, we discussed user adoption and how even a useful AI feature may not be accepted easily. If people are already comfortable with an existing process, introducing a new AI-based workflow may require additional training and changes in their way of working. I could relate this to enterprise applications, where introducing a new feature is only one part of the implementation. Getting users comfortable with it is equally important.

Overall, this session helped me understand that successful AI product development is not just about using advanced models or achieving high accuracy. It is about choosing the right approach, understanding the possible risks and making sure that the solution is genuinely useful to the people who will use it. My biggest takeaway is that AI should be introduced because it solves a problem better, not simply because the technology is available.
