# **Session 5 Reflection**

Date: 29 August 2026

Topic: Finding AI Opportunities, Product Types, Evidence & 4-Layer AI Stack

Today's session was interesting because it focused on identifying where AI can actually be useful instead of trying to bring AI into every possible solution. I understood that before deciding to build an AI-based feature, we need to first understand the problem properly. Factors like how frequently the problem occurs, how much difficulty it creates for users, whether enough data is available and how important the decision is should be considered. Just having access to AI technology is not a good enough reason to use it.

One concept that I found quite interesting was the difference between products where users want to finish their work quickly and products where users spend more time by choice. For example, in applications like Netflix or Prime Video, user engagement and time spent on the platform are important. But in internal business applications, the goal should be to help employees complete their tasks as quickly and efficiently as possible. I could relate this to the enterprise applications I work on. Ideally, users should be able to complete their activities without spending unnecessary time navigating through different screens.

The discussion about making AI claims without proper evidence was also something I could relate to. Just saying that a product uses AI or improves efficiency doesn't automatically make it valuable. We need to have actual results to support such claims. Comparing the process before and after introducing AI can help us understand whether there has been any real improvement. I also understood that initial excitement around a new feature doesn't necessarily mean that users will continue using it. Consistent usage is what shows that the product is actually useful.

We also went through the four layers of an AI product: Data, Model, API and User Experience. I found this particularly useful from a technical perspective because it connects the different components involved in building an AI-based application. Even if the model performs really well, the overall product can still fail if the API is slow or the user interface is difficult to use. At the end of the day, users experience the complete application and not just the intelligence of the underlying model.

Another point that caught my attention was the difficulty of getting users to switch from their existing processes. Even if a new solution is technically better, people may hesitate to use it if they have to learn a completely new workflow, enter the same information again or move between multiple applications. This made me realise that user convenience should be considered from the beginning rather than treating it as something to improve after development.

Towards the end, we discussed alert fatigue in Human-in-the-Loop systems. I found this an important point because having a human approval step doesn't necessarily guarantee that every AI recommendation is properly reviewed. If users keep receiving too many routine alerts, they may eventually start approving everything without checking it carefully. Instead, human intervention should be more meaningful, especially when the AI is uncertain or the decision could have serious consequences.

Overall, this session helped me understand that identifying the right problem is more important than choosing the latest technology. A successful AI product should demonstrate actual value, fit naturally into the user's existing workflow and make their work easier. My biggest takeaway was that the success of an AI solution should be measured by the improvement it brings to users, not simply by the fact that AI has been implemented.
