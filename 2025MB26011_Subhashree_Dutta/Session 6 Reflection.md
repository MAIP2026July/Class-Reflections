# **Session 6 Reflection**

Date: 5 September 2026

Topic: Re-engineering Workflows, Stakeholder-Voice PRDs, Data Governance & Building Category Killers

Today's session made me think about how we approach problem-solving while developing a product. One point that I found particularly interesting was that introducing AI into an existing process doesn't necessarily make the process better. If the existing workflow has unnecessary steps or is already inefficient, adding AI will only make those problems happen faster. Instead, we should first understand the complete process and identify what can be improved or removed before deciding where AI can fit.

The idea of looking at an existing workflow as a black box was something I could relate to my development experience. We often work on existing applications where certain processes have been followed for years. Sometimes, instead of questioning whether a particular step is still required, we simply continue with the same approach while adding new features. This session made me realise that product thinking requires us to question the existing process and look for better ways of achieving the same objective.

We also discussed how to write Product Requirement Documents (PRDs) from the perspective of individual stakeholders. I found this useful because different people involved in the same process may have completely different expectations. For example, an employee using an application, a manager approving a request and an administrator maintaining the system will each have different requirements. Writing requirements from their individual perspectives helps us understand what each person actually needs instead of treating everyone as a generic user.

Another small but important point was about not removing sections from standard product templates just because they are not applicable. Instead, we should mention why a particular section is not relevant. I initially thought this was just a documentation practice, but I understood that it also helps us think through different aspects of a product and avoid missing important dependencies.

The discussion around Build vs. Buy vs. API was particularly relevant to the current AI environment. Using external APIs can save development time and help us implement features quickly, but there are other factors to consider, such as usage costs, dependency on external vendors and data security. The discussion about the DPDP Act also made me realise that handling user and business data requires careful consideration. Especially in enterprise applications, sensitive business information should be protected, and companies should think carefully before sharing their data with external AI services.

We also discussed how biased data can affect AI-generated decisions. The examples of biased credit algorithms showed that even when a system appears to be working correctly, the data used to train it may contain hidden biases. This can lead to unfair decisions and serious consequences. It made me understand that data quality is not just a technical concern but also an important responsibility while designing AI products.

Overall, this session helped me connect product management concepts with practical challenges in software development. I understood that building a good AI product is not simply about selecting a model or integrating an API. It requires rethinking existing workflows, understanding different stakeholder expectations, protecting data and considering the impact of the decisions made by the system. My biggest takeaway was that sometimes improving a product starts with questioning the process itself rather than adding something new to it.
