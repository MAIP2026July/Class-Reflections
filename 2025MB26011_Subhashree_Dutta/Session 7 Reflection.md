# **Session 7 Reflection**

**Date:** 12 September 2026\
**Topic:** Proactive Systems, Value-Based Pricing, PFIR Model & Full-Lifecycle AI Risk

This session helped me understand how different aspects of product management connect with each other. Pricing, customer experience, data architecture and risk management may seem like separate areas, but they all contribute to the overall value and success of a product.

One of the most interesting discussions was about moving from reactive support to proactive interventions. The electricity support case study made this clear. Instead of simply building an AI chatbot to respond to customer complaints, a better approach would be to use operational data and telemetry to identify possible outages before customers report them. Ideally, customers should receive information about the issue and its expected resolution without having to contact support themselves. This made me think about how AI can improve the entire service experience rather than just automate one part of it.

Another important learning was how proactive systems should deal with uncertainty. AI predictions are not always reliable, especially when the available information is incomplete. In such situations, the product should communicate uncertainty clearly or avoid sending an alert when confidence is too low. Simply adding a disclaimer that AI can make mistakes is not enough. The user experience itself should be designed to handle uncertainty responsibly.

The discussion on value-based pricing was also useful. I usually associate pricing with development costs and the effort required to build a product, but this session highlighted that pricing should be based on the economic value delivered to the customer. The suggested 15–25% of the total value created, with 20% as a reasonable starting point, gave me a practical way to think about pricing. I also found it interesting that in B2B products, the buyer's willingness to pay is influenced by their own KPIs, incentives and risk tolerance, not just the benefits of the product.

The PFIR model — Facts/Features, Inferences and Recommendations — was another useful framework. It reminded me that product decisions should be based on clearly separated facts and assumptions. A feature or observation does not automatically prove a particular conclusion. Understanding this difference can help avoid incorrect assumptions when defining requirements or recommending a solution.

Finally, the session highlighted that launching an AI product does not mean the work is complete. Model updates, API changes, vendor pricing and data drift can affect product performance even when the product itself has not been modified. This made me realise that continuous monitoring and managing third-party dependencies are important parts of product management.

Overall, I took away that a successful AI product is not just about building something intelligent. It is about delivering measurable value, communicating honestly with users, making decisions based on evidence and continuously managing risks throughout the product lifecycle.
