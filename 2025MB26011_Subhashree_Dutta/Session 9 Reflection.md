# **Session 9 Reflection**

**Date:** 10 October 2026\
**Topic:** AI Product Metrics, Economic Cost of Errors, Tail Risk, Experimentation & Feedback Loops

This session changed the way I think about measuring the success of an AI product. Coming from a software development background, I often associate good system performance with accuracy and technical efficiency. However, the discussion made me realise that even a highly accurate model may not deliver the expected business value. What matters is how its predictions affect actual decisions, customers and business outcomes.

The discussion around false positives and false negatives was particularly interesting. The lending example helped me understand that different types of errors have different financial and operational consequences. Approving a borrower who later defaults can result in a financial loss, whereas rejecting someone who could have repaid means missing a potential business opportunity and possibly losing customer trust. So, choosing a model based only on its overall accuracy may not be the right approach. The cost of each type of error also needs to be considered.

Another important concept was tail risk. A model might perform well in most situations but still fail in rare cases where the impact is extremely high. The wildfire and insurance example showed how a single unusual event can create losses that outweigh the benefits gained during normal operations. This made me think that product managers should not focus only on average performance but also understand the worst-case scenarios and the consequences of failure.

I also found the connection between technical metrics and business metrics useful. Accuracy, precision, recall and F1-score help evaluate the model, but they should eventually connect to outcomes such as revenue, customer retention, operational efficiency and losses avoided. Having too many KPIs without a clear objective can make it difficult to understand whether a product is actually improving. It is important to identify the metrics that matter most at each stage of the product lifecycle.

The discussion about leading and lagging indicators was another takeaway. Revenue growth may look positive, but it does not always indicate that the product is healthy. For example, revenue could continue increasing even when new customer acquisition has started declining. This highlighted the importance of looking beyond headline numbers and identifying early signals that may indicate future problems.

Experimentation was also an interesting topic. A metric improving after a new feature is introduced does not necessarily mean that the feature caused the improvement. External factors may also influence the results. Using control groups, adequate sample sizes and statistical significance helps establish whether an observed change is actually related to the product intervention. This reminded me that product decisions should be supported by evidence rather than assumptions.

Finally, the discussion on customer feedback made me think about the balance between listening to users and maintaining a consistent product direction. Not every request should become a product feature. If a product continuously changes based on the demands of its most vocal customers, it may become difficult to scale. Maintaining a common product core while allowing controlled customisation for different user segments seems like a more sustainable approach.

Overall, my biggest learning from this session is that product metrics should tell us more than whether a model is performing well technically. They should help us understand the actual value being created, the risks involved and the impact of decisions on customers and the business.
