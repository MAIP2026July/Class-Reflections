# Reflection: Session 8

3 October 2026 | Alamelu G | 2025MB26016

One thing I realised from this class is that building an AI product is not just about choosing the right model and getting it to work. Even when something works perfectly during development, there is no guarantee that it will behave the same way in production. This session made me think more about the uncertainty involved in AI development and the responsibility of a product manager in managing it.

**The point that stayed with me.** The same AI model, with the same input and settings, can sometimes produce different results. The faculty discussed how the performance of AI coding tools and models can fluctuate, sometimes becoming better and sometimes worse. What I found interesting was the distinction between model probability and provider-side instability. We usually assume that if something works during testing, it should continue to work the same way, but that may not always be true with AI. This makes monitoring and tracking changes much more important than I initially thought.

**The RAG discussion was also useful.** I found the discussion around deterministic versus probabilistic outputs interesting, especially the insurance example. There are certain business calculations where the output needs to be consistent every time, and using an LLM for everything may not be the right approach. RAG can be useful when we need to retrieve information from a large knowledge base, but it cannot solve every problem. Sometimes a simple API, calculation or rule-based approach is better. I liked the idea of combining different approaches rather than expecting one AI model to handle the entire business workflow.

**Where I slightly disagree.** The suggestion to keep AI providers and vendors pluggable makes sense because we don't want to become completely dependent on one provider. However, I think switching between providers may not always be as simple as it sounds. Different models can behave differently, and even API compatibility does not guarantee the same output or performance. The class also discussed how translation gateways can introduce additional latency. So I feel vendor flexibility should be considered from the beginning, but there will still be trade-offs.

**The prototype vs MVP discussion clarified something for me.** I had generally thought of a prototype as an early version of the actual product. But the distinction made in class was that a prototype is mainly for demonstrating an idea, whereas a pilot involves an actual customer using the product and giving feedback. The product then needs to prove that it can scale beyond a controlled environment. Something that works perfectly in a demo may fail when real customers start using it. This is something I had not thought about in enough detail before. 

**The lesson I found most practical.** "Learn before we automate." I think this is particularly relevant when building AI products. There is a tendency to automate everything from the beginning, but sometimes keeping a manual process initially is actually better. We can learn from the manual process, understand where the AI is making mistakes and gradually introduce automation. The idea of having manual overrides and a fallback option also makes sense, especially when the system is not confident about a decision.

I also liked the point that we should be transparent about what is manual and what is automated. We should not give customers the impression that something is fully automated when there is actually a person doing the work behind the scenes. That could create trust issues later.

**The governance discussion stood out too.** The faculty spoke about having prototype briefs and MVP briefs with clear expectations of what the team is supposed to demonstrate. I think this is important because otherwise product managers can keep adding requirements without knowing when to stop. Teams need to know what success looks like before they start building. The go/no-go decision should also be based on evidence rather than just someone's opinion. Rework or modifying the approach should also be considered valid decisions.

At the same time, governance should not become a burden for the team. It should help people make decisions and move forward rather than create unnecessary approval processes. I liked the idea that every stage should prove something and increase our confidence in the product.

**What I will take forward.** For our own project, I want to be more careful about defining what we are trying to prove at each stage. We should not spend too much time building a complicated prototype when a smaller and simpler version can give us the information we need. I also want to think about what happens when the AI does not work as expected, whether we have a manual fallback and how we will evaluate the product before moving towards wider adoption.

**Open question.** I am still thinking about how a product manager should decide when an AI product is reliable enough to move from a pilot to full automation. We can keep testing and collecting feedback, but there will probably always be some uncertainty. So how much evidence is actually enough before we are comfortable allowing the AI to operate with less human involvement?
