## Class 1:

I fully echo what was discussed in the class. A PM is like the CEO of the product. Someone in the class called it a thankless job, and I do not fully agree with that. It becomes thankless only when the customer need is not addressed and the feature is not received well by the internal stakeholders. Communication is the key that is missing in those cases. In my experience most of the frustration comes from people not knowing why a decision was taken, not from the decision itself. When I explain the reasoning, even the people who disagree stay with me. And there is always a sense of satisfaction in the footprint we leave behind in every decision made on the product.

The CEO framing also matched the ownership without authority point. In my role the engineering team, UX team and the documentation team do not report to me. I cannot instruct anyone. I can only influence them with the reasoning, the customer evidence and the priority. But if the release does not land well, the question comes to me first. That gap between accountability and authority is the real job.

The judgement and trade-off part is where I want to take an example from my current role. As I am a PM in a product company, there is an underlying platform on which our application is built. I am bound to build the product within the design constraints of that platform. So every reasonably large feature comes with the same judgement, do I write my own code and get exactly what the customer asked for, or do I stay with the platform capability and build something that scales and stays maintainable across releases. Writing our own code is faster in the short term and it always looks attractive in a customer escalation. But it creates a component that only my team can maintain, and it will not benefit from the platform upgrades later. Most of the time I choose the platform route and accept a slightly weaker fit today. This is exactly the database example the faculty gave. The technically better option is not always the better product option, and the reason is usually about who will maintain it two years later.

## Class 2:

I work on an application product for Sales, and we cater to a wide variety of industries as vertical solutions on top of the core, like Insurance, Finance and Healthcare. When the faculty made the distinction between a project and a product, and later explained layering, I realised that is exactly the structure we already follow. There is a generic core, then an industry layer, then whatever a specific customer configures for themselves. Hearing it stated as a principle was useful, because in day to day work we sometimes let a single large customer's request come into the core. That is the point where a product quietly starts becoming a project, and it is very hard to reverse later.

The other decision I keep facing is breadth of features against depth of features. My product is relatively new in the market, so I focus on breadth first, so that we get the attention of analysts and get into the evaluation shortlists. Once we have sold enough and we start getting real usage feedback, then I go deep on the specific features that customers actually use. This is my working version of good enough. It is not that the feature is complete, it is that it is complete enough for the segment I am chasing right now.

We have three GA releases in a year and each release is focused on a specific set of market outcomes. The point about GA being the real launch milestone is something we live with, because anything before GA is only a controlled conversation with a few customers.

On artifacts, the traceability matrix is maintained in our internal tool, which captures stories, test cases, changes and so on. It is one of the reasons implementations go through cleanly, because we can show what was asked, what was built and what was tested. Earlier I saw it as process overhead. The trustee framing changed my view. We are spending someone else's money and someone else's time, so the evidence trail is not bureaucracy, it is the justification.

In the discovery stage, interviews really help to understand what a customer expects from the product, more than any written requirement. In the strategy stage I focus on competitors, what they are delivering and where we can differentiate. Once I have clarity, I build low fidelity mocks using AI tools like GitHub Copilot, which helps my team understand the functionality before it is handed over to the UX team. This has genuinely reduced the number of rounds we go through with UX.

One honest gap. I do not really use any specific prioritisation framework formally. I take these considerations into account as part of the product strategy, but not as a named framework. The class point that frameworks are dials and not the steering wheel makes me comfortable about that, but I still want to try RICE for one release cycle and see whether it changes my ordering or only justifies what I had already decided.

## Class 3:

AI trends are changing at a fast pace. What we delivered six months ago can be fully automated now. So everything is evolving, and I spend a good amount of my time keeping pace with the trends, and helping educate the sales team on where the trend is going and how our product differentiates. This is the moving target the faculty described. The target moves because the customer expectation moves, and our own product drifts because the technology under it keeps changing.

I agree with the point that as domain knowledge gets democratised, customers have the choice to build it themselves. We are facing exactly this challenge. Earlier a CRM was hard to build, so buying it was obvious. Today a customer team can prototype a workflow quickly and then question our pricing. What we still have is the depth around case management, governance and integrations, but that gap is narrowing and I do not think it is safe to assume it will hold.

We used to follow Scrum. Because of the AI trend our company has shifted its thinking, and now it is more of a Kanban mode. Anyone can pick up the work, become the intent owner, and pull in contributors to complete it. It is all about delivering faster, and responsibility is now shifted to the individual. I like the speed. But the faculty's reframing of Scrum as a negotiated window of no change made me think about what we gave up. A sprint was a promise to the team that nothing would change for two weeks. In the Kanban mode I can change the priority any day, which is good for the market but it puts the burden of stability entirely on the individual. I want to watch whether this causes fatigue over the next few releases, because the speed is visible immediately and the cost is not.

On pricing, we charge based on cases created. The class covered cost plus, willingness to pay, and value based pricing. Our model is closest to a usage proxy for value, because more cases usually means more work getting done in the product. The 20 percent rule was new to me, that the producer historically captures around a fifth of the value created. It is a useful sanity check when we discuss price increases.

And a great product always needs design thinking. That is what shifts a product to a new frontier rather than adding one more feature to the existing one.

## Class 4:

My core view after this class is that AI should always help make the business process better. It should not reinvent itself and take control of the business process. In our design, agent prompts should be written to instruct, and the key decision logic should stay in the core rules of the product. This way AI helps to proactively make suggestions or complete actions inside a business workflow, but the decision itself is still governed by rules that can be inspected and changed. Human in the loop for all key decisions is a core element in the design of AI products.

This lines up with the appropriateness ladder from the class, that you start with deterministic rules and move up only if the lower level cannot do the job. In a CRM most eligibility, approval and routing decisions are rule based, and they should stay that way. The rule is auditable, it is testable, and a business user can change it. If I move that logic into a prompt I lose all three. Generative AI is the right choice for the parts that were never rule based, like summarising a long case history or drafting an email.

All the contextual information and the knowledge base should reside within the product, not in the LLM. This matters for us because our customers are in regulated industries, and they need to know where an answer came from. The class point about vendors who hide behind IP and cannot explain a decision was a good mirror. If we ask that of our vendors, our customers will ask the same of us, so reasoning, evidence and data lineage have to be part of the product and not an afterthought.

We now flag every feature clearly as AI powered or as an agentic workflow. I have a slight tension with this, given the class warning about AI washing and the double bearing fan example. Part of the reason we flag it is genuine, the user needs to know an output is probabilistic before they trust it. Part of it is market pressure, because every vendor in this space is doing it. I want to make sure we stay on the first side of that line, and the test I will apply is whether the customer would still choose the feature if the label were removed.

For every AI conversation we track the history and keep it for audit. That is our version of designing for the tail risk rather than the average case, because the one conversation that goes wrong is the one we will be asked about.

## Class 5:

In my product the AI opportunity lies mainly in summarisation, data research and finding contextual signals. In a workflow, showing the human the context, the research and the data gaps early is what makes the feedback useful. That is the core of human in the loop design for us. It is not an approval popup at the end, it is giving the person the right information before they decide.

There are also opportunities in scoring, where AI scores something and also exposes its confidence, so the human can judge how much weight to give it. For some low hanging scenarios like lead qualification in sales and marketing, AI can be fully autonomous within a defined boundary, because a wrongly qualified lead is cheap to correct. HITL can then be reserved for cases where a sensitive topic is flagged. Deciding where the human sits is the actual design decision, and the class point about routing deliberately rather than putting a human everywhere is right. A human on every step looks safe on paper and it just creates fatigue.

Human fatigue was the part that stayed with me the most. If the AI keeps saying everything is fine, people stop reading and start clicking yes. I have seen this in myself with coding assistants. The parallel decision approach mentioned in class, where the human decides first and the two answers are compared, is something I want to explore for our higher risk flows, because it avoids anchoring the human to the AI answer.

The challenging part in the current trend is holding back the use of AI where it is not really needed. Every software vendor wants to say they are AI powered, and if they do not say it they fear losing to the competition. That fear is real in our market too. But the frequency and materiality test from the class is a good filter. If the step is rare or not painful, adding AI to it only adds cost and risk without a return.

Prompts need structured maintenance in every release. This is something we underestimated initially. A prompt that works once for one user is not a product. Since we are serving a large number of customers with different data and different configurations, prompts need version control, a test set and a review before every release, in the same way we treat code. The Role, Context, Task, Constraints structure gives us a consistent shape to review against, which makes it easier to catch what is missing.

## Class 6:

The part that stayed with me is that re-imagining is a skill and not a talent. Most of the workflows we run today were built around constraints that no longer exist. In our sales flow, an opportunity still moves stage by stage because a rep manually updates it after a call. That design came from a time when there was no way to capture the call at all. Nobody questions it because it works. So the first job of a PM is to know what is possible now, otherwise you cannot even ask the right question. This changes every few months, so it needs constant scanning.

PRD discipline was the most practical part of the class. Writing requirements in the stakeholder's own voice, as in "I as a sales rep want", forces a check on whether that person would really ask for it. Keeping non functional items like concurrency out of the PRD and into the product specification is a good separation. We usually mix both and then the document serves neither the business reader nor the engineer. Consistent terminology sounds like a minor point, but it leaks into user manuals and support content later. In our case lead, prospect and opportunity get used loosely, and it causes confusion inside requirement discussions itself.

The black box framing is useful for our product. Keep the inputs and outputs constant, change the middle, and then ask which inputs can be dropped and which new inputs are cheap enough today. For lead scoring we still depend on the fields a rep fills in, but call transcripts and email threads are now cheap to process, so the input side itself can be redesigned. The bigger risk is optimising the wrong target beautifully. If we score leads for conversion probability when the real need is a reliable pipeline forecast, the model will be accurate and still useless to the sales manager.

On data, the custodian framing is the honest one. We hold the data, we do not own it. In a CRM this is sharper than in most products, because almost everything we store is somebody else's contact information, collected by our customer from their customer. DPDP and GDPR make deletion a real obligation and many organisations have not started. Logs and prompts being data as well, with their own retention and risk, is something I had not counted before, and it directly affects the audit trail we are building for AI conversations.

Sentiment at the feature level makes far more sense to me than one overall positive or negative score. The mother comment in the class example was the interesting part. It is not about any feature at all, but it tells you that buying that phone is a socially significant purchase that needs family consultation. That maps directly to selling, where the stated objection is price but the real signal is often that another stakeholder was not consulted. As PMs we read the features and miss the emotion. The point about reputation being locked into a platform is also a real gap, and it is the kind of problem ONDC was meant to address.

## Class 7:

The part of this class that landed closest to my own work was the separation between the model being probabilistic and the provider being unstable. I had been treating both as one problem. In my own use of coding assistants I have had weeks where the tool carries a long piece of work to completion and weeks where it stops halfway through the same kind of task, and I had always assumed my prompt was the problem. The point that the provider may be changing something behind the scenes, and that we only find out if we track it, is something I had not thought about. If the ground under the product can shift quietly, then our release testing cannot assume a fixed base.

The production version of this is what worries me more. In development, if the output is wrong I lose a few hours and redo it. In production I do not even know which customer got the weak answer. We serve a large number of customers on the same build and nobody can sit and inspect every AI response. So the only honest answer is to keep a record of what was asked, what the model returned and which version produced it, and to sample it regularly rather than wait for a complaint. We already log AI conversations for audit. What this class told me is that the same log has a second purpose, which is quality monitoring, and we are not using it that way yet.

The assumption drift example, where a model turned an instruction to test with three banks into a belief that there are always exactly three banks, is a failure I can picture happening in our product. Our agentic flows run over several steps, and if the agent quietly fixes an assumption early in the chain, everything after that looks consistent and correct. It will keep working until someone's data does not match the assumption, and by then it is in front of a customer.

The point about the product manager becoming the bottleneck was uncomfortable because I can already see it starting. When the team uses AI to produce more designs, more test cases and more options, all of it eventually lands on me for a decision, and the queue only grows. The temptation is to say go ahead because saying go ahead is faster than reading properly. The line that undoing a wrong decision costs more than taking longer to decide is the one I want to keep in front of me. What I do not yet have is a way to decide which of these approvals actually need my judgement and which can be delegated with a boundary, and that is probably the real work.

On keeping the vendor pluggable, we partly do this already because our platform abstracts the model layer, but I had not thought of it as protection against the provider rather than against the price. Knowing that there are effectively two API conventions in the market, and that a gateway between them costs latency, makes the choice a product decision and not just an engineering preference.

## Class 8:

The clean separation between a prototype, an MVP and staged automation is something I will borrow directly. In our world we tend to call everything a pilot. The distinction that a prototype is for a demo and an MVP is for a real customer who uses it and tells you whether it was worth anything is a sharper line than the one we draw. We have early access programmes that are really prototypes dressed up as pilots, because the customer looks at it and gives an opinion but never puts real work through it. That feedback feels good and it proves very little.

The warning about a prototype answering the wrong question is the one I want to be careful about. It is easy for us to build something that demonstrates the technology beautifully and never tests the thing we were actually unsure about. Before the next one I want to write down, in one line, what the prototype is supposed to prove and what result would make us stop. If I cannot write that line, the prototype is probably for show.

Partial automation being acceptable, as long as we are honest that a step is manual, is a useful permission to have. We often delay a feature because one part of the flow is not automated yet, when the customer would have taken the value and done that one step by hand. The condition is that we say so openly. The examples of vendors quietly putting humans behind an AI claim are a good reminder that the dishonest version of this is not a shortcut, it is a liability.

The gate discussion made me look at our own release process honestly. We do have stage reviews, but the criteria are not written down before the work starts, which means the team can feel that the bar moved. The other half of that point was harder to read. The picture of a product manager who keeps adding requirements while there is no customer and then asks why it is not ready the day a customer appears is close enough to behaviour I have seen, and have probably been guilty of, to be worth remembering. Preserving a version and being honest that the delay is on my side is a better way to keep the team's trust.

Never build it for one customer was the strongest line in the session for me. We already layer the product as a generic core, an industry layer and customer configuration, so the principle is familiar. What is new is the idea of deliberately looking for the generic product hiding inside a specific request, and keeping the customer specific part as a wrapper that can be removed. The railway gauge example made that concrete. The same judgement applies when a large customer asks for something that sounds reasonable and would quietly become part of our core.

## Class 9:

The point that if something about AI were already well understood there would already be a product doing it is a simple test and I had not framed it that way before. It means anything we build is a bet on a hypothesis, not an implementation of a known technique. In our space that is easy to forget, because the pressure is to match whatever a competitor announced last quarter. A feature built only to close a gap on an analyst checklist has no hypothesis behind it, and it usually shows in how little it gets used after the deal is signed.

The framing of a product as a promise, and of reputation being based on value actually realised rather than value promised, is something I want to use in our internal conversations. We measure launches by what shipped. The more useful question is what the customer got out of it three months later. For a feature like an AI summary of a case history, shipping it is easy and proving that it changed how long a rep spends on a case is the part nobody owns.

The separation between what is in our control and what is not was a good correction for me. I tend to treat the market as weather. The reminder that regulation, standards bodies and even technology direction can be tracked and partly anticipated is fair. The DeepSeek example is the one that stays with me, because the lesson is not that it was unpredictable but that the direction of cost was visible to anyone watching and most people were only watching capability.

The suggestion to do a Porter's analysis for today and a second one for eighteen months out is the most practical thing I am taking from this class. I spend a fair amount of time educating our sales team on where the trend is going, and that is always a narrative. Forcing it into the same five forces twice would make the assumptions visible and arguable instead of being a slide of opinions. I expect the supplier power row to look very different in the second version, given how much of our AI capability depends on model vendors we do not control.

This also connects back to the point from an earlier class about customers being able to build things themselves. If I do that future analysis honestly, the threat of substitutes row is not another vendor, it is our own customer's internal team with better tools than they had last year. I would rather write that down now than discover it in a renewal conversation.

## Class 10:

The economic value matrix is the single most useful thing I have taken from this course so far. The loan example, where accuracy goes up and profit goes down because one bad loan wipes out the margin on forty good ones, made the point in a way no amount of precision and recall discussion would have. I have sat in reviews where a model was reported as being eighty something percent accurate and everybody nodded, including me. Nobody asked what the two wrong cases cost compared with what the right ones earned.

Applying this to our own lead qualification is uncomfortable in a useful way. A lead wrongly marked as qualified costs a rep some wasted time, which is recoverable. A good lead wrongly rejected never gets seen again and we never find out it happened. Those two errors are not worth the same and we have been treating them as if they are. The harder part is that the loss is invisible, so nobody complains about it, which is exactly why it needs a number attached to it rather than an opinion.

The point that metrics must evolve as the product matures is one I want to act on. The example of revenue growing while new customer signups quietly fall is the kind of thing our dashboards would hide, because the headline number looks healthy. Our product is relatively new and we still measure mostly adoption and volume, which was right in the first year. If we are still measuring the same things next year, the faculty's question applies, which is what we have gone blind to.

The examples of measurement driving the wrong behaviour were familiar. Rewarding a tester for the number of defects found, so defects get held back until the right moment to report them, is a pattern I have seen in different forms. The version I need to watch for is in our own AI features, because an agent will optimise for whatever target we give it without any of the hesitation a person would have. If we score an assistant on how many actions it completes, it will complete actions, whether or not they were worth completing.

On experimentation, the reminder that the ground itself is moving was sobering. We may be running a controlled comparison while the model underneath us is being changed by someone else, so our control group is not really controlled. The simpler discipline of asking for the sample size and the standard error before celebrating a movement in a satisfaction score is something I can start doing immediately. The last point, that a feedback loop can quietly narrow a product to the one segment that shouts loudest, is a trap we are close to, and the answer of keeping the core generic and pushing the specialisation into a layer is the same answer that keeps coming back in this course.

## Class 11:

Scaling as a promise rather than as infrastructure is a reframing I needed. When we discuss scale internally the conversation goes immediately to throughput and instances. The question the faculty asked, of whether the customer's promise is still being met, is a different question. For us the promise is not that the service responds, it is that a rep gets a usable summary or a sensible next action. We could be fully available and still be breaking the promise, and nothing on our current dashboards would show it.

The point about mission critical features cutting both ways is worth holding on to. We want customers to depend on the product, because dependence is retention. But the moment they depend on it, the disclaimers in the contract stop protecting us in any way that matters. People judge the product by what it led them to expect, not by what the terms and conditions say, and that gap is where the anger comes from when something breaks.

The trade-off between latency, cost and quality being a product management decision and not a technical one is the part I want to act on. The honest position is that my team does not have a stated range from me today. If someone complains about speed, the fix will be a smaller model. If someone complains about quality, the fix will be a bigger one, and I will find out about either of them afterwards. Giving the team a floor on quality, a ceiling on response time and a band on cost is a small piece of work that would prevent both of those swings. The faculty's own example of deliberately adding seconds to make the timing predictable was the one that made me think, because we always optimise for the average and the customer notices the variation.

Versioning is something I had been treating as an engineering concern. Versioning the data, the prompts and the model together, so that we can actually roll back to a known good combination, is different from versioning the code. The scenario of wanting to go back to an older model and finding the vendor has stopped supporting it is a real risk for us, because our customers are in regulated industries and an unplanned behaviour change is not something they will accept quietly. The suggestion of splitting by segment, keeping the new model where it performs better and the old one where it does not, is a more practical answer than treating the upgrade as all or nothing.

The token economics section explained something I had seen without understanding. Products survive by routing, caching and tiering rather than by sending everything to the best model, and the railway analogy of different trains on the same track made it clear. It also means our pricing and our architecture are the same conversation, which is not how we treat them today. The noisy neighbour problem is the one I expect to meet first, because our larger customers will consume disproportionately on shared infrastructure, and we have no fair use position written down.

## Class 12:

The explanation of bias as a consequence of exposure rather than of bad intent was the clearest version of this I have heard. The example of a small child who believes a security guard can only be one kind of person, because that is all the child has seen, is exactly what happens to a model trained on a narrow history. It matters for us because almost everything our AI learns from is our customer's own past behaviour. If their historical data shows that certain accounts were never pursued, our scoring will quietly keep not pursuing them, and it will look like a pattern rather than a prejudice.

The data minimisation discussion extended something I wrote about earlier in the course. I had already accepted that we are custodians and not owners of customer data. What I had not thought through is that the temptation to collect more sits inside my own team and that I am the person who has to refuse it. Not asking for a field at all is a stronger control than collecting it and promising not to use it. Consent with a declared purpose, and a stated retention period, is also something our customers will increasingly ask us about rather than something we need to volunteer.

Teaching the product when to abstain is the point I want to take into our design discussions. Our instinct is to always return something, because an answer that says nothing looks like a failure. The faculty's position that abstaining is a legitimate and even preferred behaviour, and that it is cleaner commercially because you can choose not to charge for it, flips that. For a case summary, saying there is not enough information in this case to summarise reliably is far better than producing a confident paragraph that a rep then repeats to a customer.

The instruction that controls must be visible, and that we must never make a control look like it exists when it does not, is one I want to apply to our confidence indicators. If we show a confidence level, it has to mean something the user can act on, not just decorate the screen. The related point about logging both the AI recommendation and the human decision when they differ is something we are not doing consistently, and it is exactly the record we would need later to know whether the human reviews are adding value or whether people are just clicking through.

On governance, the rule that governance must scale faster than the number of agents is a line I will remember, because we are adding agentic flows faster than we are adding controls around them. I also liked the risk assessment being more than probability and severity. Asking whether we would even detect it, and whether we could reverse it, changes the answer for several things we have shipped. Writing down both the minimum criteria to launch and the conditions that would block a launch, and treating the space between them as my judgement call, is a more honest version of the go or no go conversation we actually have.

## Class 13:

The review of a team's specification was more useful to me than any slide. Watching words like accurate, user friendly, high risk and reduction in time get pulled apart one by one was a good reminder of how much ambiguity we leave in our own requirements. The framing that the document is a contract, and that every vague word is a negotiation you will have later with your own engineering team, is right. We lose time in exactly this way, usually at the point where the feature is demonstrated and somebody says this is not what I meant. Putting a number and a measurement method next to every claim is not bureaucracy, it is avoiding that conversation.

Target costing was new to me as a discipline, even though I knew the idea. We price after we build, which means we discover whether the economics work at the end. Starting from what the customer is willing to pay, subtracting the margin we need and deriving what we are allowed to spend, is the opposite order and it would have changed at least one decision we made this year. It also makes the build versus platform choice I wrote about earlier a financial question and not only a maintainability question.

The distinction between what the customer values and what costs us is the part I will think about longest. The customer is buying the outcome. Our cost sits on the input. Outcome based pricing earns more and transfers the risk to us, and whether we can carry that risk depends on how consistently the product delivers, which for AI features is not yet a safe assumption. The example of an agentic loop running until it exhausts its budget without reaching the goal raises a question we have not answered, which is whether we charge for a run that produced nothing. I suspect the answer that builds trust is that we do not, and that we had better know how often that happens before we promise it.

Our pricing is based on cases created, which is closer to a usage proxy than to value. That worked when the cost of a case was broadly flat. With AI inside the workflow the cost per case is no longer flat, and a customer who uses the AI heavily on every case is a very different proposition from one who does not. Contribution margin by workflow path is the measurement I am missing. Without it I cannot tell which of our AI features we should be encouraging and which ones are quietly expensive, and the risk the faculty described, where adoption grows and the business gets weaker, is a real one.

The examples of licensing costs reshaping architecture, where the way a database charged changed how the system was designed, made the link between finance and design concrete. It also explains something about our own platform constraints that I had only seen from the engineering side. The closing point on ecosystems is one I agree with. We do not compete product against product, we compete as part of a stack, and the decision about whether our AI capability is a separate product, a premium tier or the reason the rest of the suite gets adopted is a positioning decision I would rather make deliberately than drift into.
