The faculty shared a framework he has not published yet, so I am recording it carefully. TFIR: truth, facts, inferences, recommendations. Truth is whether you have reliable data at all, and saying honestly that you do not have it is also truth. Facts come from lookups against a real source, and hallucination there can be pushed close to zero. Inferences are data driven and largely controllable. Recommendations are the risky rung, and the way to make them safe is to show the chain of evidence underneath, so that a person who spots a wrong fact can immediately distrust the recommendation resting on it.

That is our product described from the outside by someone who has never seen it, and it is a better description than ours. Our rule that a claim with no resolvable source record is not shown at all is the truth and facts rung. Our ranked list is inference. Our single proposed action from a fixed list is a recommendation, which means it is the part carrying almost all of the risk, and we have been treating all four as one system with one confidence number.

**Where I disagree, and it matters for us.** The faculty said that if you are completely unsure, maybe you should not show it. For most products that is right. For ours it is dangerous. If an engagement is missing from the briefing because we were unsure, the manager cannot tell that apart from the engagement being fine. Silence reads as reassurance. That is exactly the failure we are most afraid of. So our version has to be that low confidence items are still shown, marked as unjudged, with the raw evidence attached. Showing nothing is a decision that hides itself.

**The pricing part changed how I think about our value case.** He walked through the distribution company's own economics: power bought on forward contracts at four or five rupees, sold at ten or twelve, and an outage means power already purchased and paid for that cannot be sold. So fixing a fault fast is not a service improvement, it is recovered revenue sitting in their accounts. The value of your product is found in the customer's profit and loss, not in your feature list.

I have never looked at a client's numbers that way in eight years. We describe our product as saving a delivery manager three and a half hours a week. That is a cost line and a small one. The real number is revenue retained on engagements that would otherwise have gone bad, and I have not gone looking for it.

**The vendor risk I had been avoiding.** Your subscription model can be deprecated, your provider can change behaviour after launch, or the provider can improve so much that your product stops making economic sense. We are built on Agentforce. If the platform ships portfolio risk scoring natively, our economics are gone. I have known this and filed it as an acceptable trade off rather than as something with a date on it.

**One line I am taking personally.** Play customer support for your own product. I am the build lead and I have never run our weekly review myself.
