Session 3 Reflection

Date: 18 August 2026

Topic: Users, Progress and Sustainable Advantage

The thought I carried out of this class is a simple one, but it changed how I look at progress: shipping more features is not the same as moving forward. A team can complete a long list of features, yet if none of them helps the intended user reach a meaningful outcome, the product only becomes more complicated.
We spent time on who the user really is, and I realised how loosely I had been using the words "user" and "customer." For one product, the person who uses the system, the person who approves the purchase and the administrator who manages it can all expect very different things. Something that makes life easier for one of them may create extra work for another. So a good PM has to say clearly whose problem is being solved.
The caterpillar and butterfly example gave me a picture I will not forget. Making an existing process a little faster is improvement, but transformation means the work itself is done in a completely different way. I also found the discussion on organisational incentives very honest. Sometimes teams spread small improvements across many releases because they need visible results for the next performance cycle. That may look good in an internal report, but it may not be the best outcome for the product.
Product layering was another idea that made sense to me. A scalable product has a common core, a domain or industry layer, and only limited customer-specific configuration. If every customer request goes straight into the core, the product slowly turns into a pile of customised projects. This showed me that saying no to some requests is not a failure. It is part of responsible product management.
The last point felt the most relevant to today's world. AI has made prototypes and basic features much easier to build, so the ability to build a feature is no longer a strong advantage on its own. A lasting advantage is more likely to come from proprietary data, deep integration into customer workflows, trust, governance and how difficult the product is to replace.
I leave this session understanding that real progress is measured by the value users receive, not by the number of features released.
