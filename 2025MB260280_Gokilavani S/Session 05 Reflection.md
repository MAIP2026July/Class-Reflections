Session 5 Reflection

Date: 29 August 2026

Topic: Identifying Useful AI Opportunities

This session reminded me that the first step in any AI idea is not the technology, but the problem. We talked about how to find problems where AI can create real value instead of adding it just because it is popular. A few questions guide this: how often does the problem occur, how painful is it, is suitable data available, and how important is the decision involved?
One distinction I found very useful was between products that help a user finish a task and leave, and products where continued engagement is part of the value. An internal business application should help an employee complete the work quickly. If we measure its success by time spent, we may get the wrong signal, because a longer visit may only mean the tool is slow or confusing. This made me look at the software I use at work in a new way.
The ceiling fan example showed me why a product claim needs evidence. Technical words and AI labels can win attention at first, but customers will soon ask for proof. A product should be able to show what became faster, more accurate, cheaper or easier after AI was introduced. I also learned not to mistake early curiosity for product-market fit. Repeated use and continued value are the stronger signs.
The four-layer AI stack gave me a simple way to think about any AI product: Data, Model, API and User Experience. A problem can start at any layer, but the user almost always feels it through the interface. So a technically strong model is not enough if the experience is confusing or unreliable.
We also came back to switching costs and human oversight. Even a better solution can struggle if people must move between tools, type the same information again or change their whole routine. In the same way, sending every AI output for human review leads to alert fatigue. Human attention is best spent where confidence is low or the cost of a mistake is high.
I leave this session with a clearer habit of mind: before getting excited about AI, I should ask whether the problem is real, whether there is proof of value, and whether people will truly want to use it.
