Session 09 Reflection

Date: 10 October 2026

Topic: AI Product Metrics, Economic Cost of Errors, Tail Risk, Experimentation and Feedback Loops

In this session, I learnt how to think about a product by an AI. Technical accuracy of a model doesn't necessarily mean a functional product. While accuracy, precision and recall are helpful and I will continue to employ them, a product manager needs to know what both his or her business and his or her customer will experience if the model is incorrect.

Something that would be particularly helpful for me to grasp was the discussion on how false positives affect and could be employed in reverse to the negative result. Both are mistakes but not necessarily with the same repercussions. In lending situation, if the customer fails to repay the bank, it means a real loss of income of the bank. When a business opportunity goes down the drain, it's going without participating in a good customer. The conflict had even taught me to appreciate, the cost of other types of errors, rather than the slightly improved accuracy mark.

My thinking thought is tail risk. While a model can yield great performance in most scenarios, it could get bogged down in a few critical scenarios. Comforting is the medium score, but not all scores are equal, especially if the repercussions if you get it wrong are extreme.
Another thing that I appreciated was the connection between the technical metrics and business metrics. While having lots of KPIs is useful to know if the product is successful or not, it makes it more difficult to see the woods for the trees so it may be best to limit the number of KPIs tracked and only the ones that support the achievement of the product purpose, e.g. efficiency, reduction of losses, increase in conversion or customer experience. Despite the reassuring headline metrics, there are elements that can be trouble under the surface; therefore, there is a place for both leading and lagging indicators.

I learned that I have to be a little more careful about making my own conclusions with A/B testing. After launch, if the metric is improving, its easy to attribute the improvement to the new feature. A fair comparison, sufficient data and real evidence is required before it can be said that the feature caused it. But it was a good reminder of telling it like it is.

The number of times an AI product is correct is not the only metric I learned about measuring an AI product. But it is crucial to recognise what the impact is when the model is misapplied. For me, as a product manager, I will be required to strike the balance between model performance and business value, impact and risk on the customers. It's like the core of the work fits with me, and I'm happy to be working that out now.
