Session 12 Reflection

Date: 31 October 2026

Topic: AI Product Economics, Value-Based Pricing, Target Costing, Unit Economics and Platform Strategy

Many people think the good product will ‘obviously' lead to a good business. That proved to be wrong during this session! The value AI products can provide increases with each use, but if the expenses for providing that value remain high, it can be a financial problem. In the end, it's not only whether or not customers enjoy the product; it's whether or not the economics make sense when there's heavy usage.

Some teams would rather come up with an idea, design it and then think about the cost.Teams often tend to make up their minds about the price tag after developing the idea and designing it. In the target costing discussion, it was this that changed all that. First we look at the value brought, what the customer value and would pay for, then we go back to the cost to us to provide it. Planning a trip from destination and then picking a route – much like that.

What was most striking to me, was that the customers are paying for the results, not our billing. Tokens, API calls and model usage have a huge impact inside an account, but the customer doesn't really consider them. They consider thinking about things that they saved, that they cut down on work, better choices and jobs completed without hassle. Therefore, the product's language should reflect on the product itself and not the machine that created it.

The economics of AI was very palpable when it came to the business side. The more people that use and the more use they receive, does not and will not automatically translate into more income and profit, because the reinforcement of every additional interaction can incur an additional cost. That's why the selection of the appropriate model, architecture and platform is never a purely technical one. It's a business decision as well, and I think that a product manager and/or an engineer should make it together.

They moved on to talking about measurable requirements and I got a little uneasy, but in a useful,Read-Writable-Ugly way. For example, when you use terms such as fast, accurate or user-friendly in a meeting, everyone may have a different definition of what you mean by the word. Convert them into targets and make them measurable if they can be to determine, fairly, if the product has met its purpose.

The ultimate lesson that I learned is taking the AI product economics to the design room and not the review room after it launches. A sustainable product must have a multitude of factors: customer value, pricing/cost, growth of the product, architecture and scalability; each one of them is affecting the other. This session makes me feeling more confident about what it's truly about to build something that lasts.
