Session 2 Reflection

Date: 1 August 2026

Topic: Product and Project Thinking

This session made me pause and rethink a few words I had been using casually. The first was "product." A project usually starts with one known customer and a clear requirement. A product has to solve a similar problem for many customers. I realised I have seen solutions called products when they were really built around a single customer's needs, and I will look at such claims more carefully from now on.
I also learned that not every decision has to be made on day one. The house construction example made this simple for me. The foundation and structure must be fixed early because they are very hard to change later, but the colour of the walls can wait. Product development works the same way. The big, hard-to-reverse decisions deserve attention first, and the smaller ones can stay open until we know more. What matters is that open decisions are shared clearly, so nobody is confused or surprised later.
The discussion on beta releases honestly surprised me. I used to think beta meant the product was almost finished. The credit card example showed that beta may only mean the visible features are ready, while settlements, customer support and exception handling still need a lot of work. The lesson is that a team should not spend all its energy just reaching beta.
We also covered frameworks like Jobs to Be Done, RICE, Kano, QFD and the Traceability Matrix. I came away feeling that these tools are there to guide our thinking, not to think for us. The idea I valued most was traceability. A business decision should be visible in the requirements, the system design, the sprint backlog and the testing. Without that, a decision made in a management meeting may never reach the final product, and that seems to me a quiet but serious risk.
Finally, I understood the difference between an AI-enabled and an AI-native product. An AI-enabled product adds AI to improve an existing workflow, while an AI-native product could not exist without AI. I was also relieved to learn that an AI Product Manager does not need to build models. They do need enough understanding to discuss trade-offs such as cost, speed, accuracy and risk with the technical team.
Overall, this session taught me that good product thinking is about timing, clarity and connection, not just features.
