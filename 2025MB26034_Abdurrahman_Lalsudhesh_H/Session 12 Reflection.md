# Session 12 Reflection

**Date:** 31 October 2026  
**Topic:** AI Product Economics, Value-Based Pricing, Target Costing, Unit Economics & Platform Strategy  

The biggest mindset shift from today’s session was understanding that **a good product and a good business are not automatically the same thing.** We can solve a genuine customer problem, build an effective AI solution, and even achieve strong adoption, but the product still needs an economic model that works as usage grows. The principle of starting with the value delivered to the customer and working backwards changed how I think about pricing, cost, and even product architecture.

**Key takeaways & thoughts:**

* **Start with Customer Value and Work Backwards:** Target costing reverses the usual engineering mindset of calculating the development cost first and then adding a margin. Instead, we first understand the economic value created for the customer, estimate what they are realistically willing to pay, determine the margin required for the business, and then derive the cost envelope within which the product must operate. This makes profitability a product-design constraint rather than something considered only after development.

* **Customers Buy Outcomes, Not Our Input Costs:** AI providers may charge us based on tokens, API calls, compute, or model usage, but those aren't necessarily meaningful units of value for the customer. Customers care about the outcome: a qualified lead, a completed task, a faster decision, reduced operational effort, or an avoided loss. This creates an important distinction between how the product incurs cost and how it should communicate and potentially price the value it delivers.

* **Outcome-Based Pricing Also Transfers Risk:** Charging based on customer outcomes can align pricing more closely with value, but it also transfers greater responsibility to the product company. If an AI system generates a high-quality sales lead but the customer's sales team fails to follow up, the final sale is no longer completely within the product's control. Outcome-based pricing therefore works best when the product can clearly define, measure, and influence the outcome being priced. Otherwise, the commercial model can create disputes over attribution rather than stronger alignment.

* **Requirements Need Measurable Definitions:** Words such as "accurate," "fast," "user-friendly," or "reduce processing time" sound like requirements but leave too much room for interpretation. A stronger PRD converts these into measurable expectations: how accurate, how fast, and what percentage reduction is expected. This connects directly to the earlier discussions on experimentation and governance. If success isn't measurable before development begins, it becomes difficult to objectively determine whether the product actually succeeded.

* **Workflow Visualization Exposes Where AI Actually Belongs:** Swimlane diagrams provide more than documentation because they make ownership and hand-offs visible across users, systems, AI components, and human reviewers. In an AI workflow, this can clearly separate deterministic steps from probabilistic decisions and show where human intervention, escalation, and fallback mechanisms are required. It becomes easier to redesign the complete workflow rather than simply inserting AI into an existing process.

* **PMs Need Enough Architecture Knowledge to Understand Product Trade-offs:** The C4 model reinforced that product managers don't need to operate at code-level detail, but they should understand the important systems, components, interfaces, dependencies, and data flows behind the product. Architecture affects latency, scalability, vendor dependency, reliability, privacy, and ultimately cost. Understanding the architecture therefore improves product decisions without requiring the PM to become the system architect.

* **Unit Economics Matter More Than Customer Count Alone:** AI changes the economics of scaling because every additional interaction can carry inference and infrastructure costs. More customers and higher usage don't automatically translate into better profitability. Contribution margin helps reveal which customer segments, pricing tiers, or workflows actually create economic value after variable costs are considered. This extends the scalability discussion from the previous session: a product has to scale economically as well as technically.

* **Retention Doesn't Always Mean Upselling:** Growth is often associated with moving customers toward larger plans, but downselling can sometimes be the better long-term product decision. A customer receiving enough value from a smaller plan may be worth retaining rather than forcing them into a higher tier and eventually losing them completely. Pricing therefore needs to support different levels of customer value rather than treating maximum short-term revenue as the only objective.

* **Platform Economics Can Become a Product Moat:** Some products become increasingly valuable as more participants join the ecosystem, which makes them difficult to replace even when competing products offer better individual features. This creates another strategic question for AI products: should AI itself be the product, or should it strengthen an existing workflow or platform? In many cases, embedding AI inside an established product may create greater adoption and defensibility than asking users to move into an entirely separate AI application.

The broader lesson for me is that **AI product economics should influence product design from the beginning rather than being calculated after the product is built.** Customer value determines what we can reasonably charge, while architecture, model selection, usage patterns, and operational requirements determine what it costs us to deliver that value. Sustainable AI products need both sides of that equation to work. Growing adoption is valuable only when the product can continue delivering meaningful customer outcomes with healthy and scalable unit economics.
