# Session 5 Reflection

**Date:** 29 August 2026  
**Topic:** Finding AI Opportunities, Product Types, Evidence & 4-Layer AI Stack  

The core theme today was learning how to systematically identify real AI opportunities rather than forcing AI into every problem. As product managers, we have to evaluate the **frequency, intensity of pain, data availability, and decision importance** before justifying an investment in AI. 

**Key takeaways & thoughts:**

* **"Finish and Leave" vs. "Stay" Products:** A critical distinction was made between utility products designed to help users finish their job and leave (where engagement metrics shouldn't be forced) versus content/entertainment products designed to keep users engaged (e.g., Netflix, Prime Video). Internal enterprise tools should strictly optimize for speed and efficiency, not artificial "time spent inside the app."
* **Hollow AI Claims & Evidence:** The ceiling-fan "double bearing" analogy was spot on—customers see through hollow jargon. Claiming that AI improves speed or accuracy isn't enough; sustained product-market fit requires clear before-and-after evidence. Initial enthusiasm or novelty is not product-market fit; repeated, habit-forming usage is.
* **The 4-Layer AI Stack & Front-line Experience:** Evaluating AI requires looking at the full stack: *Data (Input) $\rightarrow$ Model (Intelligence) $\rightarrow$ API (Bridge) $\rightarrow$ User Experience (Output)*. Users always notice where the product breaks first at the UX layer, regardless of how complex the underlying model is.
* **Overcoming Switching Costs:** A solution can be objectively superior, but if it forces users to relearn an established workflow, re-enter data, or jump between extra systems, friction will kill adoption. PMs must account for front-line operational effort, not just technical capability.
* **Alert Fatigue in Human-in-the-Loop:** Having a human approval step doesn't automatically make an AI system safe. If the model generates constant routine recommendations, reviewers experience fatigue and start blindly approving outputs without exercising real judgment. Human oversight should be reserved for low-confidence, high-consequence edge cases.
