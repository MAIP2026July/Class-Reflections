Session 3 Reflection:

1. Who Exactly Is the User?

A small thing from this session that I think will stay with me is to stop writing "user" or "customer" without saying which one. What I mean by user may be different from what my colleague understands. If I am the one creating that confusion, it is my fault.

I am guilty of this in my own work. Our requirement documents say "the user should be able to..." and never say which user. I want to start naming the user properly and thinking about their situation, like when and where they will use the product.

2. Product, Not Project

The biggest learning for me was the difference between a product and a project. A project is built for one customer. A product is built for many customers we do not know yet. We can start with one customer, but we should keep thinking about the generic version and keep the customer specific parts separate.

I have seen at work how one big client's requests slowly change the core product until it no longer suits anyone else. I understand now that a product manager sometimes needs to say no. I still feel this is easier said than done when that client brings in a lot of money.

3. The Target Keeps Moving

I also understood that a product cannot be put on autopilot. Customers change, competitors change, and with AI even the customer might decide to build the solution themselves. So we have to keep correcting course.

The part I found useful was that it is okay to change our view when the world has changed, as long as we can explain why. I think this needs some courage in front of management.

4. Scrum From the Team's Side

I have used Scrum for a few years, but this session made me look at it differently. If the product manager keeps changing direction every few days, it is unsettling for the team. A sprint is like an agreement that nothing changes for that period, and after that we can change whatever we want. I had always seen sprints as a delivery method, not as a way of protecting the team.

5. Being Honest About Transformation

Another point I want to remember is to be honest about whether something is a real transformation or just an improvement. Both are fine, but calling a small improvement a transformation only confuses everyone. Frameworks came up again, and my takeaway was the same as last time: use them when they help the decision, and skip them when they are only ceremony.

6. My Takeaway Going Forward

Going forward, I want to name every user I write about, keep the generic product in mind even when the request comes from one client, and be more open with my team about when and why the direction is changing.
