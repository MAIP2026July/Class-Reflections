## 1. Key concepts
The first class was mostly about how the course will run and what a product manager really does.

A few ground rules stood out. We can use LLMs for assignments, but we have to disclose which one, the model, the date, the prompts and the output. These reflections are meant to be a personal journal, written before the next class, and they carry up to 10 of the 30 marks.

The main idea of the day was that the PM is the CEO of the product. The PM owns the problem and the outcome, and can't blame marketing or engineering when something fails. If an internal team can't deliver, it's the PM's job to find another way. At the same time, being accountable doesn't mean being in every meeting. We have to trust the team and decide what to delegate.

The professor described the PM's job as direction, evidence, trade-offs and escalation. He also said a PM is paid more not because they are always right, but because they make more good decisions than bad ones. And a PM who doesn't understand the domain is "just a postman". Classmates added two more points: sometimes the PM has to kill a product, and after launch someone has to check whether the business KPIs were actually met.

## 2. What changed in my thinking
I always saw the PM as the bridge between business and engineering. "CEO of the product" asks for more than that. I carry the risk even for things I don't control. It's a bit uncomfortable, but I think it's fair. I also liked the cricket line: if you score a century and the team still loses, you've still lost.

## 3. How I will apply it
In my DSD receiving work, I want to stop saying a problem belongs to store operations or to the vendor team. For VendorPulse, my capstone, the outcome I own is fewer unstaffed vendor arrivals and less waiting time, not just a working model. I'll also keep a log of my LLM use from the start, since disclosure is required.
