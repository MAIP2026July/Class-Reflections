## 1. Key concepts
The class started with topic approvals in the AssignmentBriefing repo. The professor reminded us that the product must solve a problem for many customers. Last year some teams built for one customer, which made it a project, and they had to scramble at the end. He also said not to start from the technology. Start from a problem customers would pay to have solved.

On the PRD, he described it as customer requests organised properly. Customers don't say "use this technology", they say "solve this for me". We should record which stakeholder asked for what, and if a section doesn't apply, say why instead of skipping it. The whole team should use the same terms everywhere: in documents, manuals and support. Things like "10,000 users at once" belong in a separate product specification. And he was clear that a PM who only builds clones doesn't have a job.

I liked his idea of reimagining the "black box". A problem turns inputs into outputs, and AI can replace or improve one step in the chain. He encouraged us to question old assumptions, like why exams have to be on one fixed day.

He also said stated needs aren't always real needs. A crying baby can't tell you what's wrong, and our samples are often biased.

On data, he said we are only custodians. Under the DPDP Act and GDPR we need consent for each purpose and must delete data when asked. He mentioned the Apple Card gender bias case and the importance of planning for cyber incidents. Without this, an AI product is "dead on arrival". He also pointed out that measuring something adds cost and delay, so we have to choose sensible measures.

The sentiment analysis part was interesting. One phone review talked about the screen, voice quality, battery and price, and also carried emotion: "my mother was mad I didn't tell her before buying". Sentiment has to be read per feature, per person and over time, and fake or paid reviews are a real problem.

He also said data choices are investment choices. For retail inventory, the goal is stock in the right place at the right time, not a better accuracy score. He explained the exam format (two cases, three parts each, about an hour per case) and asked us to name our reflection folders with only our roll number in capitals.

## 2. What changed in my thinking
"Model accuracy is not the problem statement" really applies to my work. In receiving, what matters is that the dock is staffed when the truck arrives. A better prediction score is only a means to that.

## 3. How I will apply it
VendorPulse is built for many chains and set up by configuration, so it stays a product. My PRD will use the same terms throughout (NEX vendor, receiving lead, arrival window) and note who asked for each requirement. Vendor emails and dock notes will be used only for scheduling, kept pseudonymised, and deleted when asked. I'll report business results, like staffed arrivals and less waiting time, next to the model numbers.

## 4. Open questions

- How do we stop the reliability score from being unfair to small local vendors?
