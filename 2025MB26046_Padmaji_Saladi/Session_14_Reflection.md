## 1. Key concepts
This was the second presentation day, with many more teams. One line from the professor sums it up: "How well you can subcontract the work is how strong your PRD is." He also said a recommendation isn't enough on its own. It has to be available and usable in the real workflow.

Feedback I noted:
- **Know both sides.** Teams had to say what's in it for us as the vendor and what's in it for the customer. He asked what the work costs to do by hand today, told one team to recheck their formulas, and reminded everyone that customers will always weigh building it themselves. When the numbers don't add up, a VC sees warning signs straight away.
- **Name a real customer.** A company with 20 departments using the tool is still one customer, and the promise decides how much they'll pay.
- **Take a fair share of the value.** If the customer saves $1.5M, about 20% of that is a fair price. You can't charge more than the value you bring. He asked teams to check engineering and hosting costs with experts, and to study competitors' offers, prices and market share.
- **Keep savings realistic.** You can't save someone 8 hours in an 8-hour day. With Surgery IQ he pointed out that salaried doctors don't turn saved minutes into cash, and asked whether reliable data even exists to train the model.
- **Without integrations, still show why you're better.** DealFit had to explain why creators should choose it over tools that already handle deal emails automatically.
- **Sharing knowledge isn't sharing authority.** For the AI Twin team, access rules between departments have to be configurable. Documents go out of date, so each source needs its own refresh cycle. He also said knowledge for its own sake is waste.
- **Ask what the user gains.** For a developer tool, he reminded the team that "the bottleneck was never coding".
- **Unit cost must fit the price.** If the tyres cost more than the car's selling price, it doesn't work. Market size decides how many copies you can sell.
- **Partner where it makes sense.** For SessionGuard, selling through Citrix or VDI platforms might work better than competing with protections those platforms already have.

## 2. What changed in my thinking
Hearing teams from different industries make the same mistakes showed me that the professor's questions work as a checklist:
- Who exactly is the customer?
- What does it cost them today?
- What do they gain?
- Why us and not their own build or an existing tool?
- What is our unit cost against our price?

## 3. How I will apply it
I'll run VendorPulse through that checklist again:
- **Customer:** a 300-store supercenter chain, buying through the VP of Store Operations.
- **Cost today:** associates waiting, docks left unstaffed, and shrink.
- **Savings:** associates are paid hourly, so saved time can be moved to other work. I still need to make sure I'm not counting it twice.
- **Competition:** I'll strengthen my analysis against receiving features in existing warehouse and store systems.
- **Unit cost:** the $14 per store per month to serve, against a $99 price, needs to hold even if people use the chat a lot.
