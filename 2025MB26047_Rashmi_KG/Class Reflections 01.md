Class Reflection 1: Ownership, Judgment and the Product Manager Mindset

Course: Management of AI Products
 Session: 1
 Date: 25 July 2026

The first session made me rethink what it really means to manage a product. Coming from a software development background, I have naturally looked at successful delivery through the lens of requirements, technical implementation, timelines, dependencies, issue resolution, and coordination across teams. While these continue to be important, this session made me realize that product management demands a much broader sense of ownership for the final outcome.

The idea that stayed with me most was to think of the product manager as the “CEO of the product.” The discussion emphasized that the product manager owns the problem and the outcome and cannot simply attribute failure to engineering, marketing, or another function. This immediately connected with my own experience. In my role, I work across multiple technical and functional teams, and achieving an outcome often depends on people over whom I do not have direct authority. Until now, I tended to view such situations mainly as coordination or dependency-management challenges. This session gave me a different perspective. Not having authority does not remove accountability.

Personal Example from My Professional Experience

I could relate this idea very closely to my experience managing Spectrum EFT and DataSafe-related work, where successful delivery often requires coordination across development, functional, implementation, and other dependent teams. There have been situations where my team's development activities were progressing as planned, but the overall outcome still depended on inputs, decisions, or actions from teams outside my direct reporting structure. Earlier, I would have primarily treated such situations as dependency management, focusing on follow-ups, status tracking, and ensuring that my team's responsibilities were completed. After this session, I see the same situation differently. If I am accountable for the overall outcome, my responsibility should extend beyond tracking the dependency. I need to anticipate it early, explain its business impact, bring the right stakeholders together, escalate when necessary, and explore alternatives before it becomes a delivery risk. This made the concept of “influencing without authority” very real for me because it closely reflects situations I already encounter in my work.

This reflection is particularly relevant to my current role because coordinating complex technology initiatives has already shown me that successful outcomes depend less on formal authority and more on early alignment, effective communication, timely escalation, and the ability to influence multiple stakeholders toward a common objective.

I found this both challenging and realistic. There have been situations in my professional experience where I have done everything expected from my role, yet the overall outcome still depended on another team, stakeholder, or organizational decision. My natural tendency has sometimes been to separate what was “within my control” from what was not. The session made me question that mindset. If I truly own an outcome, perhaps the more useful question is not“Did I complete my responsibility?” but “What else could I have done to influence the outcome?” This could mean creating alignment earlier, communicating the business impact more clearly, escalating at the right time, challenging an assumption, or finding an alternative path.

At the same time, I do not interpret the “CEO of the product” idea as having unlimited authority. In large organizations, decisions are influenced by architecture, security, compliance, budgets, resource availability, leadership priorities, and organizational processes. My interpretation is therefore ownership without the expectation of complete control. A strong product manager should respect necessary governance while still taking responsibility for navigating it rather than treating it as someone else's problem.

Another discussion that particularly resonated with my technical background was the example of choosing the second-best database rather than the technically superior but specialized option. The decision was driven by the future availability of people who could support and enhance the product. I could relate to this because, as technical professionals, we can naturally become attracted to the most elegant or advanced solution. However, what is technically best may not always be what is best for the product.

This was an important reminder for me. When evaluating a technology, I should not ask only “Is this technically superior?” I should also consider whether the organization can support it, whether the required skills are available, what it will cost to maintain, whether it creates dependency, and whether it remains the right choice as the product evolves. This becomes even more relevant for AI products, where technology is changing extremely quickly.

The discussion on trade-offs and decision-making also made a strong impression on me. Management was described as continuously making decisions where there may not be one perfectly correct answer. What matters is developing the judgment to make more good decisions than poor ones based on the information and assumptions available. I found this reassuring because, especially in technology, there is often an expectation to find the “right” answer. Product management seems to require a different mindset: make the best defensible decision with the available evidence, understand the trade-offs, and remain prepared to change direction when new information emerges.

One concept that was completely new to how I had organized my thinking was the distinction between the three ways AI can relate to product management: using AI to build products, using AI to perform product-management activities, and embedding AI within the product so that customers directly experience it. Before this session, I tended to think of an “AI product” primarily as a product containing an AI capability. I now see that AI can also fundamentally change how products are conceived, researched, documented, developed and managed.

How I Would Apply This Learning

The most immediate change I would like to make in my work is to become more outcome-oriented rather than activity-oriented. Instead of considering a workstream successful because development was completed, a release happened, or an assigned task was closed, I want to step back and ask whether the intended business or customer outcome was actually achieved.

I would also like to broaden the criteria I use when evaluating technical choices. Technical performance will remain important, but I would consciously include factors such as maintainability, skills availability, operational readiness, cost, scalability, organizational constraints, and long-term sustainability.

Where I Would Apply It with Caution

The one area where I would qualify my learning is the idea of complete ownership. I agree with the mindset, but in a large enterprise there will always be legitimate limits to individual authority. Security, compliance, architecture and regulatory controls cannot simply be bypassed in the name of product ownership.

For me, therefore, the valuable lesson is not “I control everything”, but rather “I should not use what I cannot control as an excuse for disengaging from the outcome.” That distinction feels both practical and applicable to my working environment.

My Key Takeaway

The biggest shift in my thinking after this session is that product management is less about coordinating the creation of a product and more about owning the outcome created by that product.

I entered the session thinking primarily from the perspective of someone responsible for technology and execution. I came away thinking much more about ownership, judgment, influence and trade-offs.

The question I would like to carry forward from this session is:

“If I truly owned this product and its outcome, what would I do differently?”

For me, that single question captures the most valuable learning from the first class.
