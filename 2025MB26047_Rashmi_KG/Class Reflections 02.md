Class Reflection 2: Moving from Feature Delivery to Product Outcomes

Course: Management of AI Products
 Session: 2
 Date: 1 August 2026

My Reflection

The second session made me reflect on the difference between delivering a product and making a product successful. In software development, completing requirements, clearing the backlog, meeting release timelines and ensuring technical stability are often treated as signs of success. However, the discussion made me realize that a product delivered on time can still fail if customers do not adopt it or if it does not create the intended value.

The question raised in the class,“The team shipped on time, but adoption failed. How would you own it and convert the product into a successful one?”, stayed with me. It challenged my natural delivery-oriented mindset. When a technically sound release does not achieve adoption, it is easy to assume that the issue belongs to marketing, sales, training, implementation or the customer. From a product-management perspective, however, low adoption is still part of the product outcome.

This connected closely with my own professional experience. I have worked on software initiatives where considerable effort was invested in clarifying requirements, managing dependencies, coordinating technical teams and ensuring successful delivery. My attention has often been concentrated on whether the requested functionality was completed correctly and on schedule. The session made me question whether I have always examined what happened after delivery with the same level of attention.

A feature being available does not mean it is being used. Similarly, usage does not necessarily mean that the customer is receiving meaningful value from it. I now see the need to look beyond completion status and examine questions such as:

Did users understand the feature?
Did it fit naturally into their existing workflow?
Was implementation or integration difficult?
Did the product solve an important enough problem?
Did the customer require training, configuration or continuing support?
Were we measuring adoption and outcomes, or only successful deployment?

This shift from output to outcome is one of my most important learnings from the session.

Decisions Before Frameworks

Another idea I found extremely useful was that product managers should begin with the decision that needs to be made, rather than beginning with a framework. The session emphasized naming the decision, understanding its consequences, considering the evidence and trade-offs, and then choosing the minimum framework required to support it.

This was personally relevant because, in both academic and professional settings, it can be tempting to use a familiar framework simply because it provides structure. However, applying several frameworks does not necessarily improve the quality of a decision. Sometimes it can make the analysis longer without making it clearer.

My key realization was that a framework should help me answer a question, not replace my judgment. Before using a framework, I should therefore ask:

What specific decision am I trying to make, and what information would allow me to make it responsibly?

For example, if adoption is low, the immediate need may not be a broad market analysis. The more useful first step may be to identify where users are dropping out, speak with them, examine implementation difficulties and test whether the product is solving the intended problem. The framework should follow the decision context.

Expanding My Understanding of AI Product Management

The session also helped me understand what distinguishes an AI product manager from a traditional product manager. Traditional product-management abilities such as understanding users, prioritizing requirements and managing the backlog remain necessary. However, AI introduces additional considerations such as data, evaluation, uncertainty, intellectual-property implications and trade-offs between different outcomes.

This corrected one of my earlier assumptions. Because of my technical background, I could easily begin an AI discussion with the model or algorithm. However, the class made it clear that starting with“Which model should we use?” is too narrow. Before selecting a model, I need to understand:

What user or business outcome is expected?
What data is required, and whether it can be used legitimately
How the output will be evaluated
What level of uncertainty is acceptable
What happens when the model is wrong
Whether human review is required
Whether the value justifies the operational and governance costs

This was a meaningful change in my thinking. The model is an important component, but it is not the product by itself.

Owning the Complete Product Promise

The discussion on professional services also gave me a new perspective. The class explained that many products require configuration, integration, validation, customization or implementation support before the customer can experience the value promised by the product. Even when an internal team or external partner performs this work, the product manager continues to own the overall product promise.

I could relate this to enterprise software, particularly products involving complex client environments, conversions and integrations. In such settings, the standard product may be technically complete, but the customer receives value only after it has been configured and integrated into the surrounding environment.

Previously, I might have viewed implementation as something that happens after product development. I now understand it as part of the complete customer experience. If implementation repeatedly requires workarounds or extensive customization, this is not merely a services problem. It may indicate a product-design, documentation, configurability or usability gap.

The same issue becomes even more important for AI products. Since AI produces a range of possible outcomes rather than perfectly predictable results, implementation teams must understand failure modes, validate behavior in the customer’s environment, identify when human review is required, and establish a recovery path when the AI output is unsuitable.

How I Would Apply This Learning

I would apply this learning by expanding the way I define success. In addition to tracking development completion and release readiness, I would include:

user activation and adoption;
successful implementation and integration;
repeated use of the product;
customer effort and support dependency;
measurable business or operational outcomes;
AI-specific quality, uncertainty and escalation measures.

For work involving multiple technical and functional teams, I would also try to identify the complete customer journey rather than concentrating only on the development portion under my immediate control.

If adoption is weak, I would avoid immediately requesting more features. I would first determine whether the issue is related to problem relevance, usability, trust, implementation difficulty, training, performance, integration or organizational resistance. The response should depend on evidence rather than assumption.

Where I Would Apply It with Caution

I agree that the product manager owns the complete product promise. However, ownership should not result in the product manager becoming involved in every operational detail or taking over the responsibilities of specialists. In a complex organization, implementation teams, engineers, architects, support teams, legal specialists and data scientists each have distinct responsibilities.

My interpretation is that the product manager should ensure that these responsibilities collectively support the intended outcome. The role is not to perform every activity personally, but to identify gaps, clarify ownership, resolve trade-offs and make sure that the customer is not left between organizational boundaries.

I would also apply frameworks selectively. Avoiding unnecessary frameworks does not mean avoiding structured analysis. For high-impact or regulated AI decisions, formal evaluation and governance may be essential. The important point is to use structure because it improves the decision, not merely because it makes the work appear comprehensive.

My Key Takeaway

My strongest takeaway from this session is that shipping the product is only one milestone; achieving customer value is the real measure of success.

The session moved my thinking beyond technical delivery and made me consider adoption, implementation, evidence, uncertainty and the full product promise. It also taught me to begin with the decision and use only the frameworks that genuinely help me make that decision.

The question I would carry forward from this session is:

“If the product was delivered successfully but the customer did not receive the expected value, can I genuinely call it a successful delivery?”

My answer after this class is no. As a product leader, I must look beyond whether the team built what was requested and examine whether the product achieved what it was intended to achieve.
