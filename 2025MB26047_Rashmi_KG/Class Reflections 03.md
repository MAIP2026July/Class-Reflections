Class Reflection 3: Protecting the Product Vision While Continuously Adapting

Course: Management of AI Products
 Session: 3
 Date: 18 August 2026

My Reflection

The third session made me reflect deeply on a challenge that feels very familiar from my own professional experience: how do we respond to individual customer requirements without gradually turning a reusable product into a collection of customer-specific solutions?

Coming from an enterprise software environment, I have seen how important customer requests can become. When a customer has an immediate requirement, the natural instinct is to find a solution. Technically, it may even be possible to accommodate it. However, this session helped me recognize that the more important product-management question is not simply“Can we build it?” but“Should this become part of the core product?”

The discussion around beta customers particularly resonated with me. The class emphasized that even when we are working closely with a beta customer, highly specific requirements should be kept separate if incorporating them would compromise the integrity of the core product. When a requirement provides value to only one customer, it may need to be treated as a separate project, with that customer bearing the corresponding development and maintenance cost.

This distinction between a product and a customer-specific project is something I found very relevant to my own work.

In enterprise software, particularly in areas involving implementations, conversions, integrations and custom programming, there is often pressure to accommodate legitimate customer-specific needs. I have generally viewed responsiveness to such requirements as part of good customer service. The session did not make me disagree with that view, but it added an important qualification: being customer-focused does not mean saying yes to every customer request.

Sometimes saying“no” may actually be the more responsible product decision.

Learning to Say “No” for the Right Reasons

The professor's discussion about developing the courage to say no to requirements that take a product too far down a customer-specific path was one of my strongest takeaways.

I found this particularly thought-provoking because saying yes often feels easier. A customer is satisfied in the short term, the immediate escalation is resolved, and progress is visible. Saying no requires a much stronger understanding of the product vision and the ability to explain why a request is not appropriate for the core product.

This made me reflect on how I would handle similar situations differently.

Before approving customization, I would now ask:

Does this problem exist for multiple customers?
Does the requirement strengthen our core product proposition?
Can the capability be designed generically enough to benefit a wider customer base?
Will it introduce additional complexity or long-term maintenance costs?
How will it affect the roadmap for other customers?
If it remains customer-specific, should it instead be handled separately as professional services or project work?

For me, the important learning is that good product management sometimes requires protecting the product from its own customers. Customer feedback is essential, but it still requires product judgment.

The Moving-Target Analogy Changed My Perspective

Another idea from the session that stayed with me was the analogy of the product and its market as moving targets. Customers evolve, competitors change, technology progresses, stakeholder expectations shift, and the product itself continues to develop. Therefore, the product manager cannot simply establish a roadmap and assume that following it faithfully will guarantee success. Continuous course correction is necessary.

I could connect this strongly with what I have experienced in technology.

We may start an initiative with a clear scope, architecture and delivery plan. By the time implementation progresses, priorities may have changed, new technical possibilities may have emerged, dependencies may have shifted, or customer expectations may have evolved.

Earlier, I might have interpreted significant deviation from the original plan primarily as a planning problem. After this session, I see it somewhat differently.

A change in direction is not automatically evidence that the original plan was poor. Sometimes it is evidence that the team is learning.

The real question is whether the course correction is based on meaningful new evidence or simply reactive decision-making.

This distinction is particularly important for AI products because the environment changes so rapidly. A model, platform or AI capability selected today may be surpassed much sooner than a traditional technology choice. The session therefore strengthened my view that an AI product roadmap should provide direction but should not become a rigid commitment.

Technology Should Serve the Product, Not Define It

I also strongly related to the discussion that product managers should not become emotionally attached to a particular technology. The professor's point was essentially that technologies are tools: if one creates the required value, use it; if it does not, use something else.

That sounds obvious, but with a technical background I know how easily technology itself can become interesting enough to drive the conversation.

A newer architecture, framework, AI model or approach can appear attractive simply because it is technically impressive. This session reinforced a principle I want to consciously apply:

I should not start with “Where can I use this technology?” I should start with “What problem am I trying to solve, and is this technology the best way to solve it?”

For AI product management, I believe this distinction is critical. Not every problem requires AI, just as not every AI problem requires the newest model.

Experiment, Learn and Then Invest

I found the discussion on experimentation especially practical. The class emphasized trying things leanly, validating the hypothesis before committing significant time, energy or money, and recording what was learned even when an experiment does not ultimately get incorporated into the product.

This changed how I think about unsuccessful experiments.

In a traditional delivery mindset, work that does not eventually enter production can sometimes feel wasted. In a product-learning mindset, an experiment that demonstrates that a technology is technically feasible but economically unviable has still produced something valuable: it has prevented potentially larger investment in the wrong direction.

I would like to apply this thinking more consciously in my own work. Rather than debating new technologies extensively based on assumptions, I would prefer a bounded experiment with:

a clear question or hypothesis;
defined acceptance criteria;
limited investment;
documented results; and
an explicit decision to adopt, defer or reject.

For AI products, where technologies are evolving extremely quickly, this feels much more sustainable than either adopting every new development or ignoring innovation until it becomes unavoidable.

How I Would Apply This Learning

I see three immediate applications to my professional context.

First, for customization requests, I would distinguish more consciously between core-product capability and customer-specific work. Where a requirement is valuable to multiple customers, it may belong in the product roadmap. Where it exists for only one client and cannot be generalized, I would consider isolating it rather than allowing it to increase core-product complexity.

Second, I would build deliberate reflection and course-correction points into longer initiatives. Instead of asking only whether execution is on schedule, I would also periodically ask:

“Are we still solving the right problem for the customer we are trying to serve?”

Third, when evaluating AI or other emerging technologies, I would favor small, clearly defined experiments before committing to broader implementation.

Where I Would Apply It with Caution

I would, however, be cautious about rejecting a requirement merely because only one customer has requested it.

Sometimes a single customer may be the first signal of an emerging market requirement. An important customer may also expose a problem that others have but have not articulated yet. Therefore, I would not use the number of requesting customers as the only criterion.

Instead of immediately saying either yes or no, I would ask whether the request reveals a broader unmet need and validate that assumption with other customers and market evidence.

Similarly, continuous course correction should not become continuous change. If priorities shift every time a new technology or customer request appears, the team may lose focus and never execute the product vision. The product manager therefore needs to distinguish meaningful evidence from noise.

My Key Takeaway

My strongest learning from this session is that product management requires balancing stability of purpose with flexibility of execution.

The product needs a clear vision, but neither the customer nor the technology landscape will remain static. A product manager therefore has to protect the core product, know when to say no, continually observe the environment, experiment intelligently and make course corrections without chasing every new request or technology.

For me, the question I want to carry forward from this session is:

“Am I changing the product because I have learned something important, or am I simply reacting to the loudest customer or the newest technology?”

That distinction is particularly meaningful to me because in enterprise technology both pressures are very real. My learning from this session is that strong product leadership is not about resisting change or embracing every change. It is about developing the judgment to know what should change, what should remain stable, and why.
