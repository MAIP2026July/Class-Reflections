Session 5 Reflection

Session 5 made me rethink how I approach AI opportunities. Coming from a software development background, my natural tendency is often to start by thinking about what can be automated and how we can build it. One of my strongest takeaways from this session was that an AI Product Manager needs to step back and ask a more fundamental question first: What problem are we trying to solve, and does AI actually create enough value to justify using it?

I could relate this strongly to my professional experience. I currently work with teams responsible for data conversion and software implementation activities for financial institutions. Some of these processes involve preparing and loading financial data from legacy systems into a target banking platform, validating the converted information, and investigating discrepancies before a conversion can be considered complete.

Recently, I have been involved in exploring AI and automation opportunities for some of these activities. Initially, I tended to look at repetitive processes and think, “Can we automate this?” After this session, I realized that this is not necessarily the best starting point. The better questions are: How significant is the problem? How frequently does it occur? How much effort does it consume? What improvement will the user experience? And is AI genuinely the best solution?

This was a meaningful shift in perspective for me. A process may be technically possible to automate, but that does not automatically make it a good AI product opportunity. I now see the importance of evaluating an opportunity through the combined lenses of user value, business value, feasibility, risk and scalability before investing in it.

The discussion around experimentation and POCs also connected with my experience. In my work, we have explored automation through proof-of-concept exercises before considering wider implementation. Previously, I largely viewed a POC as a way of proving that a technical approach works. This session helped broaden that understanding. A good POC should also test the fundamental product hypothesis: does the solution solve a meaningful problem well enough to justify further investment?

Another concept that particularly resonated with me was human-in-the-loop design. In the financial software implementations I work with, data validation is extremely important. Teams may need to compare source and target data, verify financial balances, investigate discrepancies and confirm that the converted information is correct.

This made me think about how I would design AI within such a workflow. I would not want an AI system simply to produce an unexplained recommendation such as “validation successful” or “validation failed.” I would find it much more valuable if the system could identify an anomaly, explain what appears unusual, provide the relevant evidence and direct the specialist to the area requiring attention. The final judgment could then remain with the appropriate person when the decision carries financial or client impact.

This helped me understand that human-in-the-loop does not simply mean adding a human approval step after an AI recommendation. The human needs sufficient context to exercise meaningful judgment. Otherwise, human approval can become a formality rather than an effective safeguard.

I could also relate this to situations I have encountered where an apparently routine data-conversion process suddenly required special handling because certain historical financial records had to be removed and reloaded for selected periods. Situations like this remind me that automating the normal path is only part of the problem. A useful AI product also needs to deal responsibly with exceptions and uncertainty, including recognizing when it does not have enough confidence or context and escalating the situation appropriately.

The session's discussion around trust was therefore particularly relevant to me. In an enterprise environment, users need more than an answer from AI. They need enough information to decide whether they should trust that answer. If an AI system identifies a financial-data mismatch, for example, showing the affected records or explaining the reason for the discrepancy would be much more useful than simply flagging an error.

I also found the discussion around build, buy, partner or use an API valuable. Because of my software engineering background, my instinct can sometimes be to ask how we can build a capability internally. The session made me realize that an AI Product Manager has to approach this differently. The decision should be based on what capability provides differentiation and genuinely needs to be owned, versus what can be obtained more efficiently from an existing model, platform, partner or API.

The prioritization discussion was equally relevant. In my professional environment, there are many areas where AI may potentially help, ranging from data mapping and validation to code generation and exception handling. It is easy to become excited by the number of possibilities. My takeaway is that trying to pursue every AI opportunity can dilute both investment and focus. The Product Manager needs to identify the opportunities with the strongest combination of customer value, business impact, feasibility and responsible implementation.

As a manager, I also found myself reflecting on how we measure the success of AI-enabled processes. Making a process faster is useful, but speed alone does not necessarily mean the process has improved. For the kind of work I manage, I would also want to understand whether AI reduces repetitive effort, minimizes rework, supports accurate validation, handles exceptions appropriately and improves the overall reliability of delivery.

Finally, the discussion around structured prompting reinforced that prompts used within an AI product should not be treated as casual instructions. If a prompt influences an important enterprise workflow, its role, context, task and constraints need to be carefully designed and tested. I now see prompts as part of the product experience and not merely as an interface to an AI model.

My Key Reflection

Overall, Session 5 marked an important shift in how I think about AI product opportunities. Earlier, my thinking naturally started with “What can we automate?” I am now more inclined to start with “What problem is worth solving?”

From there, I would ask: Does AI genuinely improve the outcome? How will we measure that improvement? Where should human judgment remain? How will users understand and trust the AI output? And what evidence do we need before scaling the solution?

For me, that is the biggest learning from this session: AI Product Management is not about finding more places to use AI. It is about making better decisions about where AI genuinely adds value and designing the solution so that people can use it confidently and responsibly.
