Session 6 Reflection: Reimagining Problems and the Art of the Possible 05-Sep-2026

One idea from Session 6 that stayed with me was the“art of the possible.” AI capabilities are evolving quickly, and solutions that were expensive or impractical earlier can become feasible in a relatively short time. The session made me realize that an AI Product Manager needs to continuously understand what technology can enable while also questioning assumptions built into existing processes.

Coming from a software development background, my natural approach is often to receive a requirement, understand it, and determine how it can be implemented. This session encouraged me to pause before moving to the solution and ask a different question:

If I were designing this process today, with the capabilities now available, would I design it the same way?

I could relate this directly to my professional experience managing enterprise software implementation and financial-data conversion activities. These processes contain several established steps, some repetitive and others requiring specialized validation and judgment. My earlier thinking around AI was largely focused on identifying which of these activities could be automated.

Session 6 broadened that perspective. Instead of simply asking which existing steps can be automated, I should also question whether some steps can be simplified, combined, standardized, or eliminated entirely. The class discussion reinforced the value of questioning existing assumptions rather than automatically reproducing the current process with new technology.

Another insight that resonated with me was the difference between a stated requirement and the underlying need. Customers may describe what they want, but that may actually be their proposed solution rather than the real problem. The session emphasized the importance of digging deeper to understand what the customer is genuinely trying to achieve.

I have experienced this in enterprise implementations. A business team may, for example, ask for an additional reconciliation report. However, the underlying problem could actually be poor visibility into discrepancies or insufficient confidence in the existing validation process. Once the real need is understood, the best solution may not necessarily be another report.

This made me reflect on my role as a manager. My responsibility should not be limited to translating business requirements into technical tasks. I should also challenge assumptions, clarify the underlying problem, and help the team explore better alternatives before committing to a solution.

The discussion around what we are actually optimizing for was equally thought-provoking. In software delivery, it is easy to focus on speed, automation, or the number of activities completed. But these measures may not represent the true business outcome. For example, automating a data-conversion step may reduce processing time, but if it creates additional exceptions and rework, the overall process may not have improved.

This reinforced an important lesson for me: the metric we choose can influence the solution we design. When evaluating an AI opportunity, I should therefore consider not only efficiency but also reliability, quality, maintainability, rework, and the overall customer outcome.

My Key Reflection

The biggest shift for me from Session 6 is learning to create a deliberate pause between requirement and solution.

Instead of immediately asking “How can we implement or automate this?”, I should first ask:

What is the real problem?
What assumptions are we carrying from the existing process?
What has become possible with current AI capabilities?
Can we simplify or eliminate steps instead of automating them?
What outcome are we actually trying to optimize?

For me, the “art of the possible” is ultimately about developing the habit of challenging existing ways of working. My strongest takeaway is:

Before using AI to improve an existing process, I should first question whether that process needs to exist in its current form. The greater opportunity may be to reimagine the solution rather than simply automate it.
