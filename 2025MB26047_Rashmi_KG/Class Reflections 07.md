Session 7 Reflection: Launch Is Not the Finish Line 12-Sep-2026

One statement from Session 7 that stayed with me was“launch is not the finish line.” The session emphasized that substantial work continues after an AI product goes live because its reliability, user experience, dependencies, and customer expectations still need to be managed.

Coming from a software development and delivery background, I found this particularly relevant. In enterprise implementations, a successful release or go-live is often treated as a major milestone. My focus naturally tends to be on ensuring that development, testing, validation, and deployment are completed successfully. This session made me reflect that, from a product-management perspective, deployment proves that we can release the product, but not necessarily that the product is creating sustained value.

The discussion on trust made this even clearer. An AI product can technically function as designed and still lose user trust if its performance is inconsistent or if expectations are poorly communicated. The class example of telling a customer that a service will recover in 30 minutes when it actually takes several hours illustrated that trust is influenced not only by technical performance, but also by the accuracy of what the product communicates.

I could connect this with my own experience managing software implementation and data-conversion activities for financial institutions. Even after a system change or conversion has been completed, we continue to validate outcomes, investigate exceptions, and address issues that become visible through actual usage. This made me realize that an AI product would require even stronger post-launch attention because its behaviour may depend on data, models, external services, and changing user patterns.

Another valuable takeaway was the importance of problem framing before data collection. The session highlighted that we should first be clear about what we are trying to solve and then determine what data is actually required. It also connected data collection with privacy considerations, including India's DPDP framework.

This challenged a common assumption that more data automatically leads to a better AI product. My takeaway is that data collection should be purpose-driven. If I cannot clearly explain why a particular piece of data is necessary for the product outcome, I should question whether it needs to be collected at all.

From my managerial perspective, the session also reinforced that launch should represent a transition from building to learning, rather than completion. After deployment, I would want to monitor whether users are actually receiving the intended value, where the product is failing, whether new risks are appearing, and whether the assumptions made during development still hold.

My Key Reflection

Session 7 shifted my thinking about product success. Earlier, I tended to associate a successful launch with successful delivery. I now see launch as the point where real-world evidence begins to test our assumptions.

For an AI product, I believe the Product Manager's responsibility continues well beyond deployment. The product must continuously earn user trust through reliable performance, honest communication, responsible use of data, and timely responses when actual behaviour differs from expectations.

My biggest takeaway is that a successful launch gets an AI product into the hands of users, but sustained trust and value are what make it a successful product.
