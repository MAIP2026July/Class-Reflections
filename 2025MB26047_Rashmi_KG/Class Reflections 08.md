Session 8 Reflection: Experimentation, Feedback and Choosing the Right Metrics 03-Oct-2026

One of my strongest takeaways from this session was that AI product development is fundamentally experimental. When we are trying to solve a problem using AI, we may have a strong idea, but we do not know in advance whether it will work well enough in the actual business context. What we start with is essentially a hypothesis that needs to be tested and refined.

I could relate this quite closely to my professional experience. I have recently been involved in exploring AI and automation opportunities within financial data-conversion processes. My technical instinct is usually to think about whether something can be automated and how we can implement it. This session reminded me that, from a product perspective, technical feasibility is only the beginning. What matters is whether the solution improves something meaningful, such as speed, cost, accuracy, user effort, or overall experience. This is similar to the professor's point that innovation needs to solve a new problem or solve an existing problem better.

This also changed the way I think about a Proof of Concept. Earlier, I mainly looked at a POC as a way to prove that the technology works. I now see it more as an experiment designed to answer specific questions. Does the AI solve the problem reliably? Does it actually reduce effort? What happens with exceptions? Is the business improvement significant enough to justify further investment? A technically successful POC does not automatically mean we have a successful product.

Another discussion that made me think was around feedback loops. Feedback is necessary for improving an AI product, but blindly optimizing around every piece of feedback can take the product in the wrong direction. The session highlighted that Product Managers need to decide which feedback loops should influence the product and which ones should not.

I see a parallel in my role as a manager. Different stakeholders can have different expectations from the same solution. A developer may value technical simplicity, an operations team may value reliability, and a business stakeholder may prioritize faster delivery. Trying to optimize equally for every expectation can make the solution unnecessarily complex. The Product Manager therefore has to make conscious trade-offs rather than trying to satisfy every request.

The discussion on metrics was probably the most useful part of the session for me. The professor emphasized that metric design is ultimately the Product Manager's accountability and that dashboards and metrics can influence both team and product behaviour. The recommendation was also to keep metrics limited and meaningful instead of creating a large set of KPIs without clarity on what is actually being optimized.

This made me reconsider how I typically look at delivery success. In software projects, it is easy to focus on whether something was completed on time, how many activities were automated, or how quickly processing was completed. But none of those measures alone tells me whether the product is actually creating value.

For example, if an AI-enabled solution completes a process much faster but creates more exceptions that need manual investigation, speed alone would give me a misleading picture of success. I would rather balance a small number of measures such as efficiency, accuracy, exception rate, and actual business outcome.

This is something I can apply directly in my work. When evaluating future AI initiatives, I want to define the business outcome first and the metrics second, rather than adding metrics after the solution has already been developed.

My Key Reflection

Session 8 made me realize that managing an AI product is not about knowing with certainty that an idea will work. It is about forming a sensible hypothesis, testing it, learning from evidence, and being willing to change direction when the evidence challenges the original assumption.

The session also reinforced something particularly relevant to my managerial role: what we measure influences what people optimize for. Therefore, choosing the right metrics is not just a reporting exercise. It is an important product decision.

My biggest takeaway is:

For an AI product, I should not ask only, “Does it work?” I should ask, “Does it create the value we expected, what evidence proves that, and are we measuring the outcomes that actually matter?”
