When building a product, the most important question is not just how to use flashy new technology, but whether it actually solves a real problem for the user. Before jumping into building anything, creators need to make sure their idea makes sense, is safe for society, and doesn't harm vulnerable groups. Just because you can build a high-tech solution doesn't mean you should; simplicity is often better than overcomplicating things.   A good product manager acts like the captain of a ship, taking full responsibility for the product's success even when things go wrong outside of their direct control. They have to look out for hidden risks—especially rare, high-impact disasters—and make sure the product gives helpful advice quickly enough for people to actually use it. Instead of getting distracted by buzzwords like deep learning or AI, the focus should always be on what the user actually needs to get their job done.   People naturally resist change because learning a new tool or changing habits takes effort.

To get past this, products should first target early adopters who are excited to try new things, and make it easy for users to try the product without feeling trapped. Trickery, like making it hard to cancel a subscription or delete your account, should be avoided entirely. Furthermore, automating tasks that people actually enjoy doing can backfire, whereas automating boring, repetitive chores is usually welcomed.   When relying on artificial intelligence or buying tools from outside vendors, you cannot just blindly trust them. You need to make sure the tools are reliable, transparent, and legally compliant, especially in industries where decisions must be explained and audited. Because technology can be unpredictable and models can change behind the scenes, keeping a close eye on performance and maintaining human oversight is essential. Ultimately, great product management is about balancing technology, business needs, and human behavior to create something genuinely useful.
