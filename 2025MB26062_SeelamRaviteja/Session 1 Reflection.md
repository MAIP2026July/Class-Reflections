# Session 1 Reflection

25 July 2026 | Seelam Raviteja | 2025MB26062

I walked in expecting the course to open with models and features. It opened with a harder management question: who is answerable when a product fails a customer.

The usual description of a product manager, a coordinator between business and engineering, explains the meetings. It does not explain the job. Coordination can be delegated. The outcome cannot. A cardholder whose payment is declined does not sort the failure into "authorization team" and "settlement team." The brand took the money, or failed to move it, and that is the only fact the customer has. Ownership exists because the customer experiences one product, even when the company is organized as many functions.

That is also why the role feels uncomfortable. The person who owns the outcome often does not hire the engineers, set the budget, or choose the vendor. Treating that gap as a reason to step back would leave the customer with nobody. The practical skill is influence without line authority: making the decision explicit, showing the trade-off, and staying with the result after the meeting ends. If the organization never gives the role any room to decide, the title is decorative. The class did not pretend otherwise. It still put the burden on the person who accepted the product.

The trade-off discussion was the part I want to keep. There is rarely a clean best option. Time, cost, talent, and the ability to maintain the choice later all sit in the same decision. A technically superior database is a poor product decision if the company cannot staff it two years from now. I had been treating "better technology" as almost the same thing as "better for the business." Those are different columns. A manager who optimizes only the column that is easy to compare will look rigorous and still choose badly.

The three ways AI enters the work helped me separate the conversation. One is using AI to build the product faster. One is using AI inside the product manager's own work, such as synthesis and drafting. One is putting AI into what the customer actually buys. Mixing them up is how teams announce an "AI product" when they have only used a tool to write the specification. The customer does not pay for the second of those. They pay when the third changes an outcome they care about, and they still hold a person accountable when it is wrong.

What I will do differently is start a product discussion with the decision I am willing to own, not with the feature list. If I cannot name who is harmed when the decision is wrong, and who benefits when it is right, I do not yet have a product problem. I have a pile of activity.
