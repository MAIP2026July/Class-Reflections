# Session 11 Reflection

24 October 2026 | Seelam Raviteja | 2025MB26062

Responsible AI is easy to treat as a review at the end, a set of approvals collected after the product works. This session made the opposite case. Whether the product should behave a certain way is part of the design, and the cost of getting it wrong lands on a real person before it lands on the company.

Bias was explained through a child who sees only one kind of security guard and concludes that every guard looks like that. Nobody taught the child a rule. The conclusion came from limited exposure. A model is the same. It learns the world it was shown, including the habits of the old process. That is why "we did not write an unfair rule" is not a defence. In hiring, credit, or identity checks, historical data can carry a disadvantage forward with nobody intending it. Bias can also enter through labels and through business policy layered on top of the model. Perfect fairness may be hard to define. That is not a reason to skip measuring the disparities we can see, split by the groups that matter, rather than hidden inside one overall accuracy number.

Data minimisation changed how I think about the data wish list. Teams collect fields because they might be useful later. Each extra field is more exposure, more consent to manage, and more to explain to a regulator. If age or gender does not change the outcome the product is meant to deliver, collecting it adds risk and no value. Blind review in academic work was the example: removing the attribute removes a whole category of bias. Purpose, consent, who can see the data, and how long it is kept should be written into the product, not left to a compliance check later. Easy access is still exposure, and the story about a candidate's details being misused made that concrete.

Hallucination is a product problem because a wrong answer can sound exactly as confident as a right one. Grounding the answer in retrieved sources reduces the risk. It does not remove it. The design choice I am taking away is that "I don't know" is a valid output. Abstaining, asking for more input, or escalating to a person is often the correct product behavior, not a failure. If the product has no path for uncertainty, it will fill the gap with something fluent. The example of map traffic being faked by someone walking a trolley of phones also stuck with me. The system can be fed misleading signals on purpose, and it will treat them as truth unless someone checks.

Human oversight only helps if it is real. Earlier sessions showed how a reviewer becomes a rubber stamp. This session added the periodic check. Even where no person reviews each decision, someone should sample the output regularly, including the cases the system rejected, to see what quality looks like. Governance has the same trap. A committee of domain experts can recommend. The owner still decides and still answers for the outcome. If the review work is unpaid and added on top of a day job, it drifts into box-ticking, so the effort has to be recognised. Governance that only adds approvals becomes bureaucracy and slows the team without reducing harm.

The launch question was the most practical part. No product reaches zero risk, so "perfect or don't ship" is not a real option. Probability, severity, how quickly we would detect it, and whether the harm can be reversed give a better basis. The answer might be a limited rollout, extra monitoring, human review on one path, a disabled feature, or a delay. A vendor model does not move the responsibility either. The customer meets the model through our product and blames us when it fails.

How we communicate an incident is part of the same discipline. A vague notice that some customers were affected creates panic and calls. A precise one, telling people exactly how to know whether they are affected, contains the damage.

The test I will apply before launch is short. Who could be disadvantaged, what data are we holding and why, what does the system do when it does not know, can a person challenge the outcome, and who owns it when it fails. If I cannot answer those, the product is not ready, however good the accuracy looks.
