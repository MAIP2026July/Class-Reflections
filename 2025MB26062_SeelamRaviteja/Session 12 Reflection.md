# Session 12 Reflection

31 October 2026 | Seelam Raviteja | 2025MB26062

A product that solves a real problem and gets used is not yet a business. This session was about the arithmetic that decides whether it becomes one, and why that arithmetic has to shape the design rather than be calculated after the build.

Target costing is the idea I will use most. The usual habit is to add up what it cost to build and put a margin on top. That price has nothing to do with what the customer will pay, so the product either loses the deal or leaves money on the table. Working backwards is more honest. Start from the value the customer gets, estimate what share of it they will realistically pay, take out the margin the business needs, and what remains is the cost envelope. The team then has to build inside that envelope. That is why model choice, caching, and how much context each request carries become business decisions. If the cheapest acceptable design still costs more than the envelope, the product does not work at that price, and it is better to learn that before the build than after launch.

The gap between how we pay and how the customer thinks about value is the next trap. We pay per token, per call, per hour of compute. The customer does not care about any of that. They care about a qualified lead, a resolved ticket, a decision made sooner. Pricing per outcome lines up with value, but it also moves risk onto us. If the product produces a strong lead and the customer's sales team never follows up, the sale is lost for reasons outside our control. Outcome pricing only works where we can define the outcome, measure it, and actually influence it. Otherwise it turns into an argument about attribution. Flat pricing has its own risk: a few heavy users with uncapped usage can make a whole cohort lose money.

Unit economics matter more than the customer count. With AI, each extra interaction carries a variable cost, so growth can make the business worse. Contribution margin by segment, tier, or workflow shows which customers are actually profitable. One point I did not expect: downselling can be the right move. A customer who gets enough value from a smaller plan is worth keeping. Pushing them into a bigger tier they do not need is how they leave altogether.

The requirements discussion connected back to measurement. "Accurate," "fast," and "easy to use" read like requirements and decide nothing. How accurate, how fast, and how much reduction in processing time are the questions the document has to answer. If success is not measurable before the build, nobody can say afterwards whether it succeeded, and every result will be called a win.

Swimlane diagrams and the C4 model were taught for the same reason. A swimlane makes ownership and handoffs visible: which step is a rule, which is a probabilistic call, where a person reviews, where it escalates. That is where you see whether AI belongs in a step or whether the step should be removed. The C4 view is about knowing the architecture well enough to understand the trade-offs. Latency, cost, vendor dependency, and privacy all come out of those boxes and arrows. I do not need to design them, but I cannot price or defend the product if I do not understand them.

The roadmap point was simple and useful. A roadmap is not a project plan. Version one needs detail. Later versions should show how the value grows, not a list of tasks. Building for extension early, with configurable boundaries rather than hard-coded choices, is what stops a product from turning back into a string of custom projects.

Platform thinking was the last layer. Some products get harder to replace as more participants join, even when a competitor has better features. That raises a strategic question for AI: is AI the product, a premium layer, or a capability embedded in a workflow people already use? Often the embedded version gets adopted faster and is harder to dislodge. I would still earn the right to be a platform by proving the core workflow first.

What I will carry forward is that both sides of the equation have to work. Customer value sets what we can charge. Architecture, model choice, and usage set what it costs to deliver. If I can explain both in a few minutes to someone deciding whether to invest, I understand the product. If I can only walk through features, I do not yet.
