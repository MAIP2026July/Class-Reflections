# Session 3 Reflection

18 August 2026 | Seelam Raviteja | 2025MB26062

A framework is useful when it changes a decision or the evidence behind a decision. If a second framework only restates the first in new boxes, it is ceremony. I am going to be stricter about that, because a full toolkit looks like rigor and can still leave the team no closer to a choice.

The list we walked through is large: jobs to be done, the double diamond, opportunity solution trees, lean canvas, working backwards, five forces, PESTLE, SWOT, Ansoff, QFD, RICE, Kano, MoSCoW, RACI, one-way and two-way doors, OODA. I do not need all of them on one problem. I need the one that exposes the assumption I am most likely to skip. For a new offer, that is usually the job the customer is hiring the product to do, and the progress they will notice if it works. Feature counts are a poor substitute. A product can gain screens and leave the customer's Tuesday unchanged.

Naming the person matters for the same reason. "User" hides a conflict. On a ride platform the rider wants a low fare and a short wait. The driver wants a high fare and a short deadhead. A feature that delights one can raise the other's cost of staying. If I write requirements for a blended "user," I will ship a compromise that neither person would pay for. The same split shows up in any two-sided or multi-stakeholder service: the buyer, the daily operator, and the person who feels the failure are often not the same.

Scrum landed differently once I heard it as a commercial bargain rather than a ritual. For the length of a sprint the team is protected from a moving brief, and in return the business gets a finished increment it can inspect. Killing a sprint is allowed. Rewriting it halfway is not, because the protection was the point. A volatile market argues for a shorter sprint. A stable one can afford a longer stretch of uninterrupted work. The mistake is copying a two-week habit from another company and calling it agile.

The pricing note at the end is the one I will reuse in a business case. The builder of a product has historically kept something like 15 to 25 percent of the value created, with about 20 percent as a working assumption when there is no better evidence. The customer keeps the rest, which is why they switch. A model that assumes the vendor captures the full saving will lose the deal or, worse, win a deal the buyer later resents. I would rather price against a share of a measured outcome than against the cost of my team plus a hopeful margin.

One caution I am keeping: calling a 5 percent improvement a transformation may help a budget conversation, but I should not believe my own label. Honest sizing changes how much risk, change management, and time the plan deserves.
