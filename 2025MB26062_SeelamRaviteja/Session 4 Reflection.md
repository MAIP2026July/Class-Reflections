# Session 4 Reflection

22 August 2026 | Seelam Raviteja | 2025MB26062

The useful question is not whether AI can do a task. It is whether this problem should be given to AI, and what happens to a real person when the answer is wrong.

I now treat that as a sequence of gates, not a brainstorm. The first gate is social legitimacy. Do we have consent. Are we depending on surveillance or on work the user did not agree to. If the answer is no, a clever model does not repair it. Only after that do value, feasibility, and the chance to learn from the data become relevant. Skipping the first gate is how products get built that a regulator, or the customer, later shuts down.

The appropriateness ladder is the second filter, and I like it because it is biased toward the cheap solution. Deterministic rules, then retrieval and analytics, then predictive models, then generative or agentic behavior. A rule that already sorts the cases should not be replaced by a model so that a slide can say AI. The cost is not only inference. It is the new failure mode: an answer that varies, is hard to explain, and still has to be supported. If a calculator needs a language model, the product is showing off.

Timing is part of the value, which I underweighted before. A correct rain forecast that arrives after the harvest window has closed does not save the crop. An accurate late alert in logistics is the same shape. The customer needed enough time to reroute a truck, call the receiver, or hold labor. Accuracy without an actionable window is a report, not a product. I would now write the window into the requirement: detect, inform, and leave enough time for the human or the system to act, including the time a person needs to understand the alert.

Averages hide the case that can end the business. A fraud model that is right almost always can still approve one transfer that wipes out a small merchant's trust, or block one payment that costs a hospital a supplier. Tail risk belongs in the business case next to the average, with a named owner for the bad case. Human review is not automatically that owner. If the model speaks first and the person only clicks approve, the review becomes a rubber stamp, and the company has added cost without adding judgement. A better design lets the person and the model decide from the evidence, and spends human attention on the disagreements.

Adoption is a separate product problem. People decline a better tool when the service network, the retraining, and the cost of being wrong sit on them. I would not call that resistance. I would call it a rational total cost, and I would design the switch, not just the feature.
