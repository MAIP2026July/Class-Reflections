# Session 5 Reflection

29 August 2026 | Seelam Raviteja | 2025MB26062

An AI opportunity is not a process with a model attached. It is a recurring decision where being wrong is expensive, the pain shows up often enough to matter, and the data to learn from actually exists. If any of those is weak, the honest move is a simpler tool or no tool.

The frequency test from class is the one I would apply first to a backlog. A screen to edit an organization hierarchy that changes once every few years does not deserve a product. A back-end change, done rarely, is enough. The instinct to treat "we could automate it" as a reason is strong. Materiality is the correction. Time saved twice a year does not pay for design, access control, testing, and the support calls when the screen is wrong.

Where the test passes, I still want evidence before a build commitment. A demo that impresses a room is not a before-and-after. If the claim is fewer handoffs, shorter handling time, or fewer repeated contacts, the comparison has to be designed while the old process is still measurable. Novelty wears off. Repeated use is the signal that the workflow actually got easier.

Build, buy, or subscribe is a strategy choice wearing a technical costume. An API gets a team to a prototype quickly and also puts price, uptime, and model behavior in someone else's roadmap. An open-weight model run inside the company is not free. The license may be, the operations are not. I would own the part that is the reason a customer stays: the workflow, the domain rules, the evidence the user sees, and the data the company is allowed to keep. I would rent the general capability until owning it changes the margin or the risk. Reviewing that choice again eighteen months later matters, because a vendor's price can rise exactly when usage, the thing we wanted, grows.

The design half of the session was about uncertainty as a user experience, not as a footnote. A confident sentence is a product decision. When confidence is low, the honest options are to ask a clarifying question, show the conflicting evidence, or say the system does not know. A legal disclaimer under a certain-sounding answer does not do that job. Customers either over-trust it or, after one bad outcome, stop trusting the rest of the product.

I also took the prompt structure seriously: role, context, task, and constraints. Inside a product that prompt is not a chat habit. It is part of the promise, and it needs a version, a test, and an owner, the same way a pricing rule would.
