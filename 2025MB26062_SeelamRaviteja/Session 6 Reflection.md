# Session 6 Reflection

5 September 2026 | Seelam Raviteja | 2025MB26062

Speeding up a weak process is an easy way to spend an AI budget and leave the customer's job intact. The more useful move is to ask which inputs the step still needs, which outputs still matter, and which rules exist only because an older constraint has expired.

The print, sign, scan, and email loop is a clear case. Each party is locally rational. Together they recreate a paper office inside email. Text that can be signed and stored without the scan does not need a smarter scanner. It needs someone willing to drop the step. The same pattern sits in expense approvals, vendor onboarding, and status reporting. I would rather delete a handoff than put a model in the middle of it. The risk of reimagining is real: a redesigned flow can be so unfamiliar that people route around it. The balance is to keep the outcome recognizable and remove the work the customer never valued.

Knowing what is newly possible belongs in that judgement. An audiobook was a costly production. Speech synthesis made a different price possible, and a different customer, the person who will listen and will not sit with the print edition. A product manager who does not update that map will keep designing for a constraint the market has already left.

How we write the requirement is part of the same discipline. "The system shall support 10,000 concurrent users" is not something a rider, a patient, or a branch manager asked for. It belongs in a specification. The requirement should stay in the stakeholder's language, and the team should keep one name for each person. Mixed vocabulary in the document becomes mixed behavior in support, in the manual, and in the metric.

Listening has a similar failure mode. An average sentiment score can be mildly positive while one feature is driving people away. An opinion has pieces: what was discussed, which aspect, how the person felt, who said it, and when. A phone camera that is excellent in daylight and poor at night is two facts. Averaging them produces a third fact that no buyer experiences. I also do not want to throw away the line that is not about a feature at all, such as a family argument about an expensive purchase. That is evidence about the weight of the decision, and it should change onboarding and reassurance, not just the spec sheet.

One risk I will not file under "bugs" is harm that appears when the system works as designed. Historical data can carry a bias forward, as in the public Apple Card controversy, without anyone writing an unfair rule. Consent sits next to that. Under a law like India's DPDP Act the company is a custodian of personal data. A model that is useful and unlawful is not a product. It is a liability with a demo.
