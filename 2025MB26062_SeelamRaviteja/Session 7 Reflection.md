# Session 7 Reflection

12 September 2026 | Seelam Raviteja | 2025MB26062

The electricity distribution case only became a product problem when I stopped answering as the utility and started answering as the company that has to sell something.

A distribution company buys power ahead of time. If a neighborhood goes dark, some of that power cannot be sold, and the complaint calls arrive on top of the lost margin. A chatbot that answers "we are aware of the outage" a little faster is a service improvement. A product that notices an abnormal drop in consumption, confirms the area, and tells people before they call is a different offer. It protects revenue the buyer has already committed, and it removes work the customer never wanted to do. That is why the proactive message exists. The customer benefit and the buyer's economics point the same way, which is rare enough that I would look for it on purpose.

Uncertainty still has to be designed. If the system is not sure whether the outage is on this street or the next one, a confident restoration time creates a second failure. I would rather show a confirmed fact, mark an inference as an inference, and stay quiet when the basis is too thin. The layered way we evaluated answers in class, separating what is known from what is inferred and what is recommended, is a practical control. Recommendations are where a fluent model can outrun its evidence. Showing the chain underneath lets a person catch a bad foundation before it becomes a customer promise.

Pricing follows the same incentive logic. A fee per seat falls as the product succeeds and the buyer needs fewer people, so the vendor is paid to stay necessary. A fee per resolved incident transfers volume risk: quiet weeks pay little, storm weeks may overwhelm the team. Neither is wrong. Each should be chosen with the behavior it will reward. The earlier point on value share still applies. The buyer will not hand over the entire saving. A price near a fifth of a measured benefit is a starting hypothesis, not a formula, and it has to be checked against who inside the utility actually feels the pain and signs the contract.

I am also less casual about life after launch. A conventional release often gets calmer once it is generally available. An AI product that depends on an outside model can change behavior without a release from us, the way a payments product stops when an OTP provider stops. That dependency is a product risk. I would want a fallback, a way to notice drift, and a contract that does not let the core promise sit on a single vendor's mood.

The personal standard I am taking from the case is blunt. If I cannot demonstrate the product, I do not understand it well enough to price it or to defend it when it is wrong.
