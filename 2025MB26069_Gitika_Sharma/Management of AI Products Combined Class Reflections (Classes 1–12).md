## Class 1

This session set out what product management means and how AI changes it. The central idea was that a PM should be seen as the owner of a product's outcomes, not merely a link between business and engineering teams. The discussion then covered how this ownership works in practice, with limited authority, constant trade-offs and a real emotional load. It also introduced a three-part lens for the rest of the course: using AI to build products, using AI to do PM work, and embedding AI inside the product.

### Core Concepts

- **Ownership over coordination:** The PM is accountable for the problem and the result, whether or not they control the people or functions involved. Blame cannot be passed on to engineering, marketing or anyone else.
- **Decision-making under assumptions:** The role is largely about trade-offs across time, cost and talent, and each one rests on assumptions about the future. The best technical option is not always the best product choice.
- **Full lifecycle responsibility:** This covers launch, adoption, evolution, sunsetting old versions and retiring the product when it no longer makes sense.
- **Three pillars of AI in product work:** AI to build, AI to manage, and AI embedded in the product. Each changes the PM's responsibilities differently, and the third is where AI product management becomes a specialization of its own.

### Reflections and Insights

- **How you describe the role shapes how you act in it.** Describing the PM as a bridge or coordinator encourages waiting for decisions. Describing the PM as an owner encourages finding a way, including going external when internal teams fall short.
- **Accountability without authority is the defining tension.** No framework removes it. What works is influence, evidence, transparency and credibility, along with recognizing that organizations that freeze on decisions or tie a PM's hands hurt their own results.
- **"Done" is harder to define for AI products.** Traditional products have frameworks for deciding when something is sufficiently complete. AI outputs are probabilistic, so quality is a spectrum and no established framework exists yet. This is an open problem, not a gap in the learner.
- **Durability is a new question.** AI products depend on models and vendors that change every few months, so owning an outcome means designing for replaceability from the start.
- **Resilience is part of the skill set.** Saying no, absorbing ambiguity and delivering bad news all come with the job. Whether the work feels like a thankless grind or visible progress is partly a mindset choice, and it affects how long someone can do it well.
- **Technical fluency is a precondition.** A PM who cannot judge what is being built cannot make credible trade-offs, and with AI, the limits and costs of the model directly constrain what can be promised to customers.

## Class 2

This session looked at how a product manager keeps a product on track when customers, competitors and technology are all changing at once. It stressed accountability as stewardship of other people's resources, and showed how AI is eroding traditional moats such as domain knowledge and build capability. A broad toolkit of product frameworks was surveyed, always with the reminder that they support judgment and never replace it.

### Core Concepts

- **Frameworks as decision aids:** Stacking frameworks that ask the same questions adds ceremony without changing decisions. Pick what fits the situation and keep the final call with the product manager.
- **Moving target and course correction:** The product, the customer and the competition keep shifting, so confidence is valid only as of a date, and re-planning is a core part of the job.
- **Product vs. project thinking:** A product serves many unnamed customers through layering: a generic core, then industry variants, then customer-specific customization kept separate. Jobs-to-be-done puts the progress a customer seeks ahead of features and personas.
- **Managing risk and reversibility:** Sprints work as a protected window of stability whose length depends on how dynamic the environment is. Two-way decisions can be delegated and made quickly, while one-way decisions need more scrutiny. Pricing should be anchored in customer value, with the provider taking only a modest share.

### Reflections and Insights

- **Incentives shape honesty.** If every improvement raises the next target, rational teams hold gains back. Leaders get real effort only when they don't penalize it.
- **"Transformation" should mean a real leap in value.** Relabelling incremental gains may help win funding, but it should not mislead the team about where the product stands.
- **AI shifts the competitive moat.** When customers can cheaply build their own version, advantage has to come from elsewhere, such as trust, data, speed or continuity. This calls for forward-looking competitive analysis.
- **Precise language is a product skill.** Terms like "user" and "customer" hide very different needs. Naming the segment and its context leads to better, more inclusive design, including for older users, who are often left behind.
- **Technology is a means, not a belief.** Architectural choices are judged by fit and economics. Teams need protected time to explore, but experiments should tie to goals, with clear conditions for adopting or rejecting an idea.
- **Compliance is non-negotiable.** Pressure to cut regulatory corners is a test of judgment, and a product that gets banned creates no value.
- **Leadership means shielding the team.** Products outlast projects, so trust with the team compounds across versions. Rigid policies and hours-tracking cultures work against building products.
- **Cheap evidence trails protect decisions.** A lightweight record of why you acted, or chose not to act, keeps the product manager honest as a custodian of others' resources.
- **Shutdowns can be done gracefully.** Ending a product that no longer makes sense, without burning goodwill, preserves the reputation needed for the next one.

## Class 3

This session continued the course on managing AI products. It walked through the full product life cycle, from discovering a problem through launch, general availability and growth, to eventually renewing or retiring the product. It covered the documents and evidence a product manager should keep, the common decision frameworks, and how AI changes each stage. It also covered the professional services and consulting roles around a product, and the skills an aspiring AI product manager needs.

### Core Concepts

- **Product life cycle as a learning loop:** Discover, define, strategize, plan, build, launch, grow, then renew or retire. It is iterative, not a waterfall. "Good enough" beats perfect, and general availability is the real launch milestone.
- **Decisions and evidence:** The product manager is a trustee of other people's money and resources, so decisions need a record. That means artifacts, traceability across design, engineering and testing, one-way vs. two-way doors, decision owners (RACI/RAPID) and an expiry date for each decision.
- **Frameworks are aids, not the management itself:** Jobs-to-be-done, double diamond, QFD, Porter's Five Forces, prioritization models and roadmaps. Pick the minimum framework that helps with a specific decision. Roadmaps should be detailed in the near term and directional further out.
- **What changes with AI:** Teams must distinguish between adding AI to an existing product and building an AI-native one. AI-native PRDs add a product thesis, data contracts, a feedback flywheel and regulatory constraints. Evaluation covers the whole product (cost, P95/P99 latency, failure modes), not just the model. Regulation is tightening, and consultants and professional services never transfer the PM's accountability.

### Reflections and Insights

- **Defer decisions strategically, not blindly.** Like building a house, lock in the structural and costly-to-change choices early (stakeholder alignment, goals) and leave the "paint colour" for later. Deferring a misalignment is not strategy.
- **"Good enough" is itself a strategic decision.** Launching too early invites criticism, but waiting for perfect means never reaching general availability. A beta is only about halfway, so pace the team's energy for the second half.
- **The cost of failure has shrunk.** AI-assisted rapid prototypes let one person test ideas with users before building anything. A vibe-coded prototype is still not production quality, though.
- **AI evidence expires faster.** A consultant's advice or a model evaluation can be outdated before it is used, so the shelf life of a decision is the shorter of the two clocks, not the longer.
- **Accountability can't be outsourced.** Consultants, specialists and services partners can advise and implement, but the product promise, and the responsibility when something goes wrong, stays with the product manager. A tilted apartment tower makes the point: the builder owns the outcome even though specialists were involved.
- **Fluency over expertise.** An AI PM need not be a data scientist. They must understand the terms, the regulations and the trade-offs well enough to translate between experts, and know where to look things up.

## Class 4

This session explored when and where AI actually creates product value, using a staged "gate" approach: first social legitimacy, then incremental value, then viability, then bounded learning. Illustrative products, such as an agri-advisory tool, a recommendation engine and a claims copilot, were used to test hypotheses, downside risks and who bears the cost of errors. The discussion also covered why users resist adopting new products and how to balance AI judgment against expert judgment. The session closed with how much autonomy AI systems should be given.

### Core Concepts

- **Legitimacy before technology:** Check consent, vulnerable groups and ethical lines before testing technical fit. Something you *can* build is not necessarily something you *should* build.
- **Right-size the AI:** Start with deterministic rules, then analytics and search, then predictive ML, and only then generative or agentic AI. Use the simplest approach that does the job, and measure value against pre-AI and post-AI baselines.
- **Actionability and tail risk:** Insight has value only within a window in which the user can act. Averages hide rare but severe harms, so guardrails should be built around the tails.
- **Adoption, switching cost and accountability:** Users adopt in waves (early adopters first), and adoption is held back by workflow disruption and lock-in. Autonomy should be graduated: suggest, draft, execute with approval, then execute within limits. The product owner stays accountable throughout.

### Reflections and Insights

- **"AI" is a means, not the value claim.** If a simpler automation delivers the outcome, say so. Defending AI for its own sake is dogma, and the same honesty applies to any technology choice, including blockchain.
- **Value is defined by the user, not the builder.** Time saved is not always value. People sometimes prize the very friction you remove, such as human interaction, and positioning that ignores psychology can sink a good product.
- **Subjectivity can be reduced but never eliminated.** Shared rubrics, a merge formula and cross-checks between humans and AI improve consistency, but people and models will still interpret the same criteria differently. The aim should be practical, defensible decisions rather than perfect objectivity.
- **Incentives shape behavior, whether human or AI.** Narrow KPIs (for example, "maximize profit") push agents toward harmful shortcuts such as rejecting every claim. Metrics should be designed with side effects in mind, because management sets the behavior it later blames.
- **Silent failures are more dangerous than loud ones.** Model quality can degrade without warning, so monitoring, evidence trails and reversible ("two-way door") decisions matter more than raw capability.
- **In regulated contexts, explainability is a requirement.** A tool that works but cannot justify its decisions fails the long-range workflow of an auditable industry, however good its demo looks.

## Class 5

This session covered how product managers spot and evaluate AI opportunities, starting from whether AI adds real customer value. It then moved to sourcing decisions (build, buy or partner), product-market fit, and designing AI experiences that handle uncertainty. A large part of the class was spent on human-in-the-loop design and its pitfalls. Along the way, the session also covered the midterm format, the AI product stack, prompt design, and the difference between portfolio and product management.

### Core Concepts

- **Value and feasibility together:** AI should be added only where it improves a customer's workflow. That means a frequent, painful, data-rich job where friction, trust or decision stress can be reduced. Every claim needs evidence from experiments, and the opportunity must also be feasible in cost, quality and autonomy, and be priced so that customers will actually pay.
- **Sourcing and platform strategy:** Choose between build, buy (API or open-weight models) and partner, weighing vendor dependency, speed, control and governance. Own the capability that gives a competitive edge rather than the model itself. Platform and ecosystem bets are hard to reverse.
- **Layered AI stack and UX for uncertainty:** An AI product has a data layer, a model layer, an API layer (to avoid lock-in) and a user experience layer. The UX layer must communicate confidence, fallbacks and errors honestly, and prompts need structure, iteration and version control.
- **Human-in-the-loop design:** Decide where humans review, how cases are escalated and what context they are given. Weigh the cost of a right or wrong decision. Guard against reviewer fatigue and rubber-stamping with real accountability and sensible performance measures.

### Reflections and Insights

- **The customer's goal is to finish and leave.** Even products built to hold attention serve the customer's own wish to be entertained or to get something done. AI is judged by how quickly it lets people finish their job, not by how long they stay.
- **Proof matters more than belief.** "I know it's valuable" is not enough. Running experiments and keeping evidence is a core PM skill, and it also cuts through slow, opinion-driven debate. A quick prototype can settle in days what a discussion cannot settle in weeks.
- **Human oversight can be only for show.** If reviewers keep saying yes because the AI sounds confident, or because they are measured on volume, the human in the loop adds nothing. The failure belongs to the system and its incentives, not to the individual. A product manager therefore also has to manage the organization around the product.
- **Strategy is only useful once it becomes decisions.** Opportunities should be time-boxed, sequenced as a series of bets and written up as a memo that leaders can act on. A PM should be able to demo the product without help, which shows real ownership.
- **Accountability cannot be delegated.** Experts can supply the numbers on tokens, cost and pricing, but the PM answers for the unit economics and for everything done in the product's name. The same logic applies to a portfolio manager, who sets a stable planning window so that product owners can work with autonomy.

## Class 6

This session covered how to turn real business problems into AI/ML problems, starting from customer value rather than technology. The second half used customer sentiment analysis as a worked example of how product managers can extract decisions from unstructured feedback.

### Core Concepts

- **Art of the possible and reimagining workflows:** AI product thinking starts by questioning old assumptions and constraints. Many processes were designed around limits that technology has since removed.
- **Translating business problems into AI/ML targets:** Treat the solution as a "black box" between inputs and outcomes. Decide which inputs to keep, cut or add, what exactly to predict, and how to evaluate it. A wrong target means optimising the wrong thing very well.
- **Data risks and governance:** Missing or biased data, leakage, consent issues and bias amplification can make a product "dead on arrival". Organisations are custodians of customer data, not owners, and must comply with laws such as DPDP and GDPR. Teams should also think about the "blast radius" and choose carefully what is worth measuring.
- **Sentiment analysis for product decisions:** An opinion has structure: object, feature, sentiment, opinion holder and time. Analysing at the feature level is far more useful than a simple positive/negative score. It is not one problem but several (entity recognition, coreference, comparisons, spam detection), and accuracy is still imperfect.

### Reflections and Insights

- **Requirements belong in the stakeholder's voice.** A PRD should be written as "I, as a \[role\], want…", with consistent terminology across the team. Technical constraints such as scale belong in the separate product specification, because a user cares about their own experience, not the system's capacity.
- **Build category killers, not clones.** A product manager adds value only by creating something that beats the reference product, not by copying it.
- **Low adoption cost is part of the MVP.** If users must juggle several platforms to get value, the product has failed even if its features are good.
- **Measurement changes the system, as in the uncertainty principle.** Measuring more adds cost, slows things down and creates data risk, so deciding what to measure is a product decision.
- **Emotion is a hidden signal.** A review that mentions the buyer's family reacting is not a feature, but it shows the human context around a purchase that feature-by-feature analysis misses.
- **Data choices are product investment choices.** In demand forecasting, the true goal is having the right stock in the right place at the right time, not model accuracy.
- **Marketing cannot rescue a flawed product.** The course focuses on building a great product, and awareness-building is left to marketing courses.

## Class 7

This session used a live case, an AI-driven customer support and outage-management product for electricity distribution companies, to explore how a product manager thinks about AI products. The discussion moved from product reimagination and pricing to market positioning, handling AI uncertainty, and managing the product across its full lifecycle. The recurring theme was that product management is an integrated discipline: ideas from pricing, design, risk and lifecycle all influence one another.

### Core Concepts

- **Reimagining the product, not just automating it:** Instead of building a better call center, the aim should be to detect problems before customers report them. This means proactive notifications, location-aware outage detection and smart dispatch, so that the support line is needed less.
- **Value-based pricing and positioning:** Cost-plus pricing sets a floor, but real pricing comes from the value created for the customer and the share of it you capture. The supplier's share typically falls in a modest band (roughly 15–25%, with about 20% as a working assumption). Understanding the buyer's motivations, financials, contracts and decision-makers matters more than generic pricing theory. Pricing models (per seat, per ticket, outcome-based) also carry side effects on future revenue.
- **Designing for AI uncertainty (Facts, Features, Inferences, Recommendations):** Hallucination is not binary. Facts can be grounded in reliable data lookups, inferences need data support, and recommendations should show their chain of evidence. The UI should signal confidence differently for sure, unsure and unknown outputs, with humans in the loop to confirm and refine.
- **Full AI product lifecycle ownership:** Launch is the beginning, not the end. AI products add risks that traditional software lacks: dependence on third-party model providers (buy vs. build vs. subscribe), model behavior drifting after launch, and the need for continuous monitoring and rollback. Data stewardship (consent, purpose limitation, deletion), safe retirement and trust management all remain the product manager's responsibility.

### Reflections and Insights

- **Reframe the problem, then the product:** The strongest idea in the session was shifting from "handle outage calls well" to "make outage calls unnecessary." Reimagining the problem often creates more value than optimizing the existing process.
- **A product's value may sit in a different place than its feature:** A support tool can matter economically because of what it prevents, such as power bought but not delivered, or reputational and political fallout. The buyer's real pain point may not be the one the product visibly addresses.
- **Know which side of the table you sit on:** The product manager is the seller creating an opportunity, not the customer organization deciding what to do. Pricing, positioning and pitches must be argued from that vantage point and grounded in the specific context rather than in generic frameworks.
- **Success can shrink your own revenue:** If a product works too well, usage-based or seat-based revenue can fall. Pricing design has to anticipate this, and land-and-expand or adjacent markets can offset it.
- **AI products never reach a stable "done" state:** Because model behavior and vendor conditions can change after launch, the product manager carries ongoing responsibility. That also means pacing energy and effort for the long post-launch phase rather than treating launch as a finish line.

## Class 8

This session looked at how AI products behave once they leave the lab. It covered the unpredictability of generative AI tools and the limits of LLMs where deterministic outputs are needed. It also covered how a product moves from concept to prototype, MVP, pilot and scale, and why the product manager's role matters at each stage. A running theme was designing for reuse, generalisation and a clearly defined customer.

### Core Concepts

- **Unpredictability of generative AI:** Provider-side instability can make the same tool and inputs perform differently over time. Self-hosted models with fixed weights, seed and temperature are far more stable. Models also do not retain learning between sessions, and AI can silently carry forward wrong assumptions during long-running tasks.
- **Fit-for-purpose AI (LLM vs. RAG vs. deterministic):** Probabilistic LLMs are a poor fit where exact, repeatable outputs are essential (e.g., rating or pricing engines). RAG, rule-based logic and API/tool calls often need to be combined into a hybrid, agentic solution. Simple RAG breaks down as data grows.
- **Staged product development with gates:** Concept validation, prototype, MVP, pilot and scale each need a clear learning objective, a documented go/no-go decision and right-sized governance. Prototypes can answer the wrong question, and an MVP must prove value to the customer. Automation should be staged, with human-in-the-loop fallbacks and honesty about any manual effort behind the scenes.
- **Customer clarity, reuse and generalisation:** Define one specific customer and their workflow, and build a generic product rather than a one-customer solution. Keep vendors and models pluggable, and use hooks and wrappers so that the product can be repurposed across markets and use cases.

### Reflections and Insights

- **The PM becomes the bottleneck.** AI speeds up execution and floods the product manager with proposals and decisions. The real risk is approving by default ("just go ahead") and later paying more to undo a wrong assumption than it would have cost to get it right. Protecting decision bandwidth, and knowing what to delegate, is a core skill.
- **Accountability cannot be delegated to AI.** Explainability, fairness and risk ownership remain with the product owner. Stakeholders and regulators will hold people responsible, not the model.
- **Not every problem needs an LLM.** The better move is often to apply AI to a narrower part of the problem, such as cleaning and standardising inputs before a human decision, rather than automating the highest-risk decision first. Choosing a non-AI approach when it fits is a sign of maturity.
- **Learn before you automate.** Premature automation and hidden manual labour both erode trust. Being transparent about what is manual, and using human-in-the-loop time as learning time, builds credibility with customers and teams.
- **Resilience, reuse and repurposing matter as much as the technology.** Markets, competitors and targets keep moving. A product manager who can repurpose a capability and spot new use cases for an apparent limitation can turn a failed fit into a new market.
- **Define success criteria up front.** Documenting decision criteria early, and avoiding endless requirement additions while waiting for the "perfect" customer, protects both the product and team morale.

## Class 9

This session looked at how AI products should be measured and evaluated from a management point of view, not just a technical one. It covered why metrics shape trust, margins and workflow, and why model-level scores can mislead. It also covered how to design experiments, learn from feedback, and choose a small set of metrics that reflect real business value and risk.

### Core Concepts

- **Economic-value metrics over mathematical accuracy:** Precision, recall and accuracy don't capture the unequal payoffs between outcomes. Build a payoff matrix with the profit, loss and opportunity cost of each prediction type, because one bad decision can wipe out the gains from many good ones.
- **Tail risk and survivability:** Averages hide edge cases, and edge cases can shut down a product or a company. Product managers must look at worst-case scenarios, simulate them, and decide how much risk to carry versus transfer (for example, through insurance or reinsurance).
- **Experimentation and feedback loops:** A/B testing needs hypotheses, control groups, significance checks and awareness of bias. With third-party LLMs that change underneath you, experiments are never fully controlled. Feedback can improve a product but can also over-tune it to one segment, so it needs governance.
- **Managerial lens on error types and metric design:** Ask who pays for false positives and false negatives, and how incentives and measurement systems drive behavior. Keep metrics few and simple (about three to start), tie them to a balanced scorecard, and evolve them as the product matures.

### Reflections and Insights

- **A product is a promise of value.** Marketing only makes the promise. Reputation comes from the value users actually receive, so AI metrics should test whether the promise holds.
- **Separate what you control from what you can only sense.** Evaluation quality is in your hands. Competitors, regulation and macroeconomics are not, but they can be anticipated, for example by comparing today's competitive analysis with a projected one 18 months out.
- **Optimizing the wrong metric is a hidden risk.** Revenue can look healthy while new-customer acquisition quietly declines. If your metrics haven't changed as the product matured, you may be blind to an emerging trend or to a disruptor attacking from another dimension, such as small, fast models replacing ever-larger ones.
- **Probability assumes you survive long enough to play.** A model that is right on average is useless if a single catastrophic loss ends the game, so one-shot, irreversible decisions need far more caution than repeatable ones.
- **Metrics are a managerial accountability.** The product manager owns metric design even when others draft it, because the metrics shape team behavior. Rewarding the wrong measure (such as raw bug counts) produces the wrong behavior.
- **Societal and emotional impact count.** AI errors affect real people, and liability for them (vendor versus deployer) is often unclear. Stakeholders also decide differently under emotional stress, which a product must account for.

## Class 10

This session looked at what it means to scale an AI-powered product, and why scaling is a business and product-promise problem as much as a technical one. It covered how growth in usage, data, workflow complexity and customer dependence raises the stakes for reliability. It also covered the trade-offs between quality, latency and cost in AI systems, and the controls that keep quality repeatable as a product grows.

### Core Concepts

- **Scale the promise, not the servers:** Customers buy a product for what it reliably delivers, not for its infrastructure. Once a product becomes mission-critical, expectations harden faster than infrastructure can keep up, and legal disclaimers offer little protection if the lived promise is broken. A customer promise matrix, stress tests that check answer quality as well as uptime, and a planned degraded mode all help.
- **The quality–latency–cost sweet spot:** Better reasoning and larger models cost more and respond more slowly. The product manager must define an acceptable range for each of the three and communicate it to the team. Otherwise, different people pull the product to opposite extremes. Smaller models, routing, caching and tiering help, and the right range changes by segment, by competitor and over time.
- **Versioning, lineage and governance:** Versioning must cover models, data, experiments and product promises, not just code, so that a regression in one segment can be detected, rerouted or rolled back. Lineage and traceability matter most in regulated domains. Governance for agents has to grow faster than the number of agents.
- **Multi-tenant fairness and AI unit economics:** At scale, rate limits, queues, customer tiers and fair-use policies decide who gets served and how fast. Token economics, prompt and context size, and caching (with its privacy limits) decide profitability. A weak economic model is exposed by growth rather than fixed by it.

### Reflections and Insights

- **Dependence is both the goal and the liability.** The same user dependence that creates lock-in and revenue also removes users' tolerance for failure. Success raises the cost of every outage, so reliability work should rise with adoption, not trail it.
- **Many AI demos hide an economic problem.** A demo that costs tens of dollars in API calls looks like a win, but every prospect who tries it repeats that cost. Unit economics need testing before a product is marketed.
- **Cost control has to be intentional.** Large organizations are already cutting per-person AI spend sharply, which shows that "use AI everywhere" and "use frontier models everywhere" are separate decisions. Deciding when a small model is enough is becoming a core product skill.
- **Predictability can matter more than speed.** Slightly slower but consistent responses can serve users better than fast but erratic ones. Many quality–latency decisions should be framed around the user's experience, not the engineering metric.
- **The product manager's real leverage is decision boundaries.** Teams make daily decisions after release, so a stated trade-off window and escalation rules stop quality or cost from drifting silently. Alerts on business metrics and promises should stay with the product owner, not be delegated away.
- **Teams need different working rhythms.** AI products are a marathon, not a sprint. Intense bursts of effort are useful but not sustainable, and some people are better suited to steady, planned operation. Good product leadership matches people to the right mode and avoids measuring everyone the same way.
- **Notice improvement as well as failure.** Monitoring should flag unexpected quality gains, which can be explained and turned into marketing, not just regressions.
- **Expectations only move upward.** What delights users today becomes the baseline tomorrow, so the acceptable range for latency, quality and cost has to be revisited regularly.

## Class 11

### Introduction

This session looked at how to build AI products ethically, responsibly and with the risks understood. It covered where bias comes from, how privacy and data choices shape a product, and the particular risks of generative AI. It then turned to the governance and risk-management practices a product manager needs before and after launch. The recurring theme was that product managers stay accountable for AI outcomes even when they cannot see inside the data or models they depend on.

### Core Concepts

- **Bias and fairness:** Bias can enter through the data, the labeling, the training set, organizational policies and workflows. It is often unintentional, and awareness is the first step. Fairness has no universal standard, so teams should meet the minimum set by law and industry guidelines and then go beyond it, treating this as ongoing work. With traditional AI you can audit your own data, while with generative AI you depend on vendors or open-weight models whose training data you cannot see.
- **Privacy and data governance:** Collect only the data you can defend needing, with clear consent and a stated purpose. Define retention limits and take extra care with sensitive categories such as children's and health data. People own their data and companies are only trustees, which matches the logic of laws like DPDP and GDPR. Minimizing data also reduces bias, because attributes that are never collected cannot be used or inferred.
- **Generative AI risks:** Hallucination, invented policies or facts, adversarial data poisoning and weak explainability all need explicit controls. Grounding with retrieval (RAG), limiting the options a model can choose from, and sharing only need-to-know information all reduce risk. A well-designed product also knows when to answer, when to abstain and when to escalate to a human.
- **Responsible AI operations and risk assessment:** This covers human-in-the-loop review (including a shadow review workflow), visible user controls, auditability, incident response and clear communication when something goes wrong. Risk is assessed on severity, likelihood, detectability and reversibility, and each risk is mitigated, transferred or accepted. Launch decisions use limited rollouts and a launch-gate checklist tailored to the product, with both minimum criteria and launch blockers.

### Reflections and Insights

- **Bias comes from limited exposure, not bad intent.** A system's view of the world is only as broad as what it has seen, so an AI trained on narrow data makes the same kind of confident, skewed generalizations a young child does. The fix is not a one-time check but continuous questioning of "who is advantaged or harmed?"
- **Generic principles are not enough.** "Ensure fairness and privacy" means nothing until it is defined for a specific product, customer base and context. Fairness, privacy and interpretability each need a concrete definition, threshold and checklist item.
- **Data is tempting, and restraint is a leadership call.** The urge to collect more is constant, and the product manager has to accept the opportunity cost of not collecting it. Whatever is collected should be defensible in front of a regulator.
- **Abstaining can be a feature.** An AI that declines to answer is safer than one that confidently invents an answer, and it also makes commercial sense, since you can avoid charging for an answer you didn't give.
- **Governance can become a bottleneck or a theater.** Review boards that nobody can justify, or that rely on unpaid extra effort from busy experts, tend to get bypassed. Governance should be lean and should scale faster than the number of AI agents it oversees. Committees can recommend, but the product owner remains accountable.
- **A product is a promise, and it carries a PR risk.** One biased outcome can undo years of work once it spreads on social media. Clear, proactive communication during an incident prevents fear from growing.
- **Match the AI type to the context.** Physics- and math-based AI, such as metering or predictive maintenance, carries little fairness risk compared with AI that makes decisions about people. Knowing whether a feature uses traditional or generative AI shapes which risks matter.
- **Judgment under uncertainty is the real skill.** Management is not an exact science, and product managers add value by making justified decisions with incomplete information. They remain answerable to the stakeholders whose money and trust they hold.

## Class 12

### Introduction

This session covered how to specify an AI product precisely enough to be built and managed predictably, and how to design its monetization. The first part dealt with removing ambiguity from product specifications through measurable targets, explicit workflows and clear stakeholders. The second part covered revenue design and pricing: value-based pricing, cost-to-serve, inference economics and platform strategy.

### Core Concepts

- **Unambiguous, contract-grade product specification:** Vague terms such as "accurate," "user-friendly" or "unsafe" create risk with engineering teams and vendors. Each should be defined with a metric, a measurement method and a numeric target, and a product should be built to be configurable and extensible rather than hard-coded.
- **Workflows as code:** Workflows are best shown as swimlane diagrams written as text (PlantUML or Mermaid), with each step marked as deterministic or probabilistic/LLM-driven. These diagrams also show where AI is uncertain, where a human is in the loop, and what each step costs. Text-based diagrams are easy to version and cheaper for AI to read than images. The C4 model gives layered views of a system, and a product manager mainly needs the context and container levels.
- **Value-based pricing and cost-to-serve:** Work backwards from what the customer will pay (target costing) rather than adding a margin to build cost. Customers value outputs and outcomes, while the vendor's costs are driven by inputs, so usage-based, outcome-linked, hybrid and subscription models each shift risk differently. Inference or API cost is the key variable cost and must be stress-tested for heavy users, usage spikes and rate limits.
- **Unit economics, trade-offs and platform strategy:** The sweet spot between latency, cost, quality and trust is a product management decision. Pricing and architecture influence each other, and unbounded agentic loops can inflate costs. Contribution margin by workflow and segment, overhead spread across volume, retention and downgrades all matter, as do ecosystem and platform effects and the choice between standalone and embedded AI.

### Reflections and Insights

- **Ambiguity is a financial risk, not just a documentation flaw.** An undefined word in a specification becomes a dispute over scope and cost once a vendor interprets it differently from the product owner.
- **Customers buy the value, not the product.** Insurance is bought for a safety net, and AI is bought for outcomes. Pricing, positioning and marketing should all follow from how each named stakeholder perceives value, so documents should name specific stakeholders instead of a generic "user."
- **Generative AI breaks the usual cost assumptions.** Success can hurt margins, since heavier usage raises inference cost, and customers pay for tokens even when the output is poor or hallucinated. Choices about charging for unmet goals, capping loops and downgrading customers are pricing decisions as well as engineering ones.
- **Architecture and economics are inseparable.** Licensing models have historically driven design decisions. A model-routing layer may save on tokens but add context-reset costs, so the trade-offs need to be measured rather than assumed.
- **Growth is not automatically healthy.** Every new customer in a low-margin or negative-margin segment can erode overall margin, so a product manager should be able to explain unit economics in a minute and know which workflows and segments actually earn money.
- **Competition now happens between ecosystems.** Platform value grows faster than user count, so aligning with or building an ecosystem is a strategic choice that sits alongside product quality.
