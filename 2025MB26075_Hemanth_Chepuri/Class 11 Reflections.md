## 1. Key Learnings & Takeaways

- Bias can enter an AI product through the data, labels, historical decisions, model design and even the assumptions made by the product team.
- “Fairness” cannot simply be a checkbox. It has to be defined in the context of the specific product and its stakeholders.
- Data minimisation can reduce both privacy exposure and unnecessary bias.
- Consent, purpose of collection, retention and deletion are product considerations, not only legal requirements.
- AI governance should keep pace with the product without becoming bureaucracy.
- Risk assessment should consider both likelihood and severity, and the PM needs to understand who carries the consequences of a failure.

## 2. What Resonated Most With Me

The biggest takeaway was that product managers cannot simply say, “The data team will handle bias” or “Legal will handle privacy.” The decisions made during product design determine what data is collected, how it is used and what outcomes the system produces.

I particularly liked the point that collecting less data can sometimes be better. There is a natural tendency to keep collecting data because it might become useful later, but unnecessary data increases privacy obligations and potentially increases the number of ways a product can go wrong.

The discussion also reinforced that a disclaimer does not automatically make a risky AI product responsible.

## 3. Constructive Thoughts & What Could Be Explored Further

I would like to explore practical fairness assessment methods for specific product contexts. It seems unrealistic to aim for an abstract definition of “zero bias”; the better question is which risks are material for the users and how they can be detected and controlled.

## 4. Personal Action Item

For my project, I will explicitly document **what data is collected, why it is needed, how consent is obtained, how long it is retained, who can access it, and what the major AI risks are**.
