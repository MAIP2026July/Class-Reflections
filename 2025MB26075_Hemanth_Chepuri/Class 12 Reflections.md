## 1. Key Learnings & Takeaways

- A strong PRD should be detailed enough that another person can understand and build the intended product without relying on the PM's memory.
- Ambiguous words such as “fast”, “accurate”, “user friendly” or “beautiful” are dangerous because different people can interpret them differently.
- Product requirements and technical specifications are related but should not be confused.
- Architecture matters because the PM needs to understand what is possible, what is risky and what creates dependency.
- AI products need to explicitly account for hallucination, probabilistic outputs and human review where necessary.
- A strong product proposal should connect the problem, product value, feasibility, technology and economics.

## 2. What Resonated Most With Me

The strongest point for me was the idea of using the PRD as a delegation test:

**“Could I give this document to someone else and expect them to build the right thing?”**

That is a much better test than simply asking whether the PRD looks complete.

It also made me think differently about ambiguity. If the PM writes “reduce response time” without defining what that means, the engineering team may meet the requirement according to a completely different interpretation. That can create cost and rework later.

## 3. Constructive Thoughts & What Could Be Explored Further

I would like to practise writing acceptance criteria and measurable requirements for AI features because the probabilistic nature of AI makes “done” more difficult to define than in deterministic software.

## 4. Personal Action Item

Before considering a PRD complete, I will try to hand it mentally to a person who knows nothing about the product and ask: **“Could this person build the intended solution without repeatedly asking me what I meant?”**
