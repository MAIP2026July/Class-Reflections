## 1. Key Learnings & Takeaways

- The team presentations made the difference between a product idea and a product that has actually been thought through much clearer.
- A prototype is useful only when it helps demonstrate the intended user experience or reduce a specific uncertainty.
- AI should be explicitly identified in the product workflow — including where it is used, where it makes decisions and where a human reviews the output.
- For higher-risk AI outputs, the product should visually communicate uncertainty rather than hiding behind a disclaimer.
- Product value needs to be connected to the actual customer and domain rather than simply describing features.
- Domain, technology, regulation and customer behaviour all influence whether a product can actually succeed.

## 2. What Resonated Most With Me

Watching different teams present gave me a perspective that is difficult to get from a normal lecture. Two teams can have technically interesting ideas but have very different levels of product maturity.

The feedback around swimlane diagrams and clearly identifying AI gates was particularly useful. It forces the team to answer exactly where AI is making a decision, where deterministic logic is used and where a human needs to intervene.

I also liked the repeated focus on customer motivation. A product can be technically possible, but that does not mean patients, doctors, employees or customers will change their existing behaviour.

## 3. Constructive Thoughts & What Could Be Explored Further

I think peer presentations are valuable because they expose assumptions that a team can easily miss when working only inside its own project. A structured peer-review checklist could make that learning even stronger.

## 4. Personal Action Item

When reviewing my own project, I will ask four questions explicitly: **Is it valuable? Is it feasible? Who needs to change behaviour? Where does AI create risk and where does a human intervene?**
