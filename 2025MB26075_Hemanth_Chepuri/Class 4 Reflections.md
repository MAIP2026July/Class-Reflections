## 1. Key Learnings & Takeaways

- The central question was where and when AI actually creates product value.
- I learned to separate business value from technical feasibility. Something technically possible is not automatically valuable.
- The FDV approach — Feasibility, Desirability and Viability — provides a useful way to compare opportunities more consistently.
- AI should not automatically mean generative or agentic AI. The simplest technology capable of solving the problem may be the better choice.
- Product-market fit depends on the actual customer segment. A technically sophisticated solution can still fail if it is solving the wrong problem for the wrong market.
- Risk, especially low-probability high-impact outcomes, needs to be considered when evaluating AI opportunities.

## 2. What Resonated Most With Me

The product-market-fit example was particularly useful. A product can look impressive technically and still fail because the assumptions about the customer are wrong. That made me realize that PMs should validate the market and customer context before investing heavily in model sophistication.

I also found the discussion around “Do we really need AI here?” important. There can be a tendency to use the most advanced AI capability available simply because it is available. The better question is what level of intelligence is actually required to create the intended outcome.

## 3. Constructive Thoughts & What Could Be Explored Further

I would like to practise the FDV scoring method on real product ideas and understand how a PM should deal with situations where desirability is high but feasibility or viability is uncertain.

## 4. Personal Action Item

For every AI idea I evaluate, I will separately ask: **What is the customer value? Is it technically feasible? Is there a viable business behind it?** Only then will I think about the AI technique.
