## 1. Key Learnings & Takeaways

- AI products increasingly involve multiple models, agents and vendors, so keeping AI components replaceable is strategically important.
- Vendor lock-in is not only a technical problem; it can become a product and business risk.
- Reuse, repurpose and resilience are useful ways to think about whether a product capability can survive changing technology.
- A prototype and an MVP are not the same thing. A prototype is primarily for learning and reducing uncertainty, while an MVP should provide real value to a real target customer.
- A prototype should have a clear learning objective. Otherwise it can become an attractive demo that does not answer an important product question.
- Governance and risk management need to keep pace with the product rather than becoming paperwork that slows everything down.

## 2. What Resonated Most With Me

The statement **“prototype answers the wrong question”** was a useful way of thinking about it. A prototype can look impressive and still be useless if it proves something nobody actually needs to know.

I also found the idea of keeping generative AI vendors pluggable important. The model ecosystem is changing quickly, so designing the product around one vendor's specific capability can create unnecessary dependence.

The discussion around reuse and repurposing also made me think beyond the initial use case. A capability may fail in one environment but become valuable somewhere else if the underlying strength is understood properly.

## 3. Constructive Thoughts & What Could Be Explored Further

I would like to understand how much architecture should be designed for flexibility at the prototype stage without over-engineering a product that may not even survive validation.

## 4. Personal Action Item

Before building a prototype, I will document **what uncertainty the prototype is supposed to remove** and what evidence would tell me that the experiment succeeded.
