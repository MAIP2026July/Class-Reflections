# Session 2 Reflection
 
**Date:** 1 August 2026


**Topic:** Product and Project Thinking

 
This session clarified the difference between a project and a product. A project normally begins with a known customer and a defined requirement, whereas a product should be capable of solving a similar problem for many customers. I found this distinction useful because sometimes a solution is called a product even though it has been heavily customised for only one customer.
 
Another useful learning was that every decision does not have to be made immediately. The house construction example made this easy to understand. Decisions related to the basic structure must be taken early, but decisions such as the colour of the walls can be postponed. Similarly, in product development, important and difficult-to-reverse decisions should be prioritised, while other decisions can remain open until better information is available. However, these open decisions should be communicated clearly to avoid confusion.
 
The discussion about beta releases was also practical. I normally viewed beta as a stage close to completion. The credit card example showed that reaching beta may only mean that the visible functionality is ready. Operational processes, settlements, support and exception handling may still require significant effort. Therefore, teams should not exhaust all their energy just to reach beta.
 
We covered frameworks such as Jobs to Be Done, RICE, Kano, QFD and the Traceability Matrix. My understanding is that these frameworks should support thinking rather than replace it. The more important requirement is traceability. A business decision should be visible in the requirements, system design, sprint backlog and testing. Otherwise, a decision made at the management level may never reach the final product.
 
I also understood the difference between an AI-enabled product and an AI-native product. An AI-enabled product adds AI to improve an existing workflow, while an AI-native product may not be possible without AI. An AI Product Manager may not need to develop models but should understand enough to discuss trade-offs such as cost, speed, accuracy and risk.
