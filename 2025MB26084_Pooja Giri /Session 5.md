# Session 5 Reflection
 
**Date:** 29 August 2026

**Topic:** Identifying Useful AI Opportunities

 
This class focused on finding problems where AI can create real value instead of adding AI only because it is popular. Some of the factors discussed were how frequently the problem occurs, how serious the pain is, whether suitable data is available and how important the related decision is.
 
One distinction I found useful was between products that help users finish a task and leave, and products where continued engagement is part of the value. For example, an internal business application should ideally help an employee complete the work quickly. Measuring success through time spent in such an application could give the wrong signal because a longer duration may indicate that the tool is inefficient.
 
The ceiling fan example explained why product claims need evidence. Technical terms and AI labels may attract initial attention, but customers will eventually expect proof of improvement. A product should be able to demonstrate what became faster, more accurate, less expensive or easier after introducing AI. Initial curiosity should not be confused with product-market fit. Repeated usage and continued value are stronger indicators.
 
The four-layer AI stack also gave me a simple way to analyse an AI product: Data, Model, API and User Experience. A failure can happen at any of these layers, but the user usually experiences it through the interface. A technically strong model is therefore not enough if the overall experience is confusing or unreliable.
 
We again discussed switching costs and human oversight. Even a better solution may struggle if users must move between different tools, enter the same information again or completely change their routine. Similarly, sending every AI output for human review may create alert fatigue. Human attention should be focused on cases where the confidence is low or the consequence of an error is high.
