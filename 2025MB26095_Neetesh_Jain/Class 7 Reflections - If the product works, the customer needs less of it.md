# Session 7: If the product works, the customer needs less of it

12 September 2026 | Neetesh Jain | 2025MB26095

---

The pricing discussion produced the sharpest thing I have heard in this course, and it was said almost in passing.

If you sell a call centre solution priced by the number of seats, and your product works, the customer needs fewer seats. Your revenue falls in proportion to how well you have done your job. Price by the unit of the problem and you are financially punished for solving it.

I own the economic model on our team product and I had not looked for this. Our value story is that the delivery manager catches problems earlier and escalations drop. If we ever price against escalations handled or reviews conducted, we have built the same trap. The pricing unit has to be something that grows with the customer's success rather than with their pain, and that is a product decision, not a commercial one, which is why it should have been in my document and was not.

**The customer's balance sheet is where the value is.** The faculty took the distribution company apart. They buy power on forward contracts at four or five rupees and sell at ten or twelve. When an area is out, that power was already bought and paid for and now cannot be sold. So detecting a fault fast is not customer satisfaction, it is money that already left their account being recovered. He found the value of the product inside the customer's accounts rather than in the feature list.

I built our model from the cost side because costs are the only numbers an engineer is ever shown, and I wrote that in an earlier reflection as a limitation. This session showed me the method I was missing. Go and read how the customer actually makes and loses money, then find your product somewhere in that. I have never once read a client's annual report.

**Willingness to pay belongs to people, not organisations.** Companies do not want things. The individuals running them do, and their motivations are personal, including reputational and political ones. I think in systems and I had been modelling the buyer as an organisation with a budget line. That is why my model has a budget and no buyer in it.

**The risk category I did not have.** Supplier viability. Porter applied to your own dependencies. What happens when the subscription stops, the model is deprecated, or the vendor's finances turn. The example used was that several AI companies are heavily in debt and that Oracle is leveraged and dependent on a small number of customers. My risk register covers what our product might do wrong. It has nothing about our suppliers simply ceasing to be there, and the mitigation, long term contracts, costs money that is not in my pricing model.

**TFIR.** Truth, facts, inferences, recommendations, with hallucination treated as shades of grey rather than a yes or no, and the trigger sitting upstream of the model. For me this finally connects to the testing gap I admitted in session 5. Truth and facts are testable the way I already know how to test. Inferences are testable statistically. Recommendations are the rung where my methods run out, and that is the rung we are shipping.
