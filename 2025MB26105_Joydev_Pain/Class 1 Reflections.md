# Reflection: Session 1
25 July 2026 | Joydev Pain | 2025MB26105
---
The class opened with familiar, textbook definitions of a product manager: bridging business and engineering, managing the lifecycle, translating strategy into execution. Having spent years in architecture and enterprise delivery, I would have defaulted to one of those answers and felt confident in it. Then the faculty pointed out that nobody had mentioned **ownership**, and the gap immediately struck a nerve.
In enterprise technology and architecture, you are trained to live in that "bridging" language. You design the blueprint, align stakeholders, and mediate between technical feasibility and business demand. That framing feels safe because it allows you to stand slightly outside the final outcome. When an initiative stalls, the unspoken defense is always ready: *the architecture was sound; the execution or the client's organizational readiness was the problem.* I may never have voiced that aloud, but I have certainly sheltered behind that rationale. The faculty’s cricket captain analogy stripped that comfort away: you cannot wear the captain's armband only in the weeks your team wins.
**Where I push back.** The faculty argued that the excuse *"engineering failed to deliver"* is off the table because engineering ultimately belongs to you. In enterprise consulting and complex matrixed organizations, that is not entirely true. Delivery teams and specialist architects are frequently staffed, rotated, or reallocated by a central resourcing function balancing utilization rates across accounts. I have had key engineers and architects reassigned mid-sprint with zero say in the matter. The honest reality in my context is that **the consequence is mine even when direct control was not.** That is a far tougher pill to swallow than the traditional "CEO of the product" trope, but it is the authentic one. If I want the authority to match the accountability, I have to proactively claim it rather than wait for an organizational chart to grant it.
**The story that landed.** An architect once proposed a technically superior but niche, exotic database, and the faculty rejected it—not because the architecture was flawed, but because the local talent pool was thin and he refused to create key-person dependency. The decision hinged on an operational risk factor that never appears on a technical evaluation scorecard.
Reflecting on my own career, I have made the opposite mistake. In pursuit of technical elegance, I approved custom abstraction layers and frameworks over vanilla platform capabilities. On pure technical merits, it was superior. In operational reality, every onboarded developer required weeks of ramp-up on proprietary patterns that added zero value to their broader career, and the moment the original author rolled off, team velocity cratered. I evaluated the design quality while completely ignoring the staffing, maintenance, and talent liquidity consequences. That may be the defining line between a Lead Architect and a true Product Owner: not the technical depth of your evaluation, but which organizational columns you consider your responsibility to fill.
**What I will do differently.**
1. **The 18-Month Maintenance Test:** On every architectural proposal and design review, mandate a one-line answer to: *"Who can maintain and debug this in 18 months if the founding engineers leave?"* If the answer depends on rare expertise, that is a red flag on the product decision.
2. **Transparent Decision Rationale:** Communicate the *why* behind rejections consistently. I often reject approaches based on unspoken operational risks, but fail to articulate those trade-offs clearly when under delivery pressure.
**Open question.** Today, enterprise teams talk incessantly about using AI to build products (code assistants) and embedding AI into customer-facing features. Yet virtually no one is leveraging AI for the hard, deliberative work of prioritization, trade-off analysis, and strategic risk modeling. Is this omission simply an early-adoption blind spot, or does it reflect how reluctant we are to let algorithmic tools scrutinize the political and subjective nature of executive decision-making?
