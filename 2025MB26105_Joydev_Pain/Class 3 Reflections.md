# Reflection: Session 3

The faculty opened with an immediate ban on the unqualified word *"user."* Name the specific stakeholder first; only then can you construct an authentic persona.

That evening, I audited the architectural specifications and requirement matrices of my recent enterprise engagements. The word "user" appeared dozens of times, casually masking four conflicting personas: the front-line analyst entering transaction data, the operations manager monitoring daily throughput, the enterprise risk auditor reviewing compliance trails, and the executive sponsor funding the platform. Because everyone used the generic label "the user," every stakeholder nodding in agreement was imagining someone entirely different. 

More importantly, generic labels conceal political and economic conflicts of interest. Once you replace "the user needs audit logs" with *"the compliance officer requires automated surveillance over the underwriting desk,"* the friction becomes glaring. The underwriter’s throughput is measured by speed, yet the new feature adds input friction to feed an audit trail that scrutinizes their decisions. When you lump both under "user experience," you fail to anticipate why the frontline silently rejects the tool.

**The leadership argument I would have instinctively lost.** A classmate argued that a Product Manager is measured strictly on business vision, roadmap delivery, and ROI—and that investing in team development drifts into program management or HR territory. In my architectural career, I would have championed that exact position. My comfort zone has always been: *I own the technical vision and architectural integrity; the people belong to the resourcing pool.* 

The faculty pushed back with a profound distinction: project teams disperse at handoff, but product and platform teams endure. A vision without an intrinsically motivated team leaves the leader isolated in an empty room. What shattered my defense was the closing diagnostic question: *"Would your past team members choose to work with you again if the financial incentives were stripped away?"* Across dozens of delivery cycles and hundreds of engineers, very few would follow purely out of loyalty to the vision. That uncomfortable truth is the direct consequence of treating human talent as an interchangeable resource rather than a co-owner of the product mission.

**Transformation vs. Optimization, and the Reality of "Sandbagging."** The faculty’s metaphor of the caterpillar and the butterfly—true metamorphosis versus just a faster caterpillar—is compelling, but it breaks down under the athlete analogy: pushing an enterprise system from 94% to 96% reliability or model precision is exponentially harder and often more commercially valuable than moving from 40% to 60%.

Where the lesson hit home was the dynamic of **sandbagging**. In large enterprises, if an architecture team automates 30% of a workflow this year, leadership immediately locks that in as the baseline and demands an additional 20% next year. Rational delivery leaders quickly learn to release a 6% efficiency gain and bank the remaining headroom to protect their teams from unsustainable quotas. This tactical rationing explains why enterprise optimization curves appear so suspiciously smooth and incremental.

**A pricing anchor to implement: The 80/20 Value Capture Rule.** The historical benchmark that a successful product captures roughly ~20% of the economic value created while leaving ~80% with the customer provides a much-needed baseline. In enterprise services and custom systems delivery, pricing is trapped in cost-plus mechanics: [Rate Card x Hours Billed + Margin]. We calculate what the solution costs us to build, but almost never quantify the net economic surplus generated for the enterprise (e.g., millions saved in underwriting cycle times or operational risk reduction). Without an anchor in customer value capture, technical leaders either leave immense value on the table or overcharge for commodity plumbing.

**Evolution from Session 1.** In Session 1, I pushed back on the idea that the team belongs to the product owner when staffing is dictated by external resourcing. While I still recognize that formal staffing control is often withheld, I now realize that using lack of control as an emotional escape hatch is a failure of leadership. The consequence of team cohesion and morale remains mine, whether corporate governance gives me the formal title or not.
