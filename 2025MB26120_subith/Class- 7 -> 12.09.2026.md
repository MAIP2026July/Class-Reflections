
 
Discussed that PMs cannot remain detached from the products they are responsible for. Even when technical teams, vendors, customer support teams, or external partners are involved, ownership of the overall outcome is with the PM. This is a recurring learning point that I think any PM must embed. One part of the discussion that resonated with me was the emphasis on staying close to users rather than managing entirely through reports and dashboards. I have got very useful insights from directly interacting with clinicians, patients, and operational teams and not through reviewing BI dashboards at work. Many issues become obvious only when one spends time understanding how work is actually being performed on the ground. We had a productive conversation around a sample case - customer support issue in electricity distribution wherein each of us pitched in with our view of what could be the ideal solution for the problem given. It was a useful reminder that many business problems are interconnected. Solving one visible issue often requires understanding several underlying processes. We also discussed the PFIR evaluation model: facts/features, inferences, and recommendations. A product must also demonstrate its value in a way that customers are willing to pay for. I have faced this often at workplace. For any initiative, value must be measurable and visible. Then only we will be able to get management buy in. The same principle appears to apply to products. There are a lot of assumptions in AI systems.customer experience, pricing, AI uncertainty and the risks that continue after launch. The electricity support example showed me the difference between reactive and proactive design. The simple approach is to use AI to answer complaints faster. The better approach is to use operational data to spot the outage before the customer even calls, and then tell them what has happened and when it will be fixed. It struck me that the best support experience may be the one where the customer never has to contact support at all. But a proactive system also carries a responsibility. It should not present uncertain information as if it were confirmed. When confidence is low, the interface should show that honestly, ask for verification, or hold back an alert that may not be needed. A general line saying "AI can make mistakes" does not solve this. The design itself must handle the uncertainty. Value-based pricing was completely new to me. I had always assumed that price follows the cost of building and running a product. The class showed that price should also reflect the economic value the customer receives. We discussed capturing a portion of that value, with around 20% as a possible starting point. Still, what a buyer will actually pay depends on their targets, their incentives and how they see the risk. The PFIR method gave me a clear way to organise my thinking. First, we state the facts or features we truly have. Then we draw inferences from those facts. Finally, we make recommendations. Keeping these steps separate stops us from presenting assumptions as facts, which is a habit I want to build in my own analysis. The last lesson was that launching an AI product is not the end of the job. Model behaviour can change because of data drift, changes by outside vendors, API updates or new pricing. So monitoring, fallback planning and vendor management must continue for the whole life of the product. I leave this session with a new respect for the work that begins after launch, and with a stronger belief that good product management means staying responsible long after the release date.
