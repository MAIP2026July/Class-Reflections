## My Learning and Reflection

My strongest learning from Class 11 is that **responsible Artificial Intelligence cannot be added as a general statement at the end of a product report**. Fairness, privacy, accountability, transparency and risk controls must be defined for the specific product, customer segment and decision. Saying “the product will be fair and protect privacy” is not enough unless the team explains what fairness and privacy mean in that workflow.

The professor’s example of a young child associating every security guard with one community helped me understand how bias can arise without bad intention. The child is reasoning from limited exposure. Artificial Intelligence can behave in the same way when its training data is incomplete or unrepresentative. The examples of image generators showing a man when asked for a pilot and a woman when asked for someone cooking showed how social patterns can become model outputs. My understanding is that awareness of bias is the first step, but the Product Manager must also identify who may receive an unfair advantage or disadvantage and how that effect will be detected.

The credit-card example made fairness more practical. Two people may have similar employment, salary and credit history, but one person may receive approval while the other is rejected because of a hidden pattern in historical data or policy. A Product Manager cannot manually inspect every application, otherwise the benefit of automation disappears. The product therefore needs segment-level monitoring, complaint analysis, disagreement review and an appeal or escalation mechanism.

I found the recruitment examples equally relevant. Requiring an MBA may remove a capable candidate before an interview. Asking for a passport number, marital status, age or other personal details too early may create privacy and bias risks without improving the hiring decision. The blind academic-review example showed a practical control: remove identity information that is not necessary for evaluation. The class made me ask a simple question before collecting any field: **Can we defend why this information is required for the product decision?**

The professor also explained that data belongs to the individual, while the organisation acts as a custodian or trustee. Consent, purpose, retention and deletion should therefore be defined. Collecting more information because it may become useful later is risky. Children’s data, health information and other sensitive attributes need stronger protection. For me, data minimisation is not only a legal requirement; it also reduces the opportunity for misuse and unwanted inference.

The guitar-on-a-motorcycle example was memorable. An Artificial Intelligence traffic system reportedly interpreted a guitar as a pillion rider without a helmet. The mistake sounds humorous, but the same type of failure can result in an incorrect fine, harassment or loss of trust. The example of several mobile phones being moved together to create a false traffic jam showed that Artificial Intelligence can also be deliberately manipulated. Product evaluation must therefore include misuse, abuse and adversarial scenarios, not only normal use cases.

The discussion on hallucination made the product boundary clear. A language model may invent an organisational policy or cite a court case that does not exist. Retrieval-Augmented Generation and approved sources can reduce this risk, but the product should also know when to abstain or escalate. A confident but unsupported answer is more dangerous than an honest statement that reliable information is unavailable.

I also understood that governance should protect stakeholders without becoming theatre or bureaucracy. A committee can recommend, but final accountability still belongs to the Product Manager or authorised decision-maker. Reviewers need enough time, information and recognition to perform meaningful review. If governance consists only of forms and checkboxes, teams may hide problems or treat the process mechanically. Governance should operate at the speed of the product and scale faster when the number and authority of Artificial Intelligence agents increase.

The class connected responsible Artificial Intelligence with launch decisions. No product is completely free from defects, so the Product Manager must define acceptable and unacceptable risk using severity, likelihood, detectability and reversibility. The possible decision may be full launch, limited rollout, additional human review, pause or no-go. The launch checklist must be specific to the product rather than copied from a generic template.

## How I Can Apply This Learning

For RiskReview AI, I will define exactly what project data is collected, why each field is required, who can access it, how long it is retained and how it can be deleted. The product should not infer sensitive employee characteristics or use writing style as a proxy for competence. Findings should preserve the original risk statement, evidence, model or rule version, human decision and final change.

The product should abstain when project context is insufficient and escalate uncertain or consequential findings to the Project Manager. Fabricated project facts, changed risk meaning, unauthorised access and incorrect automatic closure should be launch blockers. The governance checklist should cover realistic misuse and failure cases, not only broad statements about fairness and privacy.

## My Main Takeaway

My main takeaway is that responsible Artificial Intelligence is an operational Product Management responsibility. The Product Manager must understand who can be harmed, minimise unnecessary data, provide review and appeal mechanisms, prepare for incidents and decide how much risk is acceptable before launch. Ethics becomes meaningful only when it changes the product design and decision.
