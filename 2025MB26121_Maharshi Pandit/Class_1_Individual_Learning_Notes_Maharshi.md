## My Learning and Reflection

The first class changed my basic understanding of product management. Earlier, I viewed a Product Manager mainly as the person who connects business requirements with the development team and coordinates the product lifecycle. After the discussion, I understood that the role carries much stronger ownership. The professor described the Product Manager as the **CEO of the product**. For me, this means owning the problem and the outcome, even when many activities are performed by engineering, marketing, sales, operations, finance, legal, or external partners.

The cricket-captain example made this responsibility easy to understand. A captain cannot claim leadership only when the team wins and avoid responsibility when the team loses. Similarly, a Product Manager cannot blame engineering, marketing, or another department when the product fails. At the same time, product success is also a team result. The Product Manager is accountable for the overall outcome but should remain honest that many people contribute to success or failure.

Another useful example was the decision to reject an exotic database even though the technology was technically better. The professor selected the second-best option because people with the required skills were easier to find and the organisation would not become dependent on one specialist. I understood that product decisions are not always about choosing the technically best option. The Product Manager must consider talent availability, maintenance, continuity, cost, risk, and the ability to scale. A practical and supportable choice may be better than an advanced but difficult-to-operate choice.

I also learned that product management requires continuous trade-offs. Time, cost, people, talent, market opportunity, customer value, and organisational priorities are limited. Choosing one feature usually means delaying another. There may not be one universally correct answer because the decision depends on assumptions about customers, competition, technology, and the future. The Product Manager must make the assumptions clear, take the decision, and remain accountable for the result.

The class also clarified the three ways Artificial Intelligence is connected with product management. Artificial Intelligence can be used to **build a product**, to **support Product Management work and artifacts**, and to **be embedded inside the product for customers to use**. I had previously mixed these three uses together. I now understand that each use has different requirements, costs, risks, and responsibilities.

I found the discussion on personal learning notes important. The professor clearly stated that the notes should not be a summary of the recording. The notes should capture what I can use, what I cannot use, where I disagree, and what questions remain. Artificial Intelligence tools are allowed, but their use and prompts should be disclosed. My understanding is that Artificial Intelligence can help organise my thoughts, but it should not replace my own reflection.

The discussion about product retirement was also new to me. Product ownership includes launching new versions, sunsetting old versions, and sometimes deciding that a product is no longer viable. This means the Product Manager owns the full lifecycle, not only ideation and development.

## How I Can Apply This Learning

In future product work, I will begin by defining the problem, customer value, business outcome, and important assumptions. I will evaluate technical choices together with supportability, skills, cost, and long-term dependency. I will also check whether the product is achieving the original business Key Performance Indicators after launch, instead of treating deployment as completion.

## My Main Takeaway

My strongest takeaway is that a Product Manager owns the **problem, product decisions, trade-offs, and outcome**. The role may involve responsibility without complete authority, but influence, judgement, transparency, resilience, and technical fluency are part of the job. Product management is demanding, but it is also rewarding because the Product Manager can create something useful that continues to deliver value for customers and the organisation.
