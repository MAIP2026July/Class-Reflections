## My Learning and Reflection

The strongest learning for me from Class 2 was that a product lifecycle is not a fixed sequence of activities. It is a **managed learning loop**. We discover a problem, define it, plan, build, launch and grow, but at every stage new evidence can change the product. Sometimes the correct decision may even be to renew, replace or retire it. This made me realise that a Product Manager should not become emotionally attached to the original idea. The responsibility is to protect customer value, not to protect the first version of the solution.

The example of constructing a house helped me understand how decisions should be timed. The structure and number of rooms must be decided early because changing them later is costly. The paint colour can be decided much later. I connected this with product development: important architectural choices and stakeholder goals require early alignment, while reversible details should be deferred until better information is available. However, deferring a decision should be strategic, not an excuse to avoid resolving a major disagreement.

Another important point was that a Product Manager is a **trustee of someone else’s money, time and resources**. Therefore, “I felt this was a good idea” is not enough. Decisions should have evidence, assumptions, ownership and expected outcomes. This is why artifacts such as the Product Requirements Document, user stories, acceptance criteria, roadmap, experiments and decision records are useful. I previously viewed such documents mainly as formal documentation. I now see them as a way to preserve reasoning and ensure that a decision is carried consistently into design, engineering, testing, launch and support.

The traceability discussion was practical for me. A team may decide to add a feature or change a price, but if that decision is not reflected in the architecture, software, testing, customer communication and billing, the decision has no real impact. A traceability matrix can reveal where the decision was dropped. This is especially important in Artificial Intelligence products because data, model behaviour, user experience, controls and operating processes must remain aligned.

The class also clarified the difference between a project and a product. A project may begin with one known customer, but a product should generalise the problem for multiple unnamed customers. The Product Manager must decide **where to play**, which segment to serve, who buys, who uses and what will not be solved. The chocolate example made the buyer-user distinction simple: a child may use or demand the chocolate, while the parent pays. Product design and placement must therefore consider both people.

The credit-card processing example showed me why beta testing can change the product significantly. The initial team focused on authorisation but later realised that settlement was necessary to deliver complete stakeholder value. The planned June launch moved to the following February after learning from beta deployments. My learning is that “good enough” for launch is a strategic judgement, not perfection, and beta may represent only half of the total work.

I also understood that traditional Product Management foundations remain necessary for Artificial Intelligence products, but additional responsibilities are added. A Product Manager should distinguish between adding Artificial Intelligence to an existing product and creating an Artificial Intelligence-native product. The Product Requirements Document may need data contracts, expected model behaviour, uncertainty, latency, costs, regulation, feedback loops and acceptable failure boundaries. The Product Manager does not need to be the technical expert, but must be fluent enough to understand specialists and make informed decisions.

The discussion on professional services was also useful. Consultants or implementation partners may configure, integrate and validate a product for a customer, but the Product Manager still owns the product promise. Advice can be outsourced, but accountability cannot be transferred. The tilted-building example made this clear: even when specialist consultants are involved, the product owner remains responsible for the final result.

## How I Can Apply This Learning

For a product such as RiskReview AI, I would first define the specific customer segment and repeated workflow. I would record assumptions, acceptance evidence and major decisions in GitHub. I would separate reversible and irreversible decisions, use prototypes to reduce uncertainty, and ensure that every accepted requirement is traceable across data, Artificial Intelligence behaviour, interface, testing and governance.

## My Main Takeaway

My main takeaway is that Product Management is disciplined learning under uncertainty. The Product Manager must decide what to build, what not to build, what can wait, what evidence is required and when the product is sufficiently ready. Frameworks can support these decisions, but they cannot replace judgement and accountability.
