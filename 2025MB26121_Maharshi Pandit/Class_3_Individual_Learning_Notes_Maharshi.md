## My Learning and Reflection

The central question I took from Class 3 was: **Where does Artificial Intelligence genuinely create product value, and where are we only adding Artificial Intelligence because it is fashionable?** Before this class, I was more likely to begin with the capability of the technology. The class made me start from the user’s job, compare the present and proposed workflow, and then decide whether Artificial Intelligence is actually necessary.

The example of a **nuclear-powered pencil sharpener** made this point memorable. It may be technically possible, but it is expensive, risky and unnecessary when a simple sharpener already completes the job. My understanding is that the Product Manager should first test deterministic rules, search, analytics or simple automation. Predictive Machine Learning, Generative Artificial Intelligence or an agent should be introduced only when the simpler option cannot deliver the required outcome.

The multilingual agricultural-advisory example also helped me understand that technical correctness alone is not sufficient. Advice to a farmer must consider crop stage, location, weather, dialect, literacy, affordability and the time available to act. A recommendation may be scientifically correct but commercially useless if the farmer cannot afford it or if the warning arrives after the decision window has closed. The rain example made the **actionable time window** very clear: a commuter may need a warning in the morning, while a farmer may need it two days earlier to arrange labour or protect the crop.

I also understood that product evaluation should begin with **social legitimacy**. Before checking model quality, the Product Manager should ask whether the product has consent, affects vulnerable groups, creates manipulation or hidden surveillance, or may cause material harm. The class reminded me that a low-probability error can still be unacceptable when the consequence is very large. Average performance can hide serious tail risk.

The discussion on Human-in-the-Loop was practical. The loan example showed that Artificial Intelligence can provide a recommendation, but disagreement between the model and the human decision can trigger an explanation or escalation. I do not interpret Human-in-the-Loop as adding a person only for formality. The Product Manager must define who retains judgement, when review is required, what evidence is shown and who carries accountability.

The customer-adoption discussion also changed my thinking. A car delivering 100 kilometres per litre may sound attractive, but a customer may still consider purchase price, reliability, service availability, switching cost and resale. Some customers will try an unknown product early, while others will wait for evidence. The Tata Nano example showed that even a technically useful product may fail when positioning ignores social meaning. Therefore, value is not defined by the product team alone. It depends on the customer segment and the customer’s complete experience.

I found the discussion on bias and evaluation rubrics useful. Artificial Intelligence and subject-matter experts can both be biased. A common rubric with clearly defined criteria can reduce subjectivity and make disagreement visible, but it cannot eliminate judgement. The final decision still needs human reasoning, documented trade-offs and a practical response time.

Another important learning was the need for a **pre-Artificial-Intelligence and post-Artificial-Intelligence baseline**. If the team claims that Artificial Intelligence adds value, the team should measure what changed for a named customer segment. The evidence may include time saved, improved decision quality, lower cost, reduced errors or better access. Without the baseline, the product may only be an impressive demonstration.

## How I Can Apply This Learning

For RiskReview AI, I will not begin by claiming that Generative Artificial Intelligence is necessary. Missing owners, dates and mandatory fields can be checked through deterministic rules. Semantic similarity may help suggest related risks, while Generative Artificial Intelligence may draft challenge questions. Project Managers and authorised risk owners should retain decisions on probability, impact, mitigation, escalation, acceptance and closure.

I will also define the exact industry segment, user, monthly review workflow, before-and-after measures, harmful failure cases and escalation path. If a simpler workflow produces the same value more reliably, the team should use the simpler option.

## My Main Takeaway

My main takeaway is that an Artificial Intelligence Product Manager should not defend Artificial Intelligence by default. The Product Manager should defend the **customer outcome**. Artificial Intelligence is justified only when it creates measurable additional value, operates within an actionable time window, respects social legitimacy and keeps consequential decisions under appropriate human control.
