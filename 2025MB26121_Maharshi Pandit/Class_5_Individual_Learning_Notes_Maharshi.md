## My Learning and Reflection

My main learning from this class is that an Artificial Intelligence opportunity should begin with the customer’s workflow, not with a new feature or model. Customers do not pay more because the product contains Artificial Intelligence. They pay when the product helps them complete an important job faster, more safely, at lower cost, or with better decisions.

The **double-bearing ceiling-fan advertisement** was a simple but strong example. Double bearings were already normal, but the feature was presented as something unique. I connected this with today’s Artificial Intelligence marketing. A company may label ordinary automation as Artificial Intelligence, but that label will not create lasting value. If the customer does not experience a meaningful improvement, the product may gain one sale but lose trust later.

The professor’s point that customers should “finish their job and get out” also changed my thinking. A business may want more screen time, but the customer normally wants to complete the task. Even in Netflix or Prime Video, the customer is not staying for the application itself; the customer wants entertainment. The Google homepage example showed that a product can deliberately avoid using every available advertising opportunity when simplicity and customer trust are more important.

I also understood that frequency and materiality matter. The intranet example involved a screen for changing the organisation structure, but that change occurred only once in several years. Building and maintaining a complete interface was therefore unnecessary. The better decision was a controlled backend update when required. This taught me not to automate an activity only because automation is possible. The pain must be frequent and important enough to justify the cost.

Opportunity and feasibility must be considered together. The example of rare minerals on the Moon made this clear: the opportunity may be valuable, but it is not useful if the organisation cannot obtain the value at an acceptable cost, quality and time. The same applies to Artificial Intelligence. A model may be technically impressive, but latency, data quality, human support, regulation and operating cost can make the product impractical.

The **build, buy or Application Programming Interface** discussion was useful for my assignment. The Product Manager should decide which capability creates competitive advantage and therefore deserves ownership. An external provider may accelerate development, but it also creates dependency on pricing, availability, model quality and future policy. Open-weight models reduce some dependence, but hardware, support and governance still cost money. The important decision is ownership of the valuable capability, not ownership of every model.

The class also clarified the four layers of an Artificial Intelligence product: **data, model, Application Programming Interface and user experience**. I found the user-experience layer especially important. A legal disclaimer saying that the answer may be wrong is not sufficient. The interface should communicate confidence, provide evidence, ask a clarifying question, refer uncertain cases to a human, or admit that there is no reliable answer.

The Google Maps estimated-arrival-time example helped me understand expectation setting. The estimate can change with traffic, but users accept the change because the interface updates the prediction and the reason is understandable. An Artificial Intelligence product should similarly communicate uncertainty rather than speak with false confidence.

The Human-in-the-Loop discussion also showed that adding a human does not automatically make the product safe. If the reviewer receives too many recommendations, the reviewer may approve everything without applying judgement. In the professor’s lending example, the person and Artificial Intelligence decided separately. Only disagreement triggered explanation or escalation. This design reduced unnecessary review while making human judgement meaningful.

## How I Can Apply This Learning

For RiskReview AI, the customer job is not “use Artificial Intelligence.” The job is to prepare a dependable project-risk review. Rules can check missing owners, dates and mandatory fields. Semantic analysis can suggest vague or related risks. Generative Artificial Intelligence can draft challenge questions. The Project Manager must retain decisions on probability, impact, mitigation, escalation, acceptance and closure.

The product should show the original risk, reason for the flag, supporting evidence and confidence. It should allow accept, edit, reject, merge or defer. I will also compare manual review time and quality with the assisted workflow so that the team can prove whether Artificial Intelligence adds value.

## My Main Takeaway

My main takeaway is that Artificial Intelligence is valuable only when it improves a real customer workflow and remains economically, technically and operationally feasible. The Product Manager must protect customer value, choose the correct sourcing model, design for uncertainty and ensure that Human-in-the-Loop is meaningful rather than ceremonial.
