## My Learning and Reflection

My strongest learning from this class is that an Artificial Intelligence Product Manager must learn to **reimagine the workflow**, not simply automate the current steps. Existing processes were often designed around old limitations. If technology changes but the process remains unchanged, the team may only make an inefficient workflow slightly faster.

The cooking example made this idea easy for me to understand. The professor described struggling to cook every day until a friend suggested boiling dal and preparing ingredients during the weekend, storing them in the refrigerator and completing the final cooking quickly. The ingredients and desired output remained almost the same, but the internal process changed. I connected this with product design: some work can be prepared earlier, standardised or performed once at scale instead of being repeated by every user.

The print-sign-scan example showed the same problem in an organisation. One person printed a document, signed it, scanned it and emailed it. The next person repeated the same steps. The better solution was not Artificial Intelligence; it was redesigning approval through email or a digital workflow. This reminded me that reimagination can lead to rules, process change or simple automation. Artificial Intelligence should be used only when it provides additional value.

I also understood the importance of converting a vague business problem into a clear Artificial Intelligence task. The Product Manager should work backwards from the required outcome and examine the inputs, the internal decision process and the expected output. The black-box example was useful: we may keep the external output unchanged but redesign the internal process, reduce unnecessary inputs or add new data that improves quality and speed.

However, the target must represent the real business need. A demand-forecasting model may achieve low technical error, but the business problem is whether the correct quantity is available at the correct place and time. Similarly, giving marks to a student is different from measuring whether the student can apply the learning in a future role. An Artificial Intelligence system can optimise the wrong target very efficiently if the Product Manager defines the problem badly.

The class also made data responsibility more practical for me. Missing, stale, partial or biased data can create confident but unsafe decisions. The blood-sugar example showed selection bias: recording readings only on days when the value is likely to look good creates misleading data. Data leakage may make a model appear strong during testing while using information that would not be available at the actual decision time.

I found the discussion on consent and governance important. Organisations are custodians of customer data, not unrestricted owners. The professor linked this with the **Digital Personal Data Protection Act in India** and the **General Data Protection Regulation in Europe**. The example of receiving insurance-renewal calls or coaching advertisements without knowingly sharing the information showed how easily data can move beyond its original purpose. A Product Manager should ask whether the organisation has consent, whether the new use matches the original purpose, how long the data is retained and whether deletion can be completed when requested.

The iPhone-review example changed my understanding of sentiment analysis. One review can praise the screen and voice quality, criticise battery life and price, and also reveal a social signal such as the importance of discussing an expensive purchase with family. Therefore, classifying the complete review as positive or negative is not enough. Product decisions require the object, feature, sentiment, opinion holder, time and context. Comparative opinions, synonyms, reference words and fake or paid reviews add further difficulty.

The professor’s point about hidden human meaning was valuable. A comment such as “my mother was upset because I did not tell her before buying the phone” is not a normal product feature, but it signals that the purchase is important, expensive or socially significant. A Product Manager who studies only feature counts may miss this insight.

## How I Can Apply This Learning

For RiskReview AI, I will first map the present monthly risk-review workflow and remove unnecessary steps before adding Artificial Intelligence. Rules can check missing owners, dates and required fields. Semantic analysis can identify weak cause-event-impact structure or related risks. Generative Artificial Intelligence can draft challenge questions, but the Project Manager should control probability, impact, mitigation, escalation, acceptance and closure.

The product should use only approved project data, preserve the original risk text and record every human correction. Evaluation should measure whether review preparation becomes faster and more useful, not only whether the model produces many findings. A high number of flags may indicate noise rather than value.

## My Main Takeaway

My main takeaway is that Artificial Intelligence product value depends on correct problem framing, workflow redesign, suitable data and responsible interpretation. The Product Manager must ensure that the system optimises the real customer outcome rather than a convenient technical target. Customer language, data and model output are signals for judgement, not automatic truth.
