# Management of AI Products: Class 1

This session set up what product management means and how AI changes it. The central idea was that a PM should be seen as the owner of a product's outcomes, not just a link between business and engineering teams. From there the discussion covered how that ownership works in practice, with limited authority, constant trade-offs and real emotional load. It also introduced a three-part lens for the rest of the course: using AI to build products, using AI to do PM work, and embedding AI inside the product.

## Core Concepts

- **Ownership over coordination:** The PM is accountable for the problem and the result, whether or not they control the people or functions involved. Blame cannot be passed to engineering, marketing or anyone else.
- **Decision-making under assumptions:** The role is mostly trade-offs across time, cost and talent, and each one rests on assumptions about the future. The best technical option is not always the best product choice.
- **Full lifecycle responsibility:** This covers launch, adoption, evolution, sunsetting old versions and retiring the product when it no longer makes sense.
- **Three pillars of AI in product work:** AI to build, AI to manage, and AI embedded in the product. Each changes the PM's responsibilities differently, and the third is where AI product management becomes its own specialization.

## Reflections and Insights

- **How you describe the role shapes how you act in it.** Describing the PM as a bridge or coordinator encourages waiting for decisions. Describing the PM as an owner encourages finding a way, including going external when internal teams fall short.
- **Accountability without authority is the defining tension.** No framework removes it. What works is influence, evidence, transparency and credibility, plus recognizing that organizations that freeze on decisions or tie a PM's hands are hurting their own results.
- **"Done" is harder to define for AI products.** Traditional products have frameworks for deciding when something is sufficiently complete. AI outputs are probabilistic, so quality is a spectrum and no established framework exists yet. This is an open problem and not a gap in the learner.
- **Durability is a new question.** AI products depend on models and vendors that change every few months, so owning an outcome means designing for replaceability from the start.
- **Resilience is part of the skill set.** Saying no, absorbing ambiguity and delivering bad news come with the job. Whether the work reads as a thankless grind or as visible progress is partly a mindset choice, and it affects how long someone can do it well.
- **Technical fluency is a precondition.** A PM who can't judge what is being built can't make credible trade-offs, and with AI the limits and costs of the model directly constrain what can be promised to customers.
