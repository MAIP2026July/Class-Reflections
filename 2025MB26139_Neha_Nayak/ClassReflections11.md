# Management of AI Products: Class 11

## Introduction
This session looked at how to build AI products ethically, responsibly and with the risks understood. It covered where bias comes from, how privacy and data choices shape a product, and the particular risks of generative AI. It then turned to the governance and risk-management practices a product manager needs before and after launch. The recurring theme was that product managers stay accountable for AI outcomes even when they cannot see inside the data or models they depend on.

## Core Concepts
- **Bias and fairness:** Bias can enter through the data, the labeling, the training set, organizational policies and workflows. It is often unintentional, and awareness is the first step. Fairness has no universal standard, so teams should meet the minimum set by law and industry guidelines and then go beyond it, treating this as ongoing work. Traditional AI lets you audit your own data, while with generative AI you depend on vendors or open-weight models whose training data you cannot see.
- **Privacy and data governance:** Collect only the data you can defend needing, with clear consent and a stated purpose. Define retention limits and give extra care to sensitive categories such as children's and health data. People own their data and companies are only trustees, which matches the logic of laws like DPDP and GDPR. Minimizing data also reduces bias, because attributes that are never collected cannot be used or inferred.
- **Generative AI risks:** Hallucination, invented policies or facts, adversarial data poisoning, and weak explainability all need explicit controls. Grounding with retrieval (RAG), limiting the options a model can choose from, and sharing only need-to-know information all reduce risk. A well-designed product also knows when to answer, when to abstain and when to escalate to a human.
- **Responsible AI operations and risk assessment:** This covers human-in-the-loop review (including a shadow review workflow), visible user controls, auditability, incident response and clear communication when something goes wrong. Risk is assessed on severity, likelihood, detectability and reversibility, and each risk is mitigated, transferred or accepted. Launch decisions use limited rollouts and a launch-gate checklist tailored to the product, with both minimum criteria and launch blockers.

## Reflections and Insights
- **Bias comes from limited exposure, not bad intent.** A system's view of the world is only as broad as what it has seen, so an AI trained on narrow data makes the same kind of confident, skewed generalizations a young child does. The fix is not a one-time check but continuous questioning of "who is advantaged or harmed?"
- **Generic principles are not enough.** "Ensure fairness and privacy" means nothing until it is defined for a specific product, customer base and context. Fairness, privacy and interpretability each need a concrete definition, threshold and checklist item.
- **Data is tempting, and restraint is a leadership call.** The urge to collect more is constant, and the product manager has to accept the opportunity cost of not collecting it. Whatever is collected should be defensible in front of a regulator.
- **Abstaining can be a feature.** An AI that declines to answer is safer than one that confidently invents an answer, and it also makes commercial sense, since you can avoid charging for an answer you didn't give.
- **Governance can become a bottleneck or a theater.** Review boards that nobody can justify, or that rely on unpaid extra effort from busy experts, tend to get bypassed. Governance should be lean and should scale faster than the number of AI agents it oversees. Committees can recommend, but the product owner remains accountable.
- **A product is a promise, and it carries a PR risk.** One biased outcome can undo years of work once it spreads on social media. Clear, proactive communication during an incident prevents fear from growing.
- **Match the AI type to the context.** Physics- and math-based AI, such as metering or predictive maintenance, carries little fairness risk compared with AI that makes decisions about people. Knowing whether a feature uses traditional or generative AI shapes which risks matter.
- **Judgment under uncertainty is the real skill.** Management is not an exact science, and product managers add value by making justified decisions with incomplete information. They remain answerable to the stakeholders whose money and trust they hold.
