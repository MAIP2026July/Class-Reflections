# Managing AI Products: Class 2

The session looked at how a product manager keeps a product on track when customers, competitors, and technology are all changing at once. It stressed accountability as stewardship of other people's resources, and showed how AI is eroding traditional moats like domain knowledge and build capability. A broad toolkit of product frameworks was surveyed, always with the reminder that they support judgment and never replace it.

## Core Concepts

- **Frameworks as decision aids:** Stacking frameworks that ask the same questions adds ceremony without changing decisions. Pick what fits the situation and keep the final call with the product manager.
- **Moving target and course correction:** The product, the customer, and the competition all keep shifting, so confidence is only valid as of a date, and re-planning is a core part of the job.
- **Product vs. project thinking:** A product serves many unnamed customers through layering: a generic core, then industry variants, then customer-specific customization kept separate. Jobs-to-be-done puts the progress a customer seeks ahead of features and personas.
- **Managing risk and reversibility:** Sprints work as a protected window of stability whose length depends on how dynamic the environment is. Two-way decisions can be delegated and made fast, while one-way decisions need more scrutiny. Pricing should be anchored in customer value, with the provider taking only a modest share.

## Reflections and Insights

- **Incentives shape honesty.** If every improvement raises the next target, rational teams hold gains back. Leaders get real effort only when they don't penalize it.
- **"Transformation" should mean a real leap in value.** Relabelling incremental gains may help win funding, but it shouldn't mislead the team about where the product stands.
- **AI shifts the competitive moat.** When customers can build their own version cheaply, advantage has to come from somewhere else, such as trust, data, speed, or continuity. This calls for forward-looking competitive analysis.
- **Precise language is a product skill.** Terms like "user" and "customer" hide very different needs. Naming the segment and its context leads to better, more inclusive design, including for older users who are often left behind.
- **Technology is a means, not a belief.** Architectural choices are judged by fit and economics. Teams need protected time to explore, but experiments should tie to goals, with clear conditions for adopting or rejecting an idea.
- **Compliance is non-negotiable.** Pressure to cut regulatory corners is a test of judgment, and a product that gets banned creates no value.
- **Leadership means shielding the team.** Products outlast projects, so trust with the team compounds across versions. Rigid policies and hours-tracking cultures work against building products.
- **Cheap evidence trails protect decisions.** A lightweight record of why you acted or chose not to act keeps the product manager honest as a custodian of others' resources.
- **Shutdowns can be done gracefully.** Ending a product that no longer makes sense, without burning goodwill, preserves the reputation needed for the next one.
