# Managing AI Products: Class 2

This session continued a course on managing AI products. It walked through the full product life cycle, from discovering a problem to launch, general availability, growth, and eventually renewing or retiring the product. It covered the documents and evidence a product manager should keep, the common decision frameworks, and how AI changes each stage. It also covered the professional services and consulting roles around a product, and what skills an aspiring AI product manager needs.

## Core Concepts

- **Product life cycle as a learning loop:** Discover, define, strategize, plan, build, launch, grow, then renew or retire. It is iterative, not a waterfall. "Good enough" beats perfect, and general availability is the real launch milestone.
- **Decisions and evidence:** The product manager is a trustee of other people's money and resources, so decisions need a record. That means artifacts, traceability across design, engineering and testing, one-way vs. two-way doors, decision owners (RACI/RAPID), and an expiry date for each decision.
- **Frameworks are aids, not the management itself:** Jobs-to-be-done, double diamond, QFD, Porter's Five Forces, prioritization models and roadmaps. Pick the minimum framework that helps a specific decision. Roadmaps should be detailed in the near term and directional further out.
- **What changes with AI:** Teams must distinguish adding AI to an existing product from building an AI-native one. AI-native PRDs add a product thesis, data contracts, a feedback flywheel and regulatory constraints. Evaluation covers the whole product (cost, P95/P99 latency, failure modes), not just the model. Regulation is tightening, and consultants and professional services never transfer the PM's accountability.

## Reflections & Insights

- **Defer decisions strategically, not blindly.** Like building a house, lock in the structural and costly-to-change choices early (stakeholder alignment, goals) and leave the "paint colour" for later. Deferring a misalignment is not strategy.
- **"Good enough" is itself a strategic decision.** Launching too early invites criticism, but waiting for perfect means never reaching general availability. A beta is only about halfway, so pace the team's energy for the second half.
- **The cost of failure has shrunk.** AI-assisted rapid prototypes let one person test ideas with users before building. A vibe-coded prototype is still not production quality, though.
- **AI evidence expires faster.** A consultant's advice or a model evaluation can be outdated before it is used, so the shelf life of a decision is the shorter of the two clocks, not the longer.
- **Accountability can't be outsourced.** Consultants, specialists and services partners can advise and implement, but the product promise, and the responsibility when something goes wrong, stays with the product manager. A tilted apartment tower makes the point: the builder owns the outcome even though specialists were involved.
- **Fluency over expertise.** An AI PM need not be a data scientist. They must understand the terms, the regulations and the trade-offs well enough to translate between experts, and know where to look things up.
