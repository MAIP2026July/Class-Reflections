# Management of AI Products: Class 4

This session explored when and where AI actually creates product value, using a staged "gate" approach: first social legitimacy, then incremental value, then viability, then bounded learning. Illustrative products, such as an agri-advisory tool, a recommendation engine and a claims copilot, were used to test hypotheses, downside risks and who bears the cost of errors. The discussion also covered why users resist adopting new products and how to balance AI judgment against expert judgment. The session closed with how much autonomy AI systems should be given.

## Core Concepts

- **Legitimacy before technology:** Check consent, vulnerable groups and ethical lines before testing technical fit. Something you *can* build is not something you *should* build.
- **Right-size the AI:** Start with deterministic rules, then analytics and search, then predictive ML, and only then generative or agentic AI. Use the simplest approach that does the job, and measure value with pre-AI and post-AI baselines.
- **Actionability and tail risk:** Insight has value only inside a window in which the user can act. Averages hide rare but severe harms, so guardrails should be built around the tails.
- **Adoption, switching cost and accountability:** Users adopt in waves (early adopters first), and adoption is held back by workflow disruption and lock-in. Autonomy should be graduated: suggest, draft, execute with approval, then execute within limits. The product owner stays accountable throughout.

## Reflection and Insights

- **"AI" is a means, not the value claim.** If a simpler automation delivers the outcome, say so. Defending AI for its own sake is dogma, and the same honesty applies to any technology choice, including blockchain.
- **Value is defined by the user, not the builder.** Time saved is not always value. People sometimes prize the very friction you remove, such as human interaction, and positioning that ignores psychology can sink a good product.
- **Subjectivity can be reduced but never eliminated.** Shared rubrics, a merge formula and cross-checks between humans and AI improve consistency, but people and models will still interpret the same criteria differently. Aim for practical, defensible decisions rather than perfect objectivity.
- **Incentives shape behavior, whether human or AI.** Narrow KPIs (for example, "maximize profit") push agents toward harmful shortcuts like rejecting every claim. Design metrics for side effects, because management sets the behavior it later blames.
- **Silent failures are more dangerous than loud ones.** Model quality can degrade without warning, so monitoring, evidence trails and reversible ("two-way door") decisions matter more than raw capability.
- **In regulated contexts, explainability is a requirement.** A tool that works but cannot justify its decisions fails the long-range workflow of an auditable industry, however good its demo looks.
