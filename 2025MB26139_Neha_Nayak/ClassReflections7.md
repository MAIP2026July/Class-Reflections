# Management of AI Products: Class 7

This session used a live case, an AI-driven customer support and outage-management product for electricity distribution companies, to explore how a product manager thinks about AI products. The discussion moved from product reimagination and pricing to market positioning, handling AI uncertainty, and managing the product across its full lifecycle. The recurring theme was that product management is an integrated discipline. Ideas from pricing, design, risk and lifecycle all influence one another.

## Core Concepts
- **Reimagining the product, not just automating it:** Instead of building a better call center, the aim should be to detect problems before customers report them. This means proactive notifications, location-aware outage detection and smart dispatch, so the support line is needed less.
- **Value-based pricing and positioning:** Cost-plus pricing sets a floor, but real pricing comes from the value created for the customer and the share of it you capture. Supplier share typically falls in a modest band (roughly 15–25%, with about 20% as a working assumption). Understanding the buyer's motivations, financials, contracts and decision-makers matters more than generic pricing theory. Pricing models (per seat, per ticket, outcome-based) also carry side effects on future revenue.
- **Designing for AI uncertainty (Facts, Features, Inferences, Recommendations):** Hallucination is not binary. Facts can be grounded in reliable data lookups, inferences need data support, and recommendations should show their chain of evidence. The UI should signal confidence differently for sure, unsure and unknown outputs, with humans in the loop to confirm and refine.
- **Full AI product lifecycle ownership:** Launch is the beginning, not the end. AI products add risks that traditional software lacks: dependence on third-party model providers (buy vs. build vs. subscribe), model behavior drifting after launch, and continuous monitoring and rollback needs. Data stewardship (consent, purpose limitation, deletion), safe retirement, and trust management all remain the product manager's responsibility.

## Reflection and Insights
- **Reframe the problem, then the product:** The strongest idea in the session was shifting from "handle outage calls well" to "make outage calls unnecessary." Reimagining the problem often creates more value than optimizing the existing process.
- **A product's value may sit in a different place than its feature:** A support tool can matter economically because of what it prevents, such as power bought but not delivered, or reputational and political fallout. The buyer's real pain point may not be the one the product visibly addresses.
- **Know which side of the table you sit on:** The product manager is the seller creating an opportunity, not the customer organization deciding what to do. Pricing, positioning and pitches must be argued from that vantage point and grounded in the specific context rather than in generic frameworks.
- **Success can shrink your own revenue:** If a product works too well, usage-based or seat-based revenue can fall. Pricing design has to anticipate this, and land-and-expand or adjacent markets can offset it.
- **AI products never reach a stable "done" state:** Because model behavior and vendor conditions can change after launch, the product manager carries ongoing responsibility. That also means pacing energy and effort for the long post-launch phase rather than treating launch as a finish line.
