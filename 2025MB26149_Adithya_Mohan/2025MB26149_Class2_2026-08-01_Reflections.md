## 1. Class Context

The session treated the product lifecycle as a managed learning loop rather than a waterfall: discover, define, strategise, plan, build, launch, grow, then renew or retire. It also covered how decisions should be made and traced, and how AI changes PM artifacts and responsibilities.

## 2. Key Takeaways

- **Product, not project:** A product generalises a problem for many unnamed customers, even if one known customer triggered it.
- **Defer what is cheap to change:** Structural decisions are made early; reversible ones, like the paint colour of a house, can wait until more is known.
- **One-way vs two-way doors:** Flagging every decision by reversibility tells the team how much evidence and caution it needs.
- **Good enough to launch:** A product is never perfect. The PM must decide when it is good enough for general availability.
- **AI-enabled vs AI-native:** Adding AI to an existing product differs from building a product that was not possible before AI. AI-native work adds a knowledge flywheel, latency (P95/P99), cost and regulatory considerations to the PRD.
- **Professional services still carry the product promise:** Partners can configure and integrate, but the PM owns whether the customer receives the promised value.

## 3. My Reflection as a Product Manager

- **Traceability is about trust, not paperwork.** The idea that a PM spends someone else's money, time and team effort reframed decision logs for me. Recording why a decision was taken, and why something was not done, protects the team when outcomes are questioned later.
- **Reversibility should shape the debate.** Many stakeholder discussions I have seen spend equal effort on every decision. Classifying decisions as one-way or two-way would let me move faster on reversible items and slow down only where it matters.
- **Launch is a checkpoint, not the finish line.** The professor's point about not burning the team's energy before beta resonated with me. The work after a working demo, such as onboarding, support and operational readiness, is often where timelines slip.
- **The professional services point is very real in enterprise products.** When implementation is handled by another team, the customer still judges the product. I see the PM's role as making implementation artifacts and handoffs part of the product, not an afterthought.
- **Where I differ:** A full traceability matrix suits long, regulated cycles. For faster teams, a lightweight decision log linked to the affected documents may give most of the benefit with less overhead.

## 4. What I Will Carry Forward

- Tag key decisions as one-way or two-way before committing.
- Keep a simple decision log with the evidence, owner and date.
- Plan capacity for the work after beta, not just the build.
- Define "good enough" criteria for launch upfront.
- Treat implementation and onboarding artifacts as part of the product scope.

## 5. Closing Reflection

This class shifted my view of the lifecycle from a sequence of phases to a loop of decisions and learning. My main takeaway is that the quality of a product depends heavily on how well its decisions are timed, recorded and revisited.
