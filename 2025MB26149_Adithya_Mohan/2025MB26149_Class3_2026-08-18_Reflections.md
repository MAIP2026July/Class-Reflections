## 1. Class Context

The session opened with a recap of earlier learnings and then focused on how PMs should use frameworks, define their users and manage products in a constantly changing environment. It closed with sprint discipline, value-based pricing and project guidelines.

## 2. Key Takeaways

- **Progress over features:** Adding features is not progress unless the stakeholder actually uses them and moves closer to the agreed outcome.
- **Never say just "user":** Qualify who it is, under what conditions and at what time, so the PM is never the source of confusion.
- **Frameworks are dials, not the steering wheel:** JTBD, PESTLE, Porter's Five Forces and others support decisions. Stacking frameworks that do not change the decision or evidence becomes ceremony.
- **The product is a moving target:** Customers, technology and competitors keep evolving, so continuous course correction is expected.
- **Scrum as a bargain:** No change inside a sprint, anything can change after it. Sprint length is a negotiation with the team.
- **Fair value share:** A product can typically capture around 20% of the value it creates. The customer keeps the majority.
- **Protect the core product:** Heavy single-customer customisation should be handled separately, so the product does not turn into a project.

## 3. My Reflection as a Product Manager

- **Qualifying the user exposed a real gap.** In healthcare products, the same word "user" can mean a clinician, a scheduler, a client administrator or an executive. Each has different goals and timing. I now see that vague user language can quietly create misaligned requirements.
- **Frameworks should earn their place.** I have seen documents filled with frameworks that never changed a decision. The test the professor gave, whether a framework changes the decision or the evidence, is a simple filter I want to apply.
- **Customisation pressure is constant in enterprise products.** Client-specific asks are often urgent and well funded. The idea of separating truly unique work from the core product, and pricing it accordingly, gives me a clearer way to say no without damaging the relationship.
- **Measurement shapes behaviour.** The point that teams hold back improvements if every gain becomes next year's baseline made me think about how targets are set for product and delivery teams.
- **Where I differ:** The 20% value rule assumes value is measurable. In healthcare operations, value often appears later and is shared with other initiatives, so I see it as a ceiling for sanity checks rather than a pricing formula.

## 4. What I Will Carry Forward

- Replace every generic "user" in requirements with a named, qualified stakeholder.
- Use a framework only when it changes the decision or the evidence.
- Separate client-specific customisation from the core roadmap.
- Respect sprint commitments and negotiate changes at sprint boundaries.
- Sense-check pricing and business cases against the value created for the customer.

## 5. Closing Reflection

The strongest lesson for me was that clarity is a PM's core responsibility. Whether it is the user, the framework or the sprint commitment, ambiguity created by the PM becomes cost for everyone else.
