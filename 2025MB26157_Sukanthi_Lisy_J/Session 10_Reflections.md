# Personal Reflection – Session 10

### 1. Scaling an AI Product Is More Than Increasing Users

One of my key takeaways from this session is that scaling an AI product is not simply about accommodating more users or requests. It also involves managing data availability, model performance, workflow complexity, infrastructure costs, and customer expectations. I particularly agree with the point that growth should be controlled and predictable. Rapid adoption may look like success, but if the product cannot consistently deliver its intended value, growth can quickly become a liability.

I would apply this thinking by treating scalability as part of product strategy from the beginning rather than something to address after the product becomes successful.

### 2. A Product Is Ultimately a Promise to the Customer

The discussion around customer promises was particularly interesting to me. Customers do not care how many servers, models, or technical components operate behind a product. They care whether the product delivers what they expect.

I agree that product managers should focus on the actual customer experience rather than just technical performance metrics. A system may be technically available but still fail its customers if the output is inaccurate, delayed, or unreliable.

One aspect I would emphasize further is that customer promises should also include transparency about AI limitations. We should not create expectations that an AI product cannot consistently meet. Building trust requires setting realistic expectations and delivering against them.

### 3. Balancing AI Quality, Latency and Cost

I found the discussion on the trade-off between quality, speed, and cost particularly relevant. A more powerful model does not automatically make it the right choice for every use case. Similarly, choosing a cheaper and faster model at the expense of output quality can damage the product experience.

My takeaway is that product managers need to understand the economics of AI rather than leaving these decisions entirely to engineering teams. Smaller models may be sufficient for routine tasks, while complex tasks may justify using more capable models.

For my AI product development work, I would explore selective model routing based on task complexity. This could help maintain output quality while controlling operational costs. I also agree that these decisions should be based on actual customer expectations and experimentation rather than assumptions.

### 4. The Importance of Predictability in User Experience

The professor's example of customers preferring predictable processing times over inconsistent response times made me reconsider how I evaluate user experience. Traditionally, I would have considered faster response time an obvious advantage. However, predictable performance can sometimes create greater customer confidence than inconsistent speed.

I believe this is especially relevant for AI products because processing times and output quality can vary depending on the complexity of requests. Instead of focusing exclusively on reducing latency, I would prioritize communicating progress, setting realistic expectations, and delivering consistent experiences.

### 5. Product Managers Must Own the Trade-off Decisions

Another important learning is that product managers cannot simply delegate decisions about quality, cost, and performance to technical teams. Engineering teams may optimize for speed or accuracy, but these decisions must remain aligned with the product's business objectives.

I particularly agree with the idea of defining acceptable operating boundaries and escalation points. This gives teams the freedom to make everyday decisions while ensuring that important trade-offs remain controlled.

I would use this approach to establish clear decision-making guidelines, measurable thresholds, and accountability across product and engineering teams.

### 6. Monitoring, Versioning and Operational Ownership

I found the discussion on versioning particularly valuable. Versioning should not be treated as technical housekeeping. Changes in models, data, or customer segments can affect product performance and customer experience.

I had not previously considered how important it is to retain the ability to route different customer segments to different model versions when a newer model performs poorly for certain users.

I would also give importance to monitoring business outcomes, not just technical indicators. Knowing when model performance improves or deteriorates, understanding why it happens, and having the ability to roll back are essential responsibilities in managing AI products.

### 7. Governance Must Grow Along With AI Adoption

The point that governance must scale faster than the number of AI agents was something I found particularly relevant. As products become more dependent on AI agents, the potential for unexpected outcomes also increases.

I believe governance should not become an obstacle to innovation, but it should provide the necessary controls to ensure reliability, accountability, and responsible usage. Monitoring, alerts, safety guardrails, and human oversight should be considered based on the product's risk and business impact.

I would be particularly cautious about introducing extensive automation before the product has demonstrated that it consistently meets customer expectations.

### 8. AI Product Economics and Business Viability

The discussion on token economics reinforced an important point for me: a successful AI demonstration does not necessarily translate into a viable business. A product may perform exceptionally well during a demonstration but become expensive to operate when usage increases.

I agree that product managers must understand the relationship between model selection, token consumption, pricing, customer usage, and profitability. I would also evaluate whether caching, routing, and smaller models can reduce costs without compromising customer value.

One additional consideration is that cost optimization should never become the primary objective at the expense of trust and product quality. The objective should be sustainable profitability while consistently delivering customer value.

### 9. Building Sustainable Teams Rather Than Depending on Heroics

I found the distinction between people who perform well during intense short-term efforts and those who deliver consistently over longer periods quite insightful. Product development, particularly AI product development, is a continuous journey rather than a one-time delivery exercise.

I agree that product managers need to understand individual strengths, working styles, and capabilities instead of applying uniform expectations to everyone. Depending entirely on individual heroics may help meet immediate deadlines, but it is not a sustainable operating model.

I would focus on building balanced teams, establishing a regular working cadence, and distributing responsibilities in a way that supports long-term execution.

### 10. My Overall Takeaway

The biggest shift in my thinking from this session is that managing an AI product does not end with building a model that works. The real challenge is building a product that can deliver consistent value, scale economically, maintain customer trust, and remain operationally sustainable.

I particularly take away the importance of balancing customer expectations, technical capabilities, and business economics. As a product manager, I should be able to challenge both business and engineering decisions when they move the product away from its intended promise.

I would summarize my learning as follows: **AI capability may help build a product, but disciplined product management is what makes it scalable, trustworthy, and commercially viable.**
