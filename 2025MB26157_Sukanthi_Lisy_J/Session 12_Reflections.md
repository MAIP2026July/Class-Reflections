# Personal Reflection – Session 12

### 1. A Good Product Specification Must Remove Ambiguity

One of my key takeaways is that a product manager should not leave important requirements open to interpretation. Terms such as *accurate, user-friendly, efficient,* or *high risk* may mean different things to different stakeholders. I can apply this by defining measurable acceptance criteria, expected outcomes, and clear boundaries before development begins. This is particularly important when working with engineering teams or external vendors, where unclear requirements can lead to rework, delays, and contractual disagreements.

### 2. Workflows Should Reflect How the Product Actually Operates

The discussion on workflow and swimlane diagrams helped me understand the importance of documenting who does what across the product journey. For AI products, this should also identify where AI is used, where outputs may be uncertain, where human intervention is required, and what fallback options are available. I find this especially useful because a product may look convincing in a prototype but behave very differently in real-world usage. I would use workflow diagrams as practical tools for aligning business, technology, and operational teams rather than treating them as documentation created only for project submissions.

### 3. Customer Value Should Drive Pricing, Not Just Development Cost

The concept of target costing was particularly interesting. Instead of calculating the cost of building a product and adding a profit margin, the better approach is to understand what customers are willing to pay and work backwards to determine the affordable cost structure. This changes how I would approach product planning. A technically strong product does not automatically become a commercially successful product. Its price must reflect the value customers actually receive and remain competitive with alternatives.

### 4. AI Product Economics Require Continuous Attention

The discussion on inference costs and token economics was one of the most practical parts of the lecture. Unlike traditional software, AI products can become more expensive to operate as customer usage increases. A product may gain popularity but become less profitable if the cost of serving heavy users is not understood.

I would therefore evaluate cost per customer, usage patterns, contribution margins, and profitability across customer segments. I also found the discussion on model selection, routing, and caching useful. Using the most powerful model for every request may not be necessary; however, choosing a cheaper model without evaluating its impact on quality, latency, and reliability can create a different problem. The right balance should be based on actual product requirements and usage data.

### 5. Understand What Customers Are Really Paying For

I found the distinction between input-based and output-based pricing particularly valuable. Customers are generally interested in the outcome they receive, not the technical resources consumed by the product. For example, they are buying the value of a solution rather than the underlying API calls or tokens.

This made me reconsider how products should be positioned and monetized. Usage-based, subscription-based, hybrid, or outcome-based pricing should not be selected simply because they are popular models. The choice should reflect customer willingness to pay, usage patterns, perceived value, and the company's ability to deliver consistently. I would also be careful about pricing models that appear profitable initially but create customer dissatisfaction over time.

### 6. Product Management Goes Beyond the PRD

Another point I will take forward is that a PRD alone is not sufficient to manage an AI product. Risk checklists, governance considerations, data privacy requirements, workflows, business plans, and unit economics should also be part of the product management artifacts. I agree with this broader view because AI products introduce technical, operational, financial, and trust-related considerations that cannot be addressed through functional requirements alone.

I also found the emphasis on communicating a product's value proposition within a short presentation relevant. Product managers should be able to explain not just what they are building, but why customers need it, how it will generate value, and whether the business model is sustainable.

### My Overall Takeaway

This lecture helped me connect product management decisions with commercial realities. I previously tended to think of product success largely in terms of solving the customer problem and delivering the expected functionality. I now see that customer value, pricing, cost to serve, scalability, and profitability must be considered from the beginning.

One perspective I would add is that optimizing cost should never become the primary objective at the expense of customer experience. A cheaper model or lower operating cost is useful only when the product continues to meet its quality and reliability expectations. Ultimately, the product manager must make informed trade-offs and be able to justify those decisions to stakeholders.

My biggest learning is that building an AI product is not just a technology exercise; it is the responsibility of creating something customers value, the business can sustain, and the organization can confidently stand behind.
