# Personal Reflection – Session 14

This lecture reinforced that building an AI product requires much more than identifying a problem and developing a technology-based solution. What stood out to me was the professor's emphasis on **ambition, customer value, execution discipline, and commercial viability**.

One key takeaway is that I should think beyond incremental improvements and question whether my product can create meaningful impact. At the same time, ambition must be supported by a realistic execution plan. I found the emphasis on clearly defining Phase 1, Phase 2, and future enhancements particularly useful. Having a long list of features does not necessarily make a product valuable; prioritizing what solves the core problem is what matters.

I also realized how important it is to understand the customer's actual workflow. A product should not merely identify problems or generate recommendations—it should help users take action, reduce effort, and ideally prevent problems from occurring in the first place. The discussion around automation, human intervention, and improving user adoption made me think about designing AI around real user behavior rather than expecting users to adapt to the technology.

The professor's emphasis on a well-defined PRD was another practical lesson. Requirements must be clear enough for someone else to build the product without ambiguity. This is particularly relevant from a management perspective, because unclear requirements can result in poor execution, wasted resources, and financial losses.

I found the discussions on business economics and competition equally valuable. Evaluating a product from both the customer's perspective and the vendor's perspective is essential. Customer savings, willingness to pay, implementation costs, pricing, margins, competition, and the possibility of customers building their own solutions must all be considered. An AI product may work technically, but that alone does not make it commercially sustainable.

I agree with the professor's emphasis on differentiation and market positioning. In a rapidly evolving AI landscape, products that depend only on readily available AI capabilities may find it difficult to sustain a competitive advantage. Domain expertise, workflow integration, execution capabilities, partnerships, and measurable business outcomes may be stronger sources of differentiation.

My personal takeaway is that I need to approach AI product management with a balance of ambition and discipline. I should start with the customer's problem, define measurable value, validate assumptions, prioritize the MVP, evaluate the economics, and continuously question whether the product is genuinely better than existing alternatives. **The ultimate measure of an AI product is not how impressive the technology looks, but whether it creates value that customers recognize, use, and are willing to pay for.**
