# Session 1 - Personal Reflections

The first session of Management of AI Products shifted my perspective from viewing AI primarily as a technology to understanding how a product manager should think and make decisions in an AI-driven environment. What stood out to me was the Professor’s emphasis on starting with the problem, understanding the customer, and focusing on outcomes rather than simply adopting technology.

### 1. Thinking Like the CEO of a Product

One of my biggest takeaways was the idea of a product manager being the CEO of the product. I have traditionally viewed product management as a role that connects business, technology, design, customers, and other stakeholders. However, the discussion made me realize that coordination alone is not enough. A product manager must take ownership of the overall outcome, whether it involves customer adoption, technical execution, or market success.

I want to apply this by asking myself, “Do I own the outcome?” rather than simply focusing on whether I have completed my assigned responsibilities. However, I see ownership as accountability, not control. Effective product management still requires collaboration, listening to different perspectives, and making informed decisions.

### 2. Making the Right Trade-offs

The discussion on choosing a widely supported database over a technically superior but difficult-to-staff alternative challenged my assumption that the best technical solution is always the best product decision.

A technology’s value depends on the broader context, including cost, talent availability, scalability, and long-term maintainability. Sometimes, a less sophisticated solution can deliver a better overall outcome.

However, I do not believe that choosing the second-best option should automatically be the safer decision. Differentiation sometimes requires bold choices. The real lesson for me is to understand the trade-offs, evaluate their implications, and take ownership of the consequences rather than optimize for one dimension alone.

### 3. Using AI Beyond the Product Itself

The Professor’s distinction between using AI to build products, using AI to perform product-management tasks, and embedding AI into products broadened my thinking. I had primarily associated AI with adding intelligent features to existing products, overlooking how it can transform the way product managers work.

I can see immediate applications in market research, customer-feedback analysis, requirements development, prototyping, and exploring alternative solutions. However, AI can accelerate poor thinking just as easily as good thinking. Without a clear understanding of the customer problem, it is possible to develop a polished solution to the wrong problem much faster.

For me, AI should enhance my judgment and productivity, not replace critical thinking.

### 4. AI Is Not the Product Strategy

Another important takeaway was that AI itself is not a product strategy. With technologies such as generative AI, copilots, and agentic systems gaining attention, it is easy to begin with the question, “How can we use AI?” instead of “What problem are we trying to solve?”

Going forward, I want to start with the customer problem, business value, adoption challenges, risks, and measurable outcomes before deciding whether AI is appropriate and which capabilities to use.

I also found the distinction between adding AI to an existing product and building an AI-native product useful. The former involves improving established workflows and experiences, while the latter may create entirely new possibilities. In such cases, traditional customer requirements may not be sufficient because customers cannot always articulate needs they have never experienced. Rapid AI prototyping can help bridge this gap by allowing customers to respond to something tangible.

### 5. Frameworks Should Support Judgment, Not Replace It

I appreciated the Professor’s emphasis that there is no single correct framework for every situation. In an MBA program, it is tempting to collect frameworks because they make our thinking appear structured. However, the real skill lies in knowing which framework to use, when to use it, and when to rely on judgment instead.

I also valued the encouragement to reflect on what we agree with, what we may not find useful, and where we have a different point of view. This approach makes the learning more meaningful because it requires us to challenge ideas and connect them to our own experiences rather than simply summarize the lecture.

### 6. Learning to Navigate the AI Wave

The Professor’s analogy of learning to ride the AI wave rather than being overwhelmed by it stayed with me. I agree that AI is transformative and that understanding it is becoming increasingly important. However, I do not think riding the wave means adopting AI simply because everyone else is doing so.

For me, it means understanding its capabilities and limitations well enough to decide where it adds value, where it does not, and how its risks should be managed.

### My Key Takeaway

My biggest takeaway from the session is not a particular AI tool or framework, but a change in how I want to approach product decisions. I want to start with the problem, understand the customer and desired business outcome, evaluate the evidence and trade-offs, and then determine whether and how AI can help.

To me, that is what differentiates an AI product manager from someone who simply knows how to use AI tools. Technologies and models will continue to evolve, but sound judgment, customer understanding, collaboration, and ownership of outcomes will remain essential.

That is the lesson I hope to carry forward from this course, well beyond the classroom.
