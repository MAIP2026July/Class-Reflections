# Session 3 - Personal Reflections

This session broadened my understanding of product management. I now see it as more than managing requirements, prioritizing features, and coordinating teams. It is about making decisions under uncertainty, taking accountability, and continuously creating measurable customer value.

### 1. Building a Product vs. Solving a Customer Problem

The Professor’s distinction between a product and a customer-specific solution stood out to me. A true product should address a broader need that can serve multiple customers. The concept of layering a generic product core with industry-specific capabilities and customer customization offers a practical way to balance scalability with individual needs.

Going forward, I want to ask: “Can this customer requirement solve a broader problem that others would also pay to solve?” Customer-specific work can still be valuable if it helps uncover a larger market opportunity.

### 2. Customer Progress Over Features

The idea that progress matters more than feature volume challenged my traditional view of product success. Delivering more features does not necessarily mean creating more value.

The Jobs to Be Done framework reinforced the importance of understanding what customers are actually trying to accomplish rather than simply responding to their stated requirements. I want to focus more on customer outcomes than on the number of features delivered.

### 3. Adapting to a Moving Target

The Professor’s analogy of a moving target was particularly relevant to AI products. Customer expectations, competitors, and technology evolve continuously, making rigid roadmaps insufficient.

I agree that product managers must be willing to change direction when evidence changes. However, being adaptive should not mean being reactive. Roadmap changes should be driven by meaningful shifts in customer value, risk, economics, or competitive advantage not every new technology announcement.

### 4. AI and Competitive Advantage

As AI reduces the cost and complexity of development, technical capabilities alone may no longer provide a lasting competitive advantage. Products may need to differentiate through proprietary data, customer experience, workflow integration, trust, and domain expertise.

This made me realize that product managers must continuously reassess what makes their products valuable and difficult to replicate.

### 5. Frameworks and Traceability

I found the Professor’s view of frameworks as instruments rather than an operating system useful. Frameworks should support decisions, not dictate them. I would rather select a few relevant tools than apply multiple frameworks simply to appear thorough.

Similarly, traceability should establish why decisions were made and ensure that the original product intent carries through execution. My takeaway is decision transparency, not documentation for documentation’s sake.

### 6. Accountability and AI Understanding

The distinction between responsibility and accountability was another important lesson. Tasks can be delegated, but the product manager remains accountable for the outcome.

I do not need to be a machine-learning expert, but I must understand enough to question model performance, data quality, limitations, costs, and potential failure scenarios. I may not know every answer, but I need to know the right questions to ask.

### 7. Experimentation and Learning

I appreciated the emphasis on giving teams time to explore and reflect. AI product development requires experimentation, but exploration should have clear goals and success criteria.

My preferred approach would be to run small experiments with defined hypotheses, measurable outcomes, and documented learning. Even an unsuccessful experiment can create value if it helps the team make better subsequent decisions.

### 8. Regulation, Ethics, and Inclusion

The discussion on regulation reinforced that compliance and responsible AI cannot be left until the end of development. I believe involving risk and compliance teams early can help balance innovation with responsible product decisions.

The example of senior citizens struggling with technology also reminded me that efficiency and adoption metrics may overlook people who are excluded by technological change. Good product management should ask not only “Who benefits?” but also “Who might be left behind?”

### 9. Ownership and Product Leadership

The idea of being the captain or CEO of the product was both motivating and demanding. It means owning outcomes rather than hiding behind teams, frameworks, or technology.

However, I see ownership as accountability combined with collaboration. Strong product leadership is not about having every answer; it is about creating clarity, aligning people, and bringing different areas of expertise together toward a common goal.

### My Key Takeaway

This session reinforced a different mindset for product management: customer progress over feature volume, value over activity, evidence over assumptions, adaptability over rigid plans, and accountability over delegation.

My biggest personal takeaway is that an AI product manager cannot simply follow technological trends. I need to understand the customer, question assumptions, experiment thoughtfully, and adapt when the evidence changes.

Ultimately, the question I want to keep asking is: Does this help create meaningful and sustainable value for the people the product is intended to serve? That is the principle I want to carry forward from this session.
