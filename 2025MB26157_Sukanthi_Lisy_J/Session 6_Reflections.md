# Session 6 - Personal Reflections

## 1. Start with the Problem, Not the Technology

My biggest takeaway from this session is that successful AI products begin with a clear customer problem, not the technology itself. With AI gaining popularity, it is easy to focus on where it can be used rather than whether it is actually needed.

As a future manager, I want to understand the customer’s pain points, identify the value of solving them, and evaluate whether AI is the best approach. If a simpler, more cost-effective solution delivers the same outcome, I would prefer that over adding AI unnecessarily.

## 2. Rethinking Existing Workflows

I found the idea of re-imagining workflows particularly useful. Instead of simply automating an existing process, we should question whether every step is necessary and whether the process can be redesigned to deliver better results.

However, change should not be driven by technology alone. Any redesigned process must be practical and reliable. I would start with small-scale experiments, evaluate the results, and expand only when there is clear evidence of improvement.

## 3. Understanding Customer Needs

This session reinforced the importance of looking beyond what customers ask for and understanding what they actually need. A requested feature may not always address the underlying problem.

I would apply this by asking better questions, understanding stakeholder expectations, and focusing on the outcomes customers value most. I also believe an MVP should solve the core problem with minimal complexity. Adding more features before establishing real value can make a product harder to use and reduce adoption.

## 4. Measuring Meaningful Outcomes

An AI product may perform well technically without delivering meaningful business value. This made me realize how important it is to define success before development begins.

Depending on the use case, success could mean lower costs, faster processes, fewer errors, or improved customer satisfaction. I would also look beyond individual metrics. Improving one task may create additional costs or work elsewhere, so the overall impact on the business process matters more than isolated improvements.

## 5. Data Responsibility and Trust

The discussion on data governance, consent, and bias highlighted that responsible AI is essential to building sustainable products. Having access to data does not automatically justify its use, and poor-quality or unrepresentative data can lead to misleading outcomes.

I would therefore consider privacy, security, and bias from the beginning rather than treating them as final-stage checks. I also believe human oversight is important when AI influences significant decisions. A system may sound confident, but that does not guarantee that its output is correct.

## 6. Using Customer Feedback Effectively

I found the discussion on sentiment analysis useful because overall customer ratings do not always explain what needs improvement. Customers may appreciate a product’s performance while being dissatisfied with its price or ease of use.

Analyzing feedback at the feature level can help identify specific opportunities for improvement. I would use AI to organize large volumes of reviews and uncover patterns, but I would not rely on sentiment scores alone. Context and customer preferences still require human judgment.

## 7. My Key Takeaway

This session helped me understand that AI product management requires business judgment, customer understanding, and critical thinking not just technical knowledge.

My main learning is to move away from asking, “Where can I use AI?” and instead ask, “What problem am I solving, what outcome matters, and is AI the right solution?”

Going forward, I want to evaluate AI opportunities based on practical value, feasibility, reliability, and customer impact. Not every problem requires AI, and recognizing when a simpler approach is better is equally important. For me, the real skill lies in distinguishing what technology can do from what a business and its customers genuinely need.
