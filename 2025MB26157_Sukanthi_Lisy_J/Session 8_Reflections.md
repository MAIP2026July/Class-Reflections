# Session 8 - Personal Reflections

## 1. Start with the Problem, Not AI

My key takeaway is that AI should solve a real business problem rather than be the goal itself. Before choosing a technology, I would understand customer needs and assess whether AI is better than simpler alternatives. Product success should be measured by the value delivered, not the amount of AI used.

## 2. Reliability Over Impressive Demos

A successful prototype does not guarantee a reliable product. AI can misinterpret instructions, make incorrect assumptions, or behave inconsistently in real-world situations.

As a product manager, I would test different scenarios, anticipate failures, and establish monitoring and fallback mechanisms before launch. Understanding and managing limitations is more realistic than expecting AI to be perfect.

## 3. Choose the Right Technology

The discussion on generative AI, RAG, and rule-based systems reinforced the importance of matching technology to the problem. AI may be suitable for summarizing information or generating recommendations, while calculations and other predictable operations may require rules or APIs.

I would focus on building the most effective solution rather than forcing AI into every process.

## 4. Validate Before Scaling

I learned that a prototype tests an idea, an MVP validates customer value, and a scalable product must also meet reliability and operational requirements.

I would test the core value proposition before investing heavily in integrations or additional features. Clear success criteria would help determine whether to proceed, improve, or stop.

## 5. Automate Gradually

I believe automation should increase as reliability improves. Initially, AI can support tasks while humans review important decisions. As confidence grows, suitable tasks can be automated further.

Human oversight should have a clear purpose, and products should be transparent about what is automated and what still requires human involvement.

## 6. Maintain Accountability

AI does not remove the organisation’s responsibility for its decisions. In areas such as banking and insurance, errors can have significant consequences.

I would consider risk, explainability, documentation, and human approval from the beginning. Accountability should be part of product design, not an afterthought.

## 7. Design for Flexibility

AI technologies and providers evolve quickly. Where practical, I would avoid unnecessary dependence on a single provider so the product can adapt to changes in cost or performance.

However, flexibility also adds complexity, so I would pursue it only when the business benefits justify the investment.

## 8. Keep Governance Practical

I would define clear objectives and success criteria for each development stage. This helps teams make evidence-based decisions without getting caught in lengthy approval processes.

I also learned that adding features is not always progress. Testing a simple solution with customers may provide more value than continuously expanding its scope.

## 9. My Key Takeaway

This session reinforced that AI product management requires customer focus, business judgment, and accountability alongside technical understanding.

Going forward, I want to validate problems before investing, select technology based on the use case, and rely on evidence rather than excitement. I would rather deliver a simple, reliable product that solves a real problem than promise complete automation without consistent results. Ultimately, the goal is to balance innovation with practical, measurable customer and business value.
