A key takeaway for me from the introduction session is that the role of Product Manager is to take complete ownership of the product he or she is looking after. This is much more than just coordinating between cross functional teams and meeting management expectations or running behind strict deadlines. Responsibility and accountability lie with the PM who deals with several factors outside his or her control. I could relate this directly to my own work in healthcare leadership. Outcomes are influenced by multiple people, processes and systems. A clinical program like Robotic cardiac surgery program may involve cardiac surgeons, cardiologists, intensivists, nursing teams, administrators, finance teams and external vendors. No single person controls all these elements, yet leadership is still expected to take ownership of the overall outcome. 
We also discussed trade-offs. In medicine, we are often forced to choose the “best option” keeping in mind the patient, cost, resources, and infrastructure. There is no one best way. Every clinician learns to make the best decision possible with the information available at that time. Clinical outcomes are evaluated retrospectively. A good decision can still lead to a poor outcome, and vice versa. This is something that I have dealt with several times in our clinical quality discussions. The discussion made me think more about the quality of the decision-making process itself rather than evaluating every decision purely based on the final result.
The 3 important “pillars”:  1. Using AI to build products, 2. Using AI as part of product management work and 3. Embedding AI into products gave me a useful framework. Until now, I had mostly viewed AI as something that can enhance a product. This discussion highlighted how AI can influence the entire product lifecycle, not merely a part of it.
