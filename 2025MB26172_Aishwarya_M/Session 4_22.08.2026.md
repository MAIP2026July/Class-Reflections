We discussed use of AI in product management. The main learning for me from this session was that before applying AI to any product or process, we must first ask whether AI will really be useful for the business context and decision at hand. AI must create measurable value and not be brought in simply because it is the trend now. We are facing a similar wave of AI enthusiasm in health care, where people sometimes assume that introducing AI automatically improves a process. If a problem can be solved through clear rules, a checklist, search, or simple automation, then there may be no real justification for using a complex AI model. The example of an AI powered calculator from an earlier session stays with me as a very relatable one in this context.
We have to understand the actual problem before deciding on the technological approach. Delays are not always caused by lack of technology. In many cases, clearly defining responsibilities, improving communication between teams, or standardizing workflows creates a better atmosphere than just introducing a new digital tool. 
AI may support decisions, organize information, or highlight patterns, but accountability remains with the person making the final decision. This is especially true for clinical practice. Clinical decision-support systems can provide recommendations, but responsibility for patient care ultimately remains with the primary treating team. 
We also discussed the impact of an incorrect recommendation. In a hospital environment, where a person’s life is at stake, even a seemingly small error can have significant consequences. We must therefore evaluate not only the potential benefit of a product, but also the impact of failure. Product value cannot be assessed only using average performance. Safeguards, review mechanisms, and human oversight are important. Rare but serious adverse outcomes must be considered just like how we record SAEs in hospital for any clinical trial. 
Information has value only if it reaches the person making the decision at the right time to help act on it. A correct alert that comes after a clinical or operational decision has already been taken has very little practical value. This reminded me that accuracy alone is not sufficient. Timing, clarity, and the ability to act on the information are equally important.
The starting point should always be the problem, the people affected, the possible harm, and the value expected. Only after this should we decide whether the solution requires simple automation, conventional analytics, any type of AI (predictive, generative, agentic).
Also, before applying AI to any workflow, the PM must validate an opportunity thesis well (value vs feasibility). The 4-Step AI Appropriateness Ladder consists of deterministic rules, analytics and search, predictive ML, generative or agentic AI behaviour. This, I think will be very useful if I am asked to evaluate an AI product in my workplace in future.
