This session taught us how to look for AI opportunities. As responsible PMs, we must check frequency, intensity of pain, data availability, and decision importance to justify investment in AI. Repetitive activities that consume professional time are good candidates for AI assisted solutions. For instance, monthly quality reviews require manual consolidation of information from multiple departments before analysis can even begin. Similarly, we spend a lot of time identifying missing clinical documentation, following up on unresolved action items, or preparing compliance reports. Some of these may create good operational value because the gains can be measured directly in terms of time saved and process efficiency. We have incorporated AI in our quality process at our hospital recently just a week back.  
We must evaluate how they deploy across 4-layer AI stack consisting of the data (input) - model (intelligence) - API (bridge) and user experience (what output will the clinician/patient see). Also, we have to note that the target audience sees where the product breaks first. Like a patient first identifies that the appointment chatbot is working slow. 
The emphasis on evidence is very important to my domain. If we claim that AI reduces turnaround time, improves accuracy, or reduces workload, there should be a clear comparison before and after its introduction. In healthcare, we routinely use indicators and benchmarks to assess whether an intervention has improved a process. I see the same requirement for AI products. Initial interest, a successful demonstration, or positive feedback from a few users cannot establish sustained value. A new AI tool may initially attract hospital staff because it is interesting, but this does not mean that it will become part of routine practice. Novelty is not product market fit. In hospitals, I have seen new systems generate interest during the initial phase but struggle with long-term adoption because they add steps, interrupt existing workflows, or do not solve the treating team’s main problem. Repeated use is a better indication of value than initial enthusiasm.
Switching cost is something we learned in marketing and strategy courses. Applying the same here, even if a new solution is objectively better, clinicians and operational teams may hesitate to adopt it if they have to relearn an established process, enter the same information again, or move between additional systems. This does not necessarily mean that people are resistant to improvement. Product evaluation should therefore include the effort required from front line staff, not only the capability of the technology.
I also found the discussion on human-in-the-loop design useful. Having a human approval step does not automatically make an AI process safe. If the system produces many routine recommendations, the reviewer may gradually begin approving them without examining each one carefully. I have seen a similar risk with repeated alerts and mandatory approvals in clinical and administrative workflows. Over time, what was intended as a safeguard may become a routine click. The human reviewer should therefore be involved at the point where judgement genuinely adds value, particularly when confidence is low, information is incomplete, or the consequences are significant. To ensure these models generate reliable, auditable outputs, system instructions should be tightly governed by the prompts given. (who is AI? – role, what surrounds the decision – context, what must AI do – task and what it should not – constraints)
Identifying an AI opportunity is about finding a recurring and meaningful problem, proving that the proposed solution improves the workflow, and ensuring that the benefit is good enough to justify the cost, disruption, and risk. I hope to use this in work environment in future.
