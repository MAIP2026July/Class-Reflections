This session repeatedly emphasized that AI should not be viewed as just another technical feature. Instead, it should make us question why a process exists in its current form and whether it can be redesigned completely. Many processes in hospitals continue largely because they evolved that way over time. Clinical documentation, approvals, reporting structures, and audit requirements are often built layer upon layer. Sometimes we focus on making an existing process faster without asking whether the process itself still makes sense. 
We must also distinguish between stakeholder requirements and technical solutions. Customers describe problems in their own language. Product teams must think in terms of systems, features, and technology. I could relate to it because clinicians, patients, administrators, and quality teams may all describe the same problem differently. Translating these perspectives into a consistent product vision is critical. For instance, when analyzing healthcare worker feedback, we cannot just look at a generic positive or negative score. We must extract data using the opinion quintuple framework to separate the specific target entity, the sub-feature (like app layout or speed), the exact sentiment value, the named opinion holder, and the time anchor.
We must actively look for repeated points of friction and ways to remove them. Patients and caregivers often spend considerable time navigating appointments, diagnostic procedures, billing processes, and follow-up visits. The question is not whether an AI solution can be introduced, but whether it meaningfully reduces effort, confusion, or waiting time.
When sourcing these capabilities, the PM must make strategic build versus buy versus API decisions. Subscribing to commercial APIs offers high feature speed but introduces platform dependency and token cost constraints. We must also think of data governance and regulatory compliance like the DPDP Act in India, where we operate purely as custodians of patient data and should prevent any misuse. Another thing is that if we are outsourcing a component, we should not become overtly vendor dependent. And for any proprietary data it is always better to build capacity within the organization because that will give us an edge over our competitors.
We also discussed the role of imagination in product management. New opportunities often emerge when existing assumptions are questioned. Changing business processes and old constraints are important. Healthcare is an industry where many practices are based on historical systems and structures. We must periodically question long-standing assumptions, which may result in significant improvements. This is from my personal experience.
