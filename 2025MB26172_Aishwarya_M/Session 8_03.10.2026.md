One thing that stood out to me in this session was the discussion around assumptions. I could relate this to my work. Many processes in hospitals start for a specific reason, but over time people forget why they were introduced and continue following them without any questions. Sometimes during audits or quality reviews we discover that a step in the process no longer adds value, but nobody questioned it because it existed for a very long time.
Another point I found interesting was the discussion around the fact that the real bottleneck may not be technology but human decision making. AI can generate recommendations, reports and options very quickly, but someone still has to review them and take responsibility for the final decision. In today’s world, we have no shortage of data, dashboards, reports or meetings. The difficulty is deciding what deserves attention and where leadership judgement is actually needed. One thing I had not thought about earlier was that AI may actually increase this burden. If it generates hundreds of suggestions, insights and recommendations, somebody still has to decide which ones matter and what to act on. That, I think still lies within realm of “human intelligence”.
We also discussed prototypes, and pilots. A prototype proving that something works is very different from showing that it can survive in the real world. I have seen healthcare initiatives that looked promising initially but became difficult when expanded across the hospital because workflows, people and priorities were different. Scaling is a bigger challenge than building. Also, solutions should not be built only for one situation. Our aim should be broad. 
I think one thing that I would definitely take away from this session is that when we are rapidly speeding up with AI, we must also not forget the importance of slowing down at the right moments, questioning assumptions, and making sure that decisions are still being made thoughtfully under pressure.
