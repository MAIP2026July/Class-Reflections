In this session we discussed metrics and what is success in relation to AI products. I learnt one important thing. Metrics should not be viewed only as technical measurements for markers of success or good performance. They must be seriously tied to business decision-making and operational realities. We have across other AI courses and this one learnt basics of AI model evaluation with measure of accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, true positive and false negative kind of stuff. However, the discussion highlighted that even a technically accurate model may not create value if it does not align with business objectives, operational realities and management expectations. This is also very deeply tied to financial implications which is always seen with a stringent view in all organizations. As PMs, we should be clear of the fact that any model or AI tool should ultimately bring business value while also satisfying domain-based thresholds and KPIs. In my hospital, as domain experts, our clinicians always question us - what is the use of any operational or clinical improvement project to their patients first? Then comes any other financial or technical buy in. We routinely monitor quality indicators, infection rates, waiting times, and adverse events. Here I am well aware that average mask effect of individual events, and we go in to detailed RCA for each case of morbidity or infection. 
Another insight was that metrics can sometimes create unintended behaviour. If people are measured only on a specific target, they may try to concentrate too much on that metric. For example, in one of my previous work places, found that one particular department unusually had high wait times and flagged it to the department and did an RCA on it. Then we discussed the reasons with the department consultants and unit leadership to come to a good solution to reduce wait time. Eventually, there was pressure to move patients through the department faster. After 2 months, wait times improved well, but our operations team found that patient experience was affected. Teams may become highly focused on achieving a reported number and not consider the original purpose behind the measure. Hence, we must be selecting metrics that truly reflect the value we are trying to create rather than simply measuring what is easiest to quantify.
We also discussed importance of experimentation and continuous evaluation. We may  think that creating a product and proving minimal criteria success is enough for launch. Real-world performance may differ from expectations. Whether in AI products or healthcare initiatives, systems must be monitored continuously, assumptions must be challenged, and decisions must be revisited as new information becomes available. We should carefully establishing the right mechanisms for learning and adaptation over time so that our products and processes are continuously improved and made better. In this fast changing world, what was true a week back may not have relevance now. So we have to be quick, adaptable and interested in a continuous learning process to improve ourselves over time.
