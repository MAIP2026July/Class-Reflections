## Class 2 -- The Product Life Cycle and Important Product Management Documents (1 August 2026)

This lecture gave me an overview of a product's journey, from discovery
and definition through strategy, planning, development, launch, growth
and, eventually, renewal or retirement. An important point was that
product development does not follow a fixed, one-directional sequence.
It is a repeated process in which teams learn from feedback, review
their decisions and make improvements. It is uncommon for a product to
be completely right on its first release.

Two ideas from the class were especially memorable.

**The Product Manager as a Trustee:** A Product Manager looks after
resources that belong to other people, including the organisation's
budget, employees' time and the effort invested by different teams. This
makes it important to record decisions and retain supporting evidence.
Documentation is therefore not just an administrative task; it
demonstrates that resources are being managed responsibly.

While discussing decision gates and differences in expectations, our
professor explained that decisions which are easy and inexpensive to
delay can be postponed. In contrast, decisions with significant
long-term consequences should be addressed early. He compared this with
choosing the colour of a room, which can wait, versus deciding whether
to build a house or buy an apartment. I recognised that I have sometimes
done the reverse at work: I have been quick to settle technical details
while delaying important conversations and stakeholder alignment.

**The Meaning of "Good Enough":** A product does not have to be flawless
before it is released. The Product Manager needs to recognise when it
has reached an acceptable level of readiness and decide when to launch.
Our professor described a card-processing product from the 1990s. The
initial schedule was to finish development in April, run beta testing in
May and release the product in June. The CEO was not satisfied with the
proposed schedule, and the eventual launch happened in February of the
following year. The lesson was that finishing beta testing does not mean
the work is almost over; it represents only part of the overall journey.
Teams need to manage their effort so that they do not use up all their
energy before the final stages.

We also explored the difference between a **project and a product**. A
project is usually created to meet the needs of a particular customer,
whereas a product is intended to address a problem for a wider group of
existing or potential customers. I found this distinction useful in the
context of our team assignment because it encourages us to ask whether
we are delivering a one-off solution or creating something that can be
used by many people.

The class introduced several Product Management artefacts, including
PRDs, discovery documents, roadmaps, launch briefs and PR/FAQs. I
understood that these should be flexible working documents, not
templates that must be followed rigidly in every situation. Their
content should suit the needs of the product. One point that deserves
particular attention is **traceability**. A decision recorded in a
document should be carried through into design, development, testing and
user-facing documentation. Recording a decision has limited value if it
is not reflected in the actual product.

**My Perspective:** I value the idea of keeping decisions and evidence
in a central location, including the suggestion of using GitHub. In
large organisations, however, discussions and approvals are often spread
across email, Microsoft Teams and other tools. The challenge is to
create reliable traceability without introducing another process that
simply duplicates the systems people already use.

**How I Intend to Use This at Work:** I want to record important
technical and business decisions more consistently, assess which
decisions need to be made immediately and identify which ones can safely
wait. I also plan to improve the connection between requirements,
implementation and testing so that decisions remain traceable throughout
the product life cycle.
