## Class 3 -- Choosing Product Management Frameworks to Support Decisions (18 August 2026)

The session opened with two classmates summarising lessons from earlier
lectures. We then moved into a broad discussion of Product Management
frameworks. The models covered included Jobs To Be Done, Double Diamond,
Opportunity Solution Trees, Lean Canvas, Amazon's Working Backwards
method, PR/FAQ, Porter's Five Forces, PESTLE, SWOT, Ansoff Matrix, QFD,
RICE, the Kano Model, MoSCoW, RACI/RAPID, one-way and two-way door
decisions, and OODA.

The number of frameworks initially felt quite large. However, one
principle brought the discussion together: **a framework should help us
make a decision, not make the decision for us.** Our professor described
frameworks as dials rather than the steering wheel or brakes. I took
this to mean that applying several frameworks does not automatically
improve the quality of a decision. If another model does not add useful
information, strengthen the evidence or change the decision, applying it
may simply create extra work.

A number of ideas stood out:

-   **Identify the Actual Customer:** Instead of referring to everyone
    as a "user", we should name the relevant customer group, such as a
    rider, driver, farmer or parent. Being specific helps us understand
    the person's needs, build useful personas and define the problem
    more accurately.
-   **Distinguish Transformation from Incremental Change:** The example
    of helping a caterpillar crawl faster versus enabling it to become a
    butterfly illustrated the difference between making a small
    improvement and creating a fundamental change. Organisations
    sometimes label routine improvements as transformations. We should
    judge the scale of change by what has actually changed, not by the
    language used to describe it.
-   **Treat the Product as a Moving Target:** Our professor compared
    product development to aiming a missile at a target that keeps
    moving while competitors are also pursuing it. This helped me
    understand why products need regular assessment and adjustment.
    Revising a plan in response to new evidence is not necessarily a
    sign of failure.
-   **Look at Scrum from a Product Perspective:** Having worked with
    Scrum for several years, I found this explanation useful. A sprint
    was described as an agreement between the Product Manager and the
    development team. The team should have a stable scope during the
    sprint and should not face constant interruptions. Priorities can be
    reviewed when the sprint ends. Although a sprint can be cancelled,
    its scope should not be repeatedly altered halfway through. Shorter
    sprints may suit rapidly changing environments, while more stable
    settings may support longer cycles.

Towards the end, we discussed **value capture and product economics**.
One estimate shared in class was that the organisation building a
product may capture only around 15--25% of the total value created, with
20% serving as a useful planning assumption. The balance may be retained
by customers and other participants in the ecosystem. This helped me see
that creating substantial value for customers does not mean the provider
can automatically earn the same amount. Product economics must consider
how the value is shared across the ecosystem.

**Personal Reflection:** One of the strongest moments in the lecture was
unrelated to the frameworks. Our professor spoke about returning from
leave to discover that his company had closed. Rather than concentrating
only on his own circumstances, he focused on helping his colleagues find
new opportunities. I found this example meaningful because it showed the
human dimension of leadership. Technical capability, frameworks and
commercial decisions matter, but supporting people when circumstances
become difficult is just as important.

**How I Intend to Use This at Work:** I want to choose frameworks more
carefully instead of using several simply because they are available. I
will first define the problem, select the model that best fits the
situation and use its output to support a decision. I also want to
consider specific customer needs, changing priorities and the financial
value produced by the solutions I help build.
