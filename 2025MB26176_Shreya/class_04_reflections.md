## Class 4 -- Deciding When Artificial Intelligence Is the Right Solution (22 August 2026)

This was the first lecture that concentrated directly on AI. Our
professor organised the discussion around a set of decision gates to
show how we can determine whether an AI-based solution is appropriate.

The first was **Gate 0 -- Social Legitimacy**. This step comes before
assessing whether a solution is technically possible. We should first
ask whether there is a valid reason to build it and whether doing so is
socially responsible. This involves considering consent, vulnerable
populations and whether the proposed solution relies on surveillance,
manipulation or hidden human work. Only after these questions have been
considered should we assess business value, technical feasibility and
the opportunity to learn.

The next topic was the **AI Appropriateness Ladder**, which described
four levels of possible solutions, beginning with the simplest:

1.  **Deterministic Rules:** Straightforward conditions, such as
    if-then-else logic.
2.  **Retrieval, Analytics and Search:** Locating information and
    deriving insights from existing data.
3.  **Predictive Machine Learning:** Applying historical patterns to
    estimate future outcomes.
4.  **Generative or Agentic AI:** Producing content, reasoning through
    tasks or taking actions using more advanced AI capabilities.

The key principle was to start at the lowest level that can solve the
problem. If a basic rule is sufficient, there is little justification
for introducing a complex AI model. AI should not be selected merely
because it is currently popular. The professor's example of a
"nuclear-powered pencil sharpener" made the point memorable: the fact
that a solution is possible does not mean that it is sensible. Spending
₹5,000 to sharpen a pencil would be disproportionate to the benefit.

Another idea that caught my attention was the **Actionable Time
Window**. Information is useful not only when it is accurate, but also
when it arrives early enough for someone to act on it. A weather alert
received one minute before rain begins may have very little practical
value. A farmer who receives the same warning two days earlier may have
time to arrange workers and adjust the harvest plan.

The autonomous-driving example developed this idea further. Vehicle
safety depends on several stages working together, including detecting
an obstacle, delivering an alert, allowing a person to recognise and
assess the danger, making a decision and applying the brakes, all while
maintaining an adequate safety margin. I understood that a model's
accuracy figure cannot, by itself, establish that the complete product
is safe or dependable. The end-to-end system must work under real
conditions.

Two further lessons were particularly significant:

-   **Assess Extreme Outcomes, Not Just Average Results:** A system may
    perform well in most cases, but rare events with very serious or
    long-lasting consequences still need attention. A strong average
    score does not automatically make the risk acceptable.
-   **Make Human Review Meaningful:** Human-in-the-loop design should
    involve genuine judgment, not automatic approval. In the lending
    example, the human and AI assessed applications independently, and
    only cases where their conclusions differed were escalated. This is
    more meaningful than showing a person the AI's answer first and
    expecting them to approve it.

We also discussed why people may be reluctant to adopt new technology.
Resistance does not necessarily mean that people are unaware or
unwilling to learn. A change can involve training costs, disruption to
established work and uncertainty about future support. The example of a
car that could travel 100 kilometres on one litre of fuel illustrated
this well. I might be interested in buying such a vehicle, but I would
still need to know whether service stations were available. A product's
usefulness depends on the ecosystem that supports it, not just its
technical performance.

**My Personal Reflection:** The main lesson for me was that a successful
AI initiative starts by identifying the right problem and asking the
right questions. Choosing an advanced technology should come later. I
found the distinction between model accuracy and the effectiveness of
the complete product especially useful. AI needs to be assessed in
relation to the decisions people make, the time they have to respond and
the consequences of an error.

I also understood that adoption requires more than proving that a
technology performs better. It must fit into existing workflows and
address the practical concerns of the people expected to use it. Without
that, even a technically impressive solution may fail to gain
acceptance.
