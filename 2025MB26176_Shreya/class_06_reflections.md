## Class 6 -- Expanding What Is Possible and Developing a Deeper Understanding of Customers (5 September 2026)

The class started with a few administrative points about GitHub and
topic submissions. It then moved into ideas that I found particularly
relevant to product innovation and customer understanding.

One major theme was **the art of the possible**. Our professor explained
that innovation can be limited not by the technology available, but by
our knowledge of what that technology can do. For instance, someone who
does not know that computer vision can recognise incorrectly labelled
products may never think of creating a solution around that capability.
In the same way, audiobooks were once expensive and difficult to
produce, but improvements in text-to-speech technology have made them
more accessible.

This made me realise that innovation can begin by broadening our
understanding of existing capabilities. The professor also described an
organisation in which one team printed a document, signed it, scanned it
and emailed it to another team, which then repeated the same steps. The
process had continued even though the technological limitations that
originally required it may no longer have existed. Organisations should
periodically examine their workflows and question whether a step is
still necessary or is simply being repeated out of habit.

I found the guidance on writing a **Product Requirements Document
(PRD)** useful as well. Requirements should be written from the
stakeholder's point of view, using language such as "I, as a rider,
need...". The requirement should describe what the stakeholder is trying
to achieve rather than prescribe a technical implementation. For
example, a statement that a system must handle 10,000 users is a
technical specification, not a need expressed by a rider.

The professor also highlighted the importance of using terminology
consistently across an organisation. If a team agrees to use a term such
as rider, driver, right-giver or right-taker, that wording should be
consistent in engineering discussions, support processes, product
documents and user manuals. Even minor differences in language can cause
confusion when several teams contribute to one product.

Another concept was **Black Box Reframing**. This involves keeping the
inputs and outputs of an existing process in view while reconsidering
the steps that happen in between. With newer technology, some
information that was once essential may no longer be required. A process
that previously needed 20 inputs might now work with 12. Alternatively,
a team might combine three existing inputs with two new ones that were
previously too difficult or expensive to obtain. The approach encourages
us to rethink the process itself instead of automating every existing
step without question.

The latter part of the session covered **sentiment analysis**, which
broadened my understanding of customer feedback. A simple thumbs-up or
thumbs-down response, or a single positive/negative score, may not give
a Product Manager enough detail to make a useful decision.

Our professor described an opinion through five components:

1.  **Object:** The product or item being discussed.
2.  **Feature:** The particular characteristic being evaluated.
3.  **Sentiment:** Whether the view is positive, negative or neutral.
4.  **Opinion Holder:** The person expressing the view.
5.  **Time:** When the opinion was shared.

The circumstances surrounding the opinion also matter. A customer might
say that a phone camera works very well in daylight but performs poorly
at night. That review contains different views about the same feature
under different conditions. One review can also discuss several product
features and include opinions from different people.

An example from the discussion was especially memorable. A review said,
"My mother was mad with me because I didn't tell her before I bought the
phone." This does not directly assess a technical feature, but it
reveals something about the emotional importance of the purchase and the
customer's personal situation. The professor observed that people are
unlikely to have the same emotional reaction when buying an everyday
item such as ice cream.

I understood that analysing feedback only in terms of product features
may cause us to miss important human context. The feelings and
circumstances surrounding a purchase can sometimes provide insights into
customer behaviour that a simple sentiment score cannot capture.

The class ended with a reminder about **data consent and privacy**. Our
professor emphasised that organisations look after customer data; they
do not own it. Regulations such as India's DPDP framework and GDPR need
to be considered when products collect or process personal information.
Consent and responsible data handling should be part of product design
from the beginning, not matters to address after development. An AI
product that overlooks these responsibilities can damage customer trust
and may not be viable.

**My Personal Reflection:** I learned that innovation does not always
require inventing something completely new. It can also come from
challenging established processes, recognising newly accessible
technological capabilities and redesigning workflows around them. The
discussion on sentiment analysis was particularly valuable because it
demonstrated that the context behind feedback can matter as much as
whether the feedback is positive or negative.

I also took away a useful distinction for documentation: requirements
should explain what stakeholders need, while technical specifications
should describe how the system will deliver it. Keeping these separate
can reduce misunderstandings between business and engineering teams.
