## Class 7 -- Applying Product Thinking to an Electricity Distribution Case Study (12 September 2026)

This was the last class before the midterm examination. Instead of
following a conventional lecture format, our professor conducted a live
case study. I found this method effective because it gave us an
opportunity to use Product Management concepts in a realistic business
situation.

The case was about a company building a customer-service solution for
electricity distribution companies, or DISCOMs. We were taking the
perspective of a product company selling software to DISCOMs, rather
than acting as the electricity distributor. As students suggested
possible approaches, the professor encouraged us to go beyond standard
textbook responses. For example, premium pricing or brand positioning
would not necessarily solve the customer's problem. A DISCOM is unlikely
to buy a customer-service platform for prestige; it needs a solution
that addresses an operational or financial challenge.

The discussion took a different direction when a classmate brought up
**System Thinking**. Electricity distribution companies purchase power
in advance through forward contracts. If an area experiences an outage,
the company has already paid for electricity that it cannot temporarily
distribute and sell to customers. Detecting the outage and restoring
service quickly therefore has a financial benefit in addition to
improving the customer experience. It can help reduce losses and protect
margins.

This changed my understanding of the product's value proposition. Rather
than describing the solution only as a way to improve call-centre
operations, we could link it to faster outage detection, quicker
resolution and reduced financial impact. That made the business benefit
more concrete and measurable.

The most thought-provoking question in the case was: **Why should
customers need to report an outage at all?** If the system can detect an
unusual decline in electricity consumption across a locality, it may be
possible to identify a potential outage without waiting for customers to
call. The company could then send notifications proactively.

Even when a customer does call, the organisation may already have
relevant details, such as the customer's contact number, address and
meter information. If the customer is not at home, GPS information,
where appropriately available and consented to, could help identify the
relevant location. With this information, the system might prevent some
calls from being necessary in the first place.

I found this a strong illustration of the difference between **improving
an existing activity and rethinking the activity itself**. Making a call
centre more efficient is one option. Removing the need for certain calls
through proactive detection is a more fundamental change.

The pricing conversation raised another business issue. Under an
outcome-based arrangement, the provider might charge for each ticket
successfully resolved. This links payment to delivered value, but it
also means the provider takes on part of the business risk. Ticket
volumes can be low during normal periods and rise sharply when many
outages occur.

We also considered per-seat pricing. This can create a conflict of
incentives: if the customer improves operations and needs fewer support
agents, the provider may earn less. I realised that pricing should do
more than generate revenue. It should encourage behaviours that benefit
both the customer and the provider.

Another framework introduced in the session was **TFIR -- Truth, Facts,
Inferences and Recommendations**. It helped clarify how different kinds
of information should be managed in an AI product.

-   **Truth and Facts:** These should be made as dependable as possible
    through database queries and tool-based retrieval, rather than
    relying entirely on generated text.
-   **Inferences:** These are conclusions based on available information
    and should be supported by relevant evidence.
-   **Recommendations:** This is the most sensitive stage because the
    system moves beyond describing information and starts advising
    someone what to do. The product should make the evidence and
    reasoning behind a recommendation visible so that a human can
    identify faulty assumptions or unsupported conclusions.

Other useful lessons included:

-   **Third-Party and Supply-Chain Exposure:** Build, buy and subscribe
    choices involve more than development expenses. If an OTP provider
    becomes unavailable, internet banking may stop working. In the same
    way, a vendor changing or withdrawing a model can affect a launched
    AI product even when our application code has not changed.
-   **AI Products Need Ongoing Management After Launch:** In
    conventional software, uncertainty may fall substantially once the
    product reaches General Availability. In AI products, uncertainty
    can continue because models, APIs and external services may change
    over time.
-   **Product Managers Should Know How to Demonstrate Their Product:** A
    Product Manager should be able to walk someone through the solution
    independently. Always depending on another person to demonstrate it
    may indicate that the product is not understood deeply enough.
-   **Stay Connected to Customer Support:** Spending time with support
    teams after launch can reveal recurring customer problems and
    product gaps that may not be apparent during development.

**My Personal Reflection:** The main lesson from this case was that the
strongest product opportunity may come from questioning the problem
statement itself. Instead of only making the existing complaint-handling
process more efficient, we considered whether the need for customers to
complain could be reduced through proactive detection. This reinforced
the importance of understanding the customer's wider business context
and connecting the proposed solution to measurable outcomes.
