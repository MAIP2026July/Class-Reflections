## Class 14 -- Improving Product Positioning, Financial Thinking and Customer Value (21 November 2026)

The second day of project presentations was another long session,
conducted partly while our professor was travelling. I felt that this
demonstrated his commitment to reviewing our work. The comments were
more direct and detailed than in the previous session, which made them
especially useful.

A number of themes appeared across the presentations:

-   **Look for Transformational Opportunities:** In the school
    admissions solution, our professor questioned why people should
    continue contacting staff for routine information such as fee
    details or academic dates. AI should be able to handle these
    questions dependably so that employees can focus on situations that
    genuinely need human attention.
-   **Measure AI Costs Before Setting the Price:** One team estimated
    that a call would cost less than ₹1. The professor challenged the
    calculation and asked whether it included every component. RAG,
    re-ranking, reasoning tokens and retries can all increase the actual
    expense. I understood that a working prototype should be used to
    measure cost, response time and accuracy before making pricing
    commitments.
-   **Consider Value Beyond the Immediate User:** In the
    wardrobe-assistant example, our professor encouraged the team to
    examine the opportunity for clothing brands and exporters, not just
    the convenience offered to an individual household. This highlighted
    the need to identify where the solution can create the greatest
    business impact.
-   **Treat Self-Development as a Competitive Risk:** A prospective
    customer may ask why they should buy our product instead of building
    something similar with a Claude skill or an existing subscription.
    If the solution is only a thin interface around a prompt, a model
    provider could introduce the same capability and make it
    unnecessary. A product therefore needs a more durable point of
    differentiation.
-   **Move from Finding Errors to Preventing Them:** Detecting a problem
    after it occurs may not be enough to create a compelling solution.
    Our professor compared this with the red underline in an IDE:
    developers gain more value when mistakes are prevented before they
    happen rather than simply highlighted afterwards.
-   **Begin with a Specific Customer Group:** The digital twin team was
    encouraged to focus on one role, course or organisational function
    and make the solution work particularly well for that group before
    expanding. I also found the discussion of undocumented
    organisational knowledge useful. Employees often understand local
    expressions, abbreviations and informal processes that never appear
    in official documents.
-   **Consider a Smaller Offering at a Much Lower Price:** A product
    does not have to reproduce every feature offered by a large
    competitor. Removing capabilities that a particular segment does not
    need can make the solution simpler and more affordable. This is the
    principle of offering "less for a lot less".
-   **Consider Partnerships as an Alternative to Direct Competition:**
    For the screen-security product, the professor suggested becoming an
    additional capability within existing VDI platforms rather than
    competing with those platforms directly. However, this approach
    carries its own risk because the partner may eventually build the
    feature internally.
-   **Check Commercial Licensing Early:** Some open-source models and
    libraries may be free for non-commercial use but require a paid
    licence when used in a commercial product. If these conditions are
    not checked early, the actual development and operating costs will
    be unclear, making it difficult to set a reliable price.
-   **Validate Value Through Real Customer Commitments:** In the
    sales-document generator example, the professor suggested
    approaching salespeople who might be willing to accept higher
    targets in return for access to the tool. I found this compelling
    because a real willingness to take on additional targets would be
    stronger evidence of value than simply asking people whether they
    liked the idea.
-   **Present the Solution with Confidence:** Teams should avoid
    introducing their ideas as though they are only tentative thoughts.
    Every member should understand the complete product well enough to
    explain any part of it. In a VC meeting, there may not be an
    opportunity for every team member to speak.

**My Final Reflection:** Looking at both presentation sessions together,
I realised that the biggest challenge for many teams, including mine,
was not necessarily building the technology. Most teams could
demonstrate a working solution. The harder questions were about
positioning the product, proving its unit economics and presenting
convincing evidence that customers would receive value from it.

This connects with one of the central lessons of the course: AI is a way
of delivering value, but it is not the product's entire purpose. The
product is the promise made to a defined customer, the problem it solves
and the economic benefit that makes the solution worth purchasing.

## Overall Reflection -- The Accountability Involved in Product Management

The strongest lesson I have taken from these fourteen classes is that
Product Management requires a continuous sequence of decisions, often
made without having all the information we would ideally want. The goal
is not to make every choice perfectly. It is to improve the quality of
our decisions over time and avoid repeating preventable mistakes.

Frameworks help organise our thinking, evidence reduces uncertainty, and
metrics allow us to assess results. However, metrics are most useful
when they are connected to real financial consequences expressed in
rupees, rather than being limited to percentages. These methods can
support the decision-making process, but they cannot take responsibility
for the final decision.

Ultimately, the Product Manager must make the call, take ownership of it
and accept the outcome. For me, that is the essence of the
responsibility that comes with Product Management.
