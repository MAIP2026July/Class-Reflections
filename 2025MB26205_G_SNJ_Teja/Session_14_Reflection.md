# Session 14 Reflection

**Date:** 21 November 2026

I presented AdmitAssist first in this session, as a solo team (Team 16). I explained how a school gets 150 to 200 admission enquiries a month on WhatsApp, calls, walk-ins and email, how each takes the coordinator 5 to 6 minutes, and how a parent ready to join can wait behind less urgent messages. I showed the prototype that sorts messages by priority and drafts replies. The professor's feedback was that I need to be more ambitious. In his words it still looked like "paste and read." His question was why a human should answer simple questions at all, like when the term starts or what the fees are. AI should answer those reliably by itself, and only the questions it can't answer should go to the coordinator, with a draft they can accept, edit or rewrite. He also said to get one channel right first and then extend to WhatsApp, voice and email, and pointed me to his voice-in, voice-out demo for a university. I'll be honest that this was hard to hear in the moment, but he was right that my design kept a human in places where it didn't need one.

The other presentations repeated a few themes. The wardrobe assistant was asked to look at the value for Indian garment exporters, not just one family. A team claiming each AI call costs under ₹1 was asked whether retrieval, re-ranking, reasoning tokens and retries were included, which told me to measure cost on a working prototype before setting a price. Several teams were asked why a customer wouldn't just build the same thing with a Claude skill, which is a real risk for any thin layer on top of a model. Other points I noted: prevent errors instead of only flagging them, start with one narrow segment, offer less for a lot less where big products over-serve, check open-source licences before commercial use, and look for proof of value through behaviour, like salespeople willing to take a higher target in exchange for the tool.
