# Session 1 Reflection - The Product Manager as CEO of the Product

## What was familiar under different names

Accountability without authority is not new to me. As a reporting lead, I own the output of reports that are partly built by vendor teams and by functions I do not manage. The session called this influence without authority. In my world, it is a report owner chasing a vendor fix before a submission deadline.

## Looking at my current work through this lens

The cricket analogy made me uncomfortable in a useful way. When a report goes out late or with an error, our first instinct is to locate where it broke: the vendor, the requirement, or testing. Last year, during a major literature surveillance audit cycle, a critical monthly volume report was delayed due to an unannounced vendor patch that broke our query mapping. My immediate reaction was to point directly to the vendor's uncommunicated release as the root cause. That is a necessary habit for CAPA, but the session's point is that the owner cannot use root cause as distance. If I own the report, the late delivery is mine, even if the defect sat in someone else's code.

I noticed I describe my role as "coordinating" rather than "owning." That framing quietly lets the outcome belong to nobody.

## What was genuinely new to me

First, the three pillars: AI to build the product, AI for product management work, and AI inside the product. I had only been thinking about the third. I already use AI to draft specifications, but I never counted that as part of managing a product. Separating them helps, because I can adopt the first two now at low risk, while the third needs validation.

Second, the definition of done. He said that for AI products it is contextual and there is no ready framework. In GxP I always had a clear one: PQ passed, released. But that is done for a deterministic report. For an AI screening tool, passing PQ on a test set does not tell me it will keep performing next month. My most familiar definition of done may be exactly the thing that does not transfer.

## Applying it to something I am planning

For the literature surveillance tool, I asked myself: whose head is on the block if it misses a reportable case? The answer cannot be "the model." If I propose it, I must be ready to own the miss. That changes what I would build first. It is not the smartest classifier. It is the review and escalation path that lets me stand behind its outputs.

It also changes what technical fluency means for me. I do not need to build models. But I need enough understanding to question a vendor's precision and recall figures, and to notice when an average hides a failure.

## Where I think it does not fully apply

The CEO mindset assumes you can choose to ignore feedback. In PV, some feedback is not optional. I cannot overrule a QA finding or a QPPV decision because I believe the product is right. In regulated work, the PM is more like a CEO with a strong board, where some decisions are reserved.

Similarly, "if internal engineering does not deliver, get it built elsewhere" sounds simple. In a validated environment, switching vendor means requalification. The threat is real but slow, so it carries less weight in negotiation than it would elsewhere.

## What I will do differently

I will change how I write status updates. Instead of "coordinating with vendor," I will state what I own and the outcome expected by when. I will also review the batch of ~20 legacy reports under my ownership that no longer have active users and propose retiring at least two before the next audit cycle.
