# Session 10 Reflection - Scaling as a Promise, the Quality-Latency-Cost Sweet Spot, and Versioning

## What was familiar under different names

The observation made in class that people often value predictability over speed matched my stakeholders exactly. The professor described adding extra processing time to a product so that each unit of work took a predictable duration, rather than being sometimes fast and sometimes very slow. My report consumers behave the same way. A report that arrives reliably every Monday morning is valued more than one that sometimes arrives early and sometimes late. We call it a delivery schedule; the session called it a promise.

The progression of features moving from "wow" to "expected" to "hygiene" was also familiar. A full audit trail and electronic signatures were once key selling points in safety systems. Today, a system without them would not even be shortlisted.

## Looking at my current work through this lens

The idea that a product's promise is rewritten once it becomes mission critical made me look at my Non-GxP reports differently. Across our portfolio, I can identify about 3 Non-GxP reports that started as simple internal convenience trackers but have quietly drifted into de facto compliance inputs - their numbers are now regularly quoted in executive compliance discussions and periodic safety updates. The reports themselves did not change, but the business dependence on them did. Their original classification and light testing level no longer match how they are actually used, because nobody re-assesses a report when its use quietly grows.

The concept of "noisy neighbours" and tiered queues (express vs. passenger trains running on the same track) also maps directly to our environment. During our peak month-end reporting windows, ad-hoc exploratory queries and routine operational summaries compete for server resources on the exact same database instance as our time-critical safety intelligence reports. Last quarter, a heavy unindexed ad-hoc query executed during peak hours caused our critical literature surveillance summary report to stall for nearly two hours. When critical reports slow down, the cause is often not the report itself but what else is running beside it.

## What was genuinely new to me

The sweet spot between quality, latency, and cost, set and communicated explicitly by the product manager. It was warned in class that if the team does not know the acceptable range, each person optimises one dimension in isolation: someone reduces cost and quality silently drops, or someone chases quality and cost rises. I have never given a vendor or developer an explicit target range balancing all three dimensions together. We typically specify functional requirements and leave performance trade-offs unstated.

The second new idea was the "commandos versus army" distinction. Some team members perform best in short, intense pushes - such as audit preparation or peak reporting deadlines - while others excel at steady, sustained daily operations. I tend to measure everyone in my area against the same operational yardstick, which suits neither group.

## Applying it to something I am planning

For the literature surveillance tool, I would explicitly define the sweet spot before choosing a model architecture:

- **Quality:** A non-negotiable minimum sensitivity floor (e.g., 99%+) that cannot be traded away.
- **Latency:** A maximum turnaround per batch that comfortably fits within regulatory reporting timelines.
- **Cost:** A defined maximum processing cost per article screened.

Any model or routing choice must stay within all three constraints. If a smaller, lower-cost model reliably meets the sensitivity floor for routine articles, it can handle those initial passes, routing only complex or uncertain items to a larger model.

## Where I think it does not fully apply

It was suggested in class that legal disclaimers do not protect you because users judge a product by how they actually use it. In GxP, the intended-use statement and validated scope carry significant formal weight with a regulatory inspector. However, the core lesson still holds in practice because operational usage frequently drifts beyond original intended use, as my Non-GxP example demonstrates.

I also partly disagree with delaying operational controls until a product proves itself. In a validated regulatory environment, monitoring, audit trails, and version control are the initial price of entry, not features added after achieving product-market fit. Furthermore, dynamic model routing introduces a decision boundary that itself requires formal validation, meaning the cost savings from mixing small and large models are somewhat smaller in GxP than suggested in class.

## What I will do differently

I will write a one-line promise for each of our critical reports detailing who relies on it, by when, and to what level of accuracy, and verify whether my team can state it clearly. I will also initiate a formal review of the 3 Non-GxP reports whose actual operational use has made them de facto compliance inputs, raising them for official re-classification and validation assessment.
