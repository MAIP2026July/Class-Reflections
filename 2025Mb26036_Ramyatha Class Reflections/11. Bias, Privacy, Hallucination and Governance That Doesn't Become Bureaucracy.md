# Session 11 Reflection - Bias, Privacy, Hallucination and Governance That Doesn't Become Bureaucracy

## What was familiar under different names

The professor's example of a drug tested on one population and prescribed to another is the reason pharmacovigilance exists. Clinical trials under-represent children, pregnant women, the elderly and many ethnic groups. Post-marketing surveillance is how the industry corrects for that bias after launch. I had never thought of PV as bias correction, but that is what it is.

The risk assessment discussed in class, combining severity, likelihood and detectability, is FMEA under another name. We already score risks this way in validation, multiplying the three into a risk priority number. What the session added was reversibility: can the decision be undone if it turns out to be wrong? We don't score that today.

Monitoring even when there is no human in the main workflow was also familiar. In literature surveillance, we QC a sample of the articles the screener excluded, not only the ones it flagged. That is a shadow workflow under a different name.

## Looking at my current work through this lens

The "child who thinks every security guard is a Gurkha" example made me think about where my own tools learn from. A screening tool trained mostly on English-language, indexed journal abstracts has only seen that world. Case reports published in regional or non-indexed journals, often in local languages, look different. The tool may under-detect them, not because of a flaw in the model, but because of what it was exposed to. That creates a regional blind spot in safety surveillance, and our overall QC numbers would not reveal it unless we split them by source.

Need-to-know access also applies. Line listings with patient-level detail are sometimes shared more widely than the task requires, simply because it is easier than building role-specific versions. The story shared in class of a team member misusing a candidate's personal details was a reminder that convenient access is still exposure.

## What was genuinely new to me

How incidents are communicated. The session contrasted a vague breach notice ("some customers were affected") with a precise one ("if you have not received an email by 7 a.m., you are not affected"). When we find an error in a report that has already been distributed, our correction notice usually goes to the whole distribution list in general terms. A precise message, naming which version, which recipients and which figures changed, would reduce both confusion and rework.

The second idea was the free-labour trap in governance. Subject-matter experts on review boards often do it on top of their day job. When review is unpaid extra work, it drifts toward box-ticking. The point made in class that governance should not become theatre explains some approvals I have seen pass with very little scrutiny.

## Applying it to something I am planning

For our group project, I will write a data inventory section: each data element collected, why it is needed, who can see it and how long it is kept. For a diabetes medication companion, that means stating clearly what patient health data we hold and why.

For the literature tool, I would design "abstain" as a route to human review, never as an exclusion. In PV, an article the tool is unsure about must never silently disappear.

## Where I think it does not fully apply

The session recommended not collecting attributes such as age or gender, as in blind academic review. In PV, that would be harmful. Age, sex, pregnancy status and weight are clinically essential for assessing an adverse event, and a paediatric or pregnancy exposure is itself a special situation. Data minimisation in PV means the minimum needed for a sound safety assessment. It does not mean removing demographics.

Blind review also conflicts with causality assessment, which depends on patient context. Fairness in PV comes from consistent criteria applied to complete information, not from hiding information.

## What I will do differently

I will add a reversibility score to our validation risk assessments, next to severity, likelihood and detectability. I will also propose splitting literature screening QC results by journal region and language, so any blind spot shows up in the numbers.
