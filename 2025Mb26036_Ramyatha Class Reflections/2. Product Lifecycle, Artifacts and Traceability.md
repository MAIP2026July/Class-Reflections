# Session 2 Reflection - Product Lifecycle, Artifacts and Traceability

## What was familiar under different names

Most of what was covered I already do, but we call it something else. What the session called a PRD, I know as a functional specification requirements (FSR) document. Discovery is requirement collection. The design specification and code review sit exactly where "build and learn" sits on the professor's diagram. Report verification and IQ/OQ/PQ are what the professor called acceptance evidence and launch readiness. Risk assessment appears on both lists under the same name. It was useful to see that these are the same discipline with different vocabulary.

## Looking at my current work through this lens

Most of what I do is enhancement rather than new build: a new requirement from regulatory authorities or a change to an existing report. Seeing it laid against the lifecycle showed me something I had not noticed.

For a change, we run the full lower half of the loop properly: requirement, specification, design, review, testing, release. That part is rigorous and auditable. Before building anything new, we check whether an existing report already covers the requirement, so we are not duplicating.

But that check answers a different question than the session was asking. Ours is "does this already exist?" The session's is "is this a problem worth solving, and does the same need exist across other teams?" One prevents duplication. The other decides whether the request should become a product at all. We are good at the first and rarely ask the second in a documented way. What we also do not record is what we chose not to build, and why.

He made the distinction that a project solves for one known customer, while a product generalises to users you have not met. By that definition, most of what I deliver are projects, even though the outputs are used across a portfolio. The trigger stays a single request and never gets generalised.

## What was genuinely new to me

Traceability applied to decisions, not just requirements.

In my work, traceability is a compliance obligation: what is in the specification must appear in the design and must be tested, and an audit will find it if it drops. But we trace what was built. The session traced why it was chosen: the evidence behind it, the option that was rejected, who decided. I have never seen that written down anywhere in my working life. When someone asks two years later why a report was built a particular way, the answer comes from memory or from an old email thread.

## Applying it to something I am planning

I have been sketching an in-house literature surveillance tool. The session changed how I am thinking about it in three ways.

First, I had been treating it as a system to build rather than a product. My starting point was one team's problem and one team's product list. His distinction that a product generalises to users you have not met turns that into a design question. Do I hardcode the product list, or make it configurable? That is not a technical detail. It is the difference between a project and a product.

Second, I now see it sits on the ground-up AI-native side rather than the AI-enablement side. It is not adding AI to an existing report; there is no established workflow for the tool to slot into. That matters because he said discovery is hardest exactly here: users cannot describe a way of working they have never seen. My instinct was to gather requirements first, as I would for a report. A rough prototype people can react to will teach me more than a requirements meeting.

Third, and this is the part I had not thought about at all: the tool is not one AI system, it is several, and they cannot be evaluated the same way. Highlighting products and adverse events in an article is a predictive task, judged on precision and recall. A knowledge graph supporting causality assessment is judged on whether its output is grounded and where the evidence came from. Workflow triggers that route items for review behave like agents, judged on whether they escalate correctly and can be recovered when they do not. I had been thinking of "the AI" as one thing, and it is not.

Where I think I have an advantage over the framework rather than a gap is the regulated addendum. He described intended-use classification and regulatory constraints as an extension the PRD needs. In my environment that is not an extension; GxP and validation are the starting conditions. What was new was seeing them treated as product constraints that shape architecture, rather than only as compliance steps that follow it.

I should also be honest that the idea has not passed any decision gate. It is an idea I like, not one that has been through an evidence check.

## Where I think it does not fully apply

Two qualifications.

"Good enough to launch" assumes you can iterate cheaply after release. In a validated system the floor is set externally: PQ either passes or it does not. Good enough is not mine alone to decide.

And the claim that AI-assisted prototyping has shrunk the cost of failure holds for the build only. In my environment the build was never the expensive part. Qualification, change control and the audit trail were, and none of those got cheaper. So the cost of attempting something has fallen much more than the cost of failing at it.

## What I will do differently

Add a short decision log to one piece of work in progress: the choice made, the evidence behind it, what was rejected, who decided. Not a new document type, just capturing reasoning that currently lives in email threads.
