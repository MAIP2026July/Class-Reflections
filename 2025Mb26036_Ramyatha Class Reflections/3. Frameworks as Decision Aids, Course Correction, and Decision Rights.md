# Session 3 Reflection - Frameworks as Decision Aids, Course Correction, and Decision Rights

## What was familiar under different names

Scrum explained as a labour negotiation matched my world more closely than I expected. We don't run sprints, but our release windows work the same way. Once a change is approved and enters validation, the scope is strictly frozen. Our formal change-control "freeze" typically holds for 8 to 12 weeks while validation protocol execution and hypercare run their course. Anything new waits for the next change request cycle. The professor's point was that the team gets stability for a fixed period, and everything can change at the boundary. Our change control does exactly that, except our "sprint" is set by regulatory validation effort rather than by how dynamic the market is.

The layering idea also felt familiar: a generic core, an industry variant on top, then customer-specific customisation. Our report portfolio already has this shape. Global standard reports form the core, regional variants sit on top, and affiliate-specific requests come last. We just never designed it deliberately as layers.

## Looking at my current work through this lens

The "art of saying no" landed. When one affiliate asks for a highly specific version of a report, we usually build it because the request is legitimate and the effort seems small. A few quarters ago, an affiliate requested a custom weekly literature tracking dashboard with unique local language triage tags. We built it in a two-week sprint, but it has turned into an ongoing maintenance burden - requiring individual re-testing during every system upgrade, separate data mapping validations, and constant audit maintenance for a team of just three users. He said that if a request is too specific, either the requester carries the full operational cost or you keep it separate from the core. We have never made that downstream cost visible to the requester.

The point about customers being able to build it themselves also applies to me. With self-service BI tools and AI assistants, some internal teams now build their own screening dashboards instead of raising a formal change request. My competitive advantage used to be knowing the safety data model and query logic. That knowledge is becoming far less exclusive.

## What was genuinely new to me

One-way vs two-way doors, paired with blast radius. I have always applied the same rigorous review depth to every single change. But changing a case-count filter in a report that directly feeds a periodic safety update report (PSUR) is a classic one-way door: once submitted to a health authority, it can't be quietly reversed. Changing a column layout on an internal operational tracker is a two-way door. They deserve completely different levels of review and different decision owners.

The second new idea was the moving target. I used to treat requirements as fixed once signed off in a functional specification. He described a product as a missile chasing a target that keeps moving, with competitors aiming at the same target. In literature surveillance and pharmacovigilance, regulatory expectations shift while we build. A requirement signed off in one quarter can be outdated by the time the software is validated and deployed.

## Applying it to something I am planning

For the literature surveillance tool, I tried a quick Porter view, looking at where we sit now versus 18 months ahead. Today, literature database vendors hold strong supplier power. In 18 months, built-in AI screening features from those same vendor platforms may cover a large part of what I want to build. If that happens, building in-house only makes sense for the niche parts they won't offer - such as seamless, validated handoff into our internal safety database workflow. The forecast changes what I would build first.

## Where I think it does not fully apply

The 20% value-capture guideline assumes there is a direct commercial price. Internal compliance and safety tools have no price tag, only a budget approval, so I'm not sure how that specific rule translates to internal chargebacks or cost-avoidance calculations.

I also partly disagree with the PR-FAQ critique. He said it fails because the target moves. In regulated GxP work, a fixed end-state statement is useful precisely because it anchors the initial validation scope. Moving the target mid-build is extraordinarily expensive once validation protocols are signed. The critique fits fast consumer software better than validated safety systems.

And "don't bother giving people timesheets" is right for pure research teams, but my vendor partner work is billed directly by effort and billable hours, so time tracking isn't optional in that setup.

## What I will do differently

I will tag each open change request as one-way or two-way and agree on a fast-track review level for two-way doors with my QA counterpart. For the next affiliate-specific report request that comes in, I will state the long-term maintenance, revalidation, and audit cost upfront before agreeing to add it to our release build.
