# Session 4 Reflection - Where AI Actually Creates Value

## What was familiar under different names

The actionable time window is something PV already lives by. The professor's example was rain: a warning one minute before it rains is useless. In PV, the expedited reporting clock starts on Day 0, when anyone in the company first becomes aware of a valid case. A literature tool that flags a serious case correctly but after the reporting deadline has passed has no value, however accurate it was. We call it compliance timeliness. The session called it the actionable window. It is the same idea.

Tail risk over averages was also familiar. One missed fatal or serious case outweighs thousands of correctly screened non-serious articles. Our inspection exposure comes from the tail, not the average.

## Looking at my current work through this lens

The AI ladder changed how I look at my own ideas. It runs from deterministic rules, to search and analytics, to predictive ML, and only then to generative or agentic AI. You start at the lowest rung that solves the problem.

My QC oversight concept includes checks like pregnancy reported for a male patient, or an important medical event coded but not marked serious. Laid against the ladder, those are rule checks. They need an if-then condition, not an LLM. I had been describing the whole thing as an "agentic AI project" because the tool I planned to build it in is an agent builder. The agent may be useful for orchestration and routing, but the checks themselves are the bottom rung. Calling it AI end to end was the "nuclear-powered pencil sharpener" he warned about.

## What was genuinely new to me

The parallel decision design from his lending startup was new to me. The human and the AI decide independently. If they agree, it moves on. If they disagree, the human must justify the decision. I have seen reviewers start to accept tool suggestions without real review once they trust the tool. His design keeps the human's judgment active instead of turning review into a confirmation click.

The second new idea was the pre-AI baseline. I have read vendor claims of time saved, but we have never measured our own minutes per article, or our miss rate, before introducing a tool. Without a baseline, I cannot prove that AI added anything.

## Applying it to something I am planning

When I evaluated a vendor signal intelligence platform, one question mattered most: can it show why it flagged something? A classmate described rejecting a tool because the vendor called its decision logic proprietary. In PV, that should be an automatic rejection. An inspector will ask why a signal was or was not raised. "The vendor won't say" is not an answer I can give.

For causality, a classmate's question about AI and subject-matter experts disagreeing applies directly. The answer was a shared rubric for both. In PV that rubric already exists as standard causality assessment criteria. I could make the tool score against the same criteria our reviewers use, so any disagreement is visible and comparable.

## Where I think it does not fully apply

The parallel decision model is expensive at volume. With a portfolio of 700+ products, asking a human to decide every article independently removes most of the efficiency the tool was meant to create. I would apply it to a sample, or to high-risk categories only, rather than every item.

The social legitimacy gate also feels different in PV. Legitimacy is defined less by society and more by the regulator. Good pharmacovigilance practice already allows automation, provided it is validated. The harder gate for us is inspection readiness, not public acceptance.

## What I will do differently

Before any AI pilot, I will record a pre-AI baseline: minutes per article, and misses found in QC. I will also classify each check in the QC oversight concept by ladder rung, and use AI only where a rule cannot do the job.
