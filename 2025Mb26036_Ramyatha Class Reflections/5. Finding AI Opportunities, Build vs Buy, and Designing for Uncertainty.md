# Session 5 Reflection - Finding AI Opportunities, Build vs Buy, and Designing for Uncertainty

## What was familiar under different names

The opportunity signals matched how I already judge where automation pays off. He listed frequent pain, high decision intensity and data-rich workflows. Literature screening has all three: high volume, repeated every week, and a relevance decision on every article. His red flag was the opposite case, an intranet screen built for a hierarchy that changes once in years. We have equivalents, such as the annual reconfiguration of product lists in reports. That happens rarely enough that a back-end update is cheaper than building and validating a screen for it.

Materiality was also familiar from accounting. Not every step deserves AI, only the ones where the gain is large enough to matter.

## Looking at my current work through this lens

I asked in class how to work with the product owners of the vendor tools we use. My team depends on several vendor platforms for literature, safety data and reporting, each with its own product owner. His answer reframed the relationship. Those product owners are not part of my team. They are my suppliers, and I am their customer. I can influence their roadmap, especially as a large customer, but I do not own it. If a feature is not on their roadmap, my options are to accept that, build around it, or switch.

That changed how I see vendor requirement meetings. I had been approaching them as if I could direct the outcome. In a Porter view, they are a supplier-power question. How much leverage I have depends on how dependent they are on us, and how costly it would be for us to switch.

## What was genuinely new to me

Designing the interface for uncertainty. None of the tools I use show the reviewer how confident the system is. Every suggestion looks equally certain. He described colour or font changes for low confidence, fallback behaviour when the system is unsure, and telling the reviewer why an item was escalated. That last point matters most. If a case reaches the reviewer flagged "low confidence: drug name not found in product list," the reviewer knows what to check. Without it, they redo the whole review.

The second new idea was overwhelming the reader. Some AI tools I have evaluated produce long narrative summaries that reviewers skip. A short answer that is used is worth more than a long analysis that is ignored.

## Applying it to something I am planning

For the literature surveillance tool, I would keep the model behind its own API layer, as he recommended, so it can be swapped without rebuilding the product. But in a validated system, swapping the model still triggers change control and revalidation. The API layer makes the swap technically easy. It does not make it cheap. I need to plan for that.

He also contrasted weeks of debate with a 48-hour AI-built prototype. I can build a mock triage screen, with confidence flags and an escalation reason, outside the validated environment, and show it to reviewers before writing a single requirement.

## Where I think it does not fully apply

He suggested holding back part of a reviewer's bonus, or withdrawing a promotion, if decisions were found to be rubber-stamped. In PV, I would be cautious. Safety depends on people reporting their own errors and near misses. A penalty-led approach could make reviewers hide mistakes instead of raising them. I would rather fix fatigue through design, such as escalation reasons and sampling, than through penalties.

Calling an external LLM API is also less simple for us. Patient-level safety data has privacy and residency limits, so some use cases may force a self-hosted or open-weight model regardless of cost.

## What I will do differently

I will map each vendor platform my team depends on as a supplier: what we depend on them for, how easily we could switch, and where we have leverage. For any review queue I design, I will include a mandatory "why escalated" field and show the supporting evidence alongside the item.
