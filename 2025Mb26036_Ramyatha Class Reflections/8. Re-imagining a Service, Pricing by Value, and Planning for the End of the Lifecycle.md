# Session 7 Reflection - Re-imagining a Service, Pricing by Value, and Planning for the End of the Lifecycle

## What was familiar under different names

The electricity case discussed in class moved from "answer the complaint faster" to "detect the outage before anyone calls." In PV we know this shift as the difference between waiting for spontaneous reports and actively looking for signals. Proactive surveillance is the core of what we do with safety data. The session applied the same logic to customer service.

The point about planning for data at the end of the lifecycle was also familiar. In PV, safety data must be retained long after a product leaves the market. Retention, disposal, and audit trail are already part of our system documentation. What the session added was treating them as product decisions from day one, not as an archiving task at the end.

## Looking at my current work through this lens

The most useful idea for me was detecting a fault from a change in the pattern. As highlighted in class, if power consumption in a locality suddenly drops, something is probably broken, even before a customer complains. My reports work the same way. In my Session 1 reflection I described a vendor patch that broke a query mapping and delayed a critical report. If that report had compared its case count with previous periods, a sudden drop would have shown the break the same day. Instead, we found out when the stakeholder noticed. Today, none of our recurring reports check their own output for unusual volumes.

It was also emphasized in class that a product manager should be able to demo the product end-to-end and should spend time in customer support. Across our 12 primary recurring reports, I can currently demonstrate about 8 of them end-to-end; for the remaining 4 complex technical pipelines, I still rely on our developer to navigate the underlying scripts. By the standard set in class, that is a clear gap I need to close.

## What was genuinely new to me

The TFIR framework: truth, facts, inferences, recommendations. It gives me a way to explain to a reviewer, or an inspector, which parts of an AI output can be trusted. In a safety context, case data pulled from the safety database is fact. A suggested causality assessment is inference. "Escalate this as a potential signal" is a recommendation. If every recommendation shows the facts and inferences it rests on, a reviewer can reject it quickly when the underlying fact is wrong. Hallucination was described in class as shades of grey rather than a yes-or-no failure, and this structure makes handling that practical.

The second new idea was escrow. A large customer demands the source code so it is not held hostage if the vendor goes out of business. I had never connected this to AI tools. If an AI screening vendor shuts down or retires a model, can we still explain to an inspector, years later, why an article was classified the way it was?

## Applying it to something I am planning

For the literature surveillance tool, I would store the model version, the input, and the output for every decision. Any decision could then be reproduced or explained after the model changes. I would also add a vendor exit question to every AI evaluation I do: what happens to our decision history if this vendor or model is withdrawn?

## Where I think it does not fully apply

Outcome-based pricing sounded attractive in class, but in PV it can distort behaviour. If a screening vendor were paid per case identified, the incentive would be to classify borderline articles as cases. That raises false positives and reviewer workload. A per-outcome price only works if the outcome is defined so that it cannot be gamed - for example, cases confirmed after review rather than cases flagged.

Being hands-on in support also has limits in a validated environment. Segregation of duties means a report owner may not have production access to fix a ticket directly. I can still sit in on support, read the tickets, and learn from them, even if I cannot resolve them myself.

## What I will do differently

I will add a volume-anomaly check to our 4 most critical recurring reports. It will flag any output whose count moves outside the normal range compared with previous periods. I will also practice full end-to-end demonstrations for the 4 technical report pipelines I currently defer to developers on, and add vendor exit and decision-reproducibility criteria to my AI vendor evaluation checklist.
