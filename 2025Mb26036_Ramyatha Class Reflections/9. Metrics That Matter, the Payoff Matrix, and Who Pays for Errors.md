# Session 9 Reflection - Metrics That Matter, the Payoff Matrix, and Who Pays for Errors

## What was familiar under different names

Precision, recall, and sensitivity are not new to me. In literature surveillance, we already report them when evaluating screening performance. The session also emphasised that averages hide the tail. That matches how inspections work. Nobody audits our average; they look for the one serious case that was missed.

The point made in class that metrics must evolve with the product was familiar too. The KPIs we agreed at go-live - such as articles screened and percentage completed on time - still sit on our dashboards. They tell me the process ran, but they don't tell me whether quality has drifted since then.

## Looking at my current work through this lens

I shared in class that we use precision, accuracy, and sensitivity in literature surveillance but have never converted them into economic value. The loan example presented in class showed why that matters: a model with 90% accuracy still lost money because one bad loan wiped out the margin of about forty good ones. Accuracy treats every error as equal, but the business never does.

Laid against my work, the four cells of the matrix look like this:

- **Case captured on time:** compliance met.
- **Missed reportable case:** late submission, possible inspection finding and, most importantly, a safety signal that may not be seen.
- **Irrelevant article flagged as relevant:** reviewer minutes lost.
- **Irrelevant article correctly excluded:** time saved.

Example from our QC data: In our literature screening QC auditing over a sample of ~2,500 articles screened last quarter, our baseline metrics showed an overall precision of 94.2% and a sensitivity of 99.1%. We identified 142 false positives (irrelevant articles flagged as relevant) which cost us roughly 12-15 reviewer hours to triage and discard, but we also identified 2 false negatives (missed safety-relevant cases). While a 99.1% sensitivity looks great on paper, those 2 missed cases represent an unacceptable regulatory risk. The costs of the two error types are nowhere near equal, yet our threshold discussions rarely say so explicitly.

## What was genuinely new to me

Asking who pays for each cell. A missed case is paid for by the patient, the QPPV, and the company's regulatory standing. A false positive is paid for by the screening team in extra effort. Because the people bearing each cost are different, they will push the decision threshold in different directions. The screening team wants fewer false positives, while the QPPV wants zero misses. The product manager has to make that trade-off visible and own the decision.

The banker example was also new to me. The bank manager is rewarded for loans given, but the defaults show up years later, after they have moved on. The PV parallel is a miss discovered in a later reconciliation or audit, long after the screening period's metrics were reported as green. If metrics close before errors surface, they reward the wrong thing.

## Applying it to something I am planning

Our portfolio spans nutraceuticals, cosmetics, herbal supplements, and OTC products. The warning given in class about feedback loops overfitting to one segment applies directly. A screening tool tuned mostly on one product category can look strong overall while performing poorly on another. Moving forward, I will report every metric split by product category, not as a single portfolio number.

I would also apply the point on statistical significance emphasized in class. If a QC error rate moves from 3% to 2%, I need to verify whether the sample size supports calling it a true improvement rather than random noise.

## Where I think it does not fully apply

Putting a monetary value on a missed case is uncomfortable, and in PV I don't think it is right. Patient harm is not a cost to be balanced against reviewer time, and no regulator would accept an "economically optimal miss rate." The professor noted that pharma decisions can be one-shot, and this is why. I would use a severity-weighted cost instead of money: a missed serious case weighted far above any number of false positives. That keeps the logic of the payoff matrix without pretending harm can be priced.

A/B testing is also harder for us. I cannot run two screening approaches on live cases unless both are validated. A parallel run inside validation is the closest equivalent, and it is slower and more expensive.

## What I will do differently

I will build a severity-weighted confusion matrix from our existing literature screening QC data, split across our product categories (cosmetics, nutraceuticals, OTC, etc.). Before reporting any QC quality improvement, I will verify that the sample size statistically supports the claim.
