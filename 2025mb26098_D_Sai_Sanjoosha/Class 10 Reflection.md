Scaling AI Products, Product Economics and Customer Promises

This session gave me a better understanding of what it actually means to scale an AI product. Earlier, I mainly associated scaling with increasing the number of users or improving the technical infrastructure. However, the discussion made it clear that scaling involves much more than handling increased traffic. It also includes managing costs, data availability, model performance, workflow complexity and customer expectations.
One of the important topics discussed was the economics of AI products. Unlike project-based work, where the cost and effort can be associated with a specific customer, a product is developed for a larger and sometimes unknown customer base. Therefore, the organisation needs to recover its development and operational costs across multiple users while maintaining profitability. This makes cost management an important responsibility for product managers. The increasing cost of AI usage, including model and token consumption, also means that product managers must carefully evaluate where and how AI is being used.
I found the discussion on controlled growth particularly useful. Increased usage is generally a positive sign because it indicates that customers find the product valuable. However, uncontrolled growth can create operational challenges, increase expenses and eventually affect customer satisfaction. A product manager must ensure that the organisation can support the growth without compromising the quality of service.
Another important concept was that every product is essentially a promise to its customers. Customers do not use a product because it has more servers or sophisticated technology; they use it because they expect it to solve a particular problem. For example, a customer using a cab-booking application expects a cab to arrive within the promised time. Even if the application itself works perfectly, the overall promise is not fulfilled if the driver does not accept the booking. This example helped me understand why product managers need to focus on the complete customer experience rather than only the technical components.
The session also covered the importance of versioning, traceability and rollback mechanisms. AI models, data and software components may change over time, and these changes can affect product performance. Maintaining proper versions and tracking experiments helps teams identify issues and recover when a new version does not perform as expected. The discussion also highlighted practical considerations such as latency, routing, capacity, rate limits, caching, privacy and recovery mechanisms.
My key takeaway: Scaling an AI product is not simply about increasing the number of users. It is about growing sustainably while maintaining product quality, controlling costs, managing technical complexity and consistently delivering the promise made to customers.
