Responsible AI, Ethics, Bias and Product Risk Management

This session helped me understand the importance of ethics and responsibility when developing and managing AI-based products. AI can create significant business value, but it can also introduce risks when the data, models or decisions are not evaluated properly. As product managers, we have a responsibility to ensure that the products we introduce are useful, fair and safe for the people who depend on them.
One of the major topics discussed was bias in AI systems. Bias does not always arise because someone intentionally designs an unfair system. It can also come from limited exposure, incomplete datasets, incorrect labelling or assumptions already present in historical data. For example, if a lending model is trained using historical data that reflects unfair assumptions about a particular group, the model may continue rejecting applications from that group even when individual applicants are eligible. This made me realise that AI can reproduce existing social and organisational biases if the data and outcomes are not examined carefully.
The discussion also highlighted that bias can exist in both traditional AI and generative AI. In traditional AI, product teams may have more control over the data and model development process. With generative AI, teams may depend on external providers whose training data and model development processes are not fully visible. This makes it important to understand the limitations of the technology being used rather than assuming that an AI-generated response is automatically reliable or neutral.
Another important learning was that responsible AI cannot be handled through a generic checklist. Concepts such as fairness, privacy and interpretability must be defined according to the specific product and its intended users. For example, fairness in a recruitment product would involve different considerations from fairness in a preventive maintenance application. Product teams need to identify the possible impact of incorrect or biased decisions and establish suitable monitoring and corrective mechanisms.
The session also discussed the importance of human oversight. AI systems should have ways to escalate uncertain or sensitive decisions to human reviewers. Even when human review is not required for every transaction, organisations can maintain a separate monitoring process to periodically evaluate the quality of AI outputs. The discussion on hallucinations was also useful because generative AI may sometimes invent information, policies or references that do not exist. A product should be designed to acknowledge uncertainty rather than always attempting to provide an answer.
Finally, I learned that responsible AI needs to be considered before deployment through a product-specific launch checklist. Product managers should evaluate intended use cases, possible misuse and abuse cases, potential harms, mitigation measures and the conditions under which a product should not be launched. This is an important management responsibility because product decisions involve uncertainty and affect customers, organisations and society.
My key takeaway: Responsible AI is not just a technical requirement or a compliance activity. It is an ongoing product management responsibility that requires awareness, judgement, monitoring and accountability throughout the product lifecycle.
