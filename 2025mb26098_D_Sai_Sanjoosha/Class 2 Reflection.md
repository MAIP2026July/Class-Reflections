Product Lifecycle and Decision-Making Frameworks

The second class helped me understand the different stages involved in product management and how a product evolves throughout its lifecycle. One of the major concepts discussed was the product lifecycle, which includes discovery, definition, strategy, planning, building, launching, and growing. Depending on the product's performance and relevance, it may eventually be renewed or retired.
I found the concept of treating the product lifecycle as a continuous learning process particularly interesting. Product development is not always a linear process where everything is planned and executed exactly as expected. Instead, products require multiple iterations based on customer feedback, changing market requirements, and learnings from previous releases. This made me understand why continuous improvement is essential for product success.
We also discussed several product management frameworks, including Jobs to Be Done, Double Diamond, Design Thinking, Opportunity Solution Trees, and Lean Canvas. I understood that these frameworks help product managers identify customer problems, explore possible solutions, evaluate alternatives, and make informed decisions. However, frameworks are only decision-support tools and should not replace actual managerial judgment.
Another important learning was about decision rights and responsibility allocation. Concepts such as RACI help clarify who is responsible, accountable, consulted, and informed during different activities. We also discussed product roadmaps and the importance of maintaining detailed plans for immediate activities while keeping longer-term plans more flexible.
The discussion on pricing foundations helped me understand that pricing decisions can be based on costs, customer willingness to pay, or the value delivered by the product.
My key takeaway: I learned that successful product management requires continuous learning, flexibility, and the ability to make decisions using the right frameworks. A product manager should not blindly follow a predefined process but should select the approach that best supports the decision at hand.
