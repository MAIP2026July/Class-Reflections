Topic: Project workflow on GitHub; writing a PRD in the stakeholder's own words; the "category killer, not a clone" mindset; data risk, bias, and consent; sentiment analysis as a multi-step problem

Watching a handful of real project pitches get reviewed live — a diabetes safety and doctor-consultation companion, a personal-fashion decision assistant, a complaints checker for automobile software — was more useful than any slide could have been, mostly because I could see in real time which ideas got waved through and which got a pointed question about who exactly the target user is. Our own team needs to tighten that before we submit.
The instruction to write requirements in the stakeholder's own voice — "I, as a rider, want..." rather than folding everyone's needs into one generic "user" — is a small change that I think will noticeably improve our documentation discipline. Same with the reminder that we're not building an Uber clone, we're building something meant to compete and win against it; "clone" as a word was banned for a reason, and I appreciated the directness of that correction.
The data governance section was the most sobering part of the day — the Apple Card story, where two people with near-identical financials got different credit outcomes and the difference blew up publicly, is a reminder that a biased dataset doesn't announce itself, it just quietly produces a headline later. Between that and the note on DPDP/GDPR obligations, I'm now looking at our own product's data retention practices with more suspicion than I did last week.
Last point, and I'll be honest about it rather than skip past it: this was also the session where the instructor repeated, pointedly, that he doesn't want AI-generated reflections — he wants ours. I've disclosed at the top of this document exactly where and how I used AI assistance this time around, and why, and I've tried to make sure the opinions, the discomforts, and the connections to my own work and family life above are genuinely mine and not manufactured. That tension — using the tool responsibly versus using it to fake effort — feels like exactly the kind of judgment call this whole course is trying to train us to make.
