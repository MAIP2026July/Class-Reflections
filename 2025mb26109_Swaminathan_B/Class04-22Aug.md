## Personal Reflection

This session fundamentally changed the way I think about AI products. Before attending the class, I often evaluated AI solutions mainly from a technology perspective. If a tool used AI and produced useful results, I assumed it was a good product. However, the discussion made me realize that technology alone does not create value. A product succeeds only when it solves a meaningful problem for a specific user in a practical and responsible way. 【1-d8f66a】

One of the most important lessons I learned was that not every problem requires AI. This sounds obvious, but in today's environment where almost every company wants to label its products as "AI-powered," it is easy to forget. The class emphasized that product managers must first identify the actual customer problem and then determine the simplest solution capable of addressing it. Sometimes that solution may be rules-based automation, analytics, or search rather than generative AI. This insight helped me understand that good product management requires discipline and not just enthusiasm for technology. 【1-d8f66a】

The discussion around social legitimacy made me think deeply. I had previously focused on whether a solution was technically feasible, but I rarely considered whether it should be built in the first place. The idea that products should be evaluated for ethical impact, societal acceptance, potential harm, and responsibility expanded my understanding of product management. I realized that product managers are not only responsible for building products but also for considering their broader consequences. 【1-d8f66a】

I found the agricultural advisory example particularly interesting because it highlighted the risks of AI-based recommendations. A wrong movie recommendation has minor consequences, but a wrong farming recommendation could affect a person's livelihood. This made me realize that product decisions cannot be evaluated only by average performance. Product managers must understand failure scenarios, edge cases, and the impact of incorrect outcomes. 【1-d8f66a】

One part of the class that strongly resonated with me was the discussion about customer adoption. During the session, I shared an example from my automotive experience where teams were hesitant to adopt AI-based tools even when they offered clear benefits. The instructor's explanation helped me understand that resistance to change is not always irrational. Customers evaluate products based on cost, convenience, risk, trust, familiarity, and workflow disruption. Looking back, I realized that many product failures occur not because the solution is bad, but because the transition cost is underestimated. 【1-d8f66a】

Another important insight was learning to look at products through the lens of customer psychology. The discussion around vehicle purchasing decisions and product adoption showed that people do not always make purely rational choices. Emotional factors, trust, habits, social status, and personal preferences influence decisions. This reminded me that successful products are designed for real people rather than idealized users. 【1-d8f66a】

I also reflected on the concept of Human-in-the-Loop systems. Before this class, I naturally assumed that greater automation meant a better product. However, the session highlighted situations where human judgment remains essential. In industries involving financial, medical, legal, or safety-critical decisions, complete automation may introduce unacceptable risks. I realized that the challenge is not to remove humans entirely but to determine where human judgment adds the most value. 【1-d8f66a】

The discussion about evidence-based product decisions was another valuable takeaway. Product managers are often passionate about their ideas, but passion alone is insufficient. Every assumption must eventually be validated through evidence, measurable outcomes, and real-world testing. This reinforced the importance of experimentation and structured decision-making. 【1-d8f66a】

I particularly connected with the idea that AI products should be evaluated based on outcomes rather than technical sophistication. A simple automation that saves customers meaningful time may create more value than a highly sophisticated AI system that introduces complexity without solving a real problem. This perspective helped me think more critically about the products and tools I evaluate in my professional work. 【1-d8f66a】

---

## Key Learnings

- AI should only be used when it creates meaningful customer value.
- Product managers must evaluate both benefits and potential harms.
- Social legitimacy and ethics are as important as technical feasibility.
- Simpler solutions should be considered before adopting complex AI systems.
- Customer adoption depends on trust, cost, habits, and workflow impact.
- Human-in-the-Loop systems remain important for high-risk decisions.
- Product decisions should be supported by evidence rather than assumptions.
- AI success should be measured by outcomes, not by technological complexity.
- Understanding customer psychology is critical for product-market fit.
- Product managers must think about both opportunity and accountability.

## Personal Takeaway

The biggest lesson I learned from this class is that AI product management is ultimately about judgment. Building an AI solution is only a small part of the challenge. The larger responsibility is deciding when AI should be used, how much autonomy it should receive, what risks exist, and whether it genuinely improves the user experience.

As someone working in the automotive domain, I could relate many concepts from the discussion to real-world challenges I have observed. The session encouraged me to think beyond features and models and focus on the actual value delivered to the customer. Going forward, I want to evaluate AI opportunities more critically and ensure that technology serves a clear business and user purpose.

## Conclusion

This session provided one of the most practical perspectives on AI product management so far. It encouraged me to move beyond the excitement surrounding AI and focus on evidence, customer value, risk management, and responsible decision-making. More importantly, it reinforced the idea that product managers are accountable not only for what they build but also for the consequences of what they choose to build.

The session helped me develop a more balanced view of AI products, and I believe these lessons will be useful both in my academic projects and in my professional career.
