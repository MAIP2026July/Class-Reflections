## Class 1 – What a Product Manager Really Owns (25 July 2026)

The first class went in a direction I did not expect. I came in thinking we would start with AI, and instead we spent almost the whole session arguing about what a product manager actually is.

Most of us in the class answered in the same safe way – "a bridge between business and engineering", "someone who gathers requirements", "someone who coordinates". Sir kept pushing and finally said the word we had all avoided: **ownership**. He asked us to think of ourselves as the CEO of the product. That line changed how the rest of the session landed for me.

The cricket examples made it stick. If the team wins, the captain takes the trophy even if he scored zero. If the captain scores a century and the team loses, he still lost. You cannot say "engineering failed" or "marketing didn't do their job" – all of it comes back to you. That is uncomfortable, but it is honest.

One classmate asked whether this is simply a thankless job. I liked that he asked it out loud, because I have felt the same in my own work. Sir's answer was that it depends on which lens you use – every appraisal cycle someone is unhappy, every school year children are uncomfortable, but that does not mean there is no progress. If you genuinely feel it is thankless, then it is time to move on rather than stay and be bitter.

**What I am taking away:**
- Accountability cannot be delegated; responsibility can.
- You will often have to influence people who do not report to you. That is the job, not a complaint about the job.
- Being the "postman" between teams is not product management. If I do not understand the domain, I have no business running the product.

**Where I disagree slightly:** in large organisations the product manager often genuinely does not control engineering or budget. Saying "the buck stops with you" is right in spirit, but in practice you also need the organisation to give you some room. I think the honest version is: own the outcome, and fight for the authority you need.

**How I will use this at work:** on the platform work I handle, I have been behaving like a coordinator. I want to start writing down the decisions I actually own versus the ones I have been quietly passing upward.

## Class 2 – The Product Life Cycle and the Artifacts (1 August 2026)

This class was about the full journey of a product – discover, define, strategise, plan, build, launch, grow, and then either renew or retire. The point that stayed with me is that this is not a waterfall. You go round and round, and rarely do you get it right the first time.

Two ideas hit home.

**First, the trustee idea.** We are spending somebody else's money, somebody else's time, somebody else's career. That is why we keep records and evidence – not to create paperwork, but because we are custodians. When a classmate asked about decision gates and misaligned expectations, sir's answer was very practical: defer the decisions that are cheap to defer (the paint colour of the room), but settle the structural ones up front (whether you are building a house or buying a flat). I have been guilty of the opposite – locking in technology choices early and leaving the hard stakeholder alignment for later.

**Second, "good enough".** The product will never be perfect. At some point you take a call and say it is good enough to launch. Sir shared his own story of a 1990s card processing product where the plan was development by April, beta in May, launch in June – and the CEO laughed at them. The actual launch happened the following February. His personal learning: beta is only halfway. Don't burn out your team before the second half starts.

He also introduced the difference between a **project and a product** – a project serves one customer, a product serves N unknown, unnamed customers. That distinction is going to matter a lot for our team assignment.

On artifacts – PRD, discovery notes, roadmaps, launch briefs, PR/FAQ – the message was that these are starting templates, not scripture. Adapt them. But the traceability point was strong: a decision that gets made but does not flow into design, code, testing and the user manual is not really a decision at all.

**Where I am unsure:** he wants everything recorded in GitHub as evidence. I like the discipline, but in a corporate setting a lot of my decision trail sits in email and Teams. I need to think about how to make that traceable without creating a second parallel system.

## Class 3 – Frameworks Are Dials, Not the Steering Wheel (18 August 2026)

This session started with two classmates recapping the earlier classes, and then turned into a walk through the framework toolkit – Jobs To Be Done, Double Diamond, Opportunity Solution Trees, Lean Canvas, Amazon's working-backwards and PR/FAQ, Porter's Five Forces, PESTLE, SWOT, Ansoff, QFD, RICE, Kano, MoSCoW, RACI/RAPID, one-way versus two-way doors, OODA.

That is a lot. But the framing I found most useful was the warning that came with it: **frameworks are your dials, not your steering wheel and not your brakes.** Don't stack them up. If a second framework only repeats the questions of the first and does not change the decision or the evidence, it is ceremony, not value.

Some specific things I want to remember:

- **Stop using the word "user".** Name them. A rider, a driver, a farmer, a parent. Once you name them, you can build a persona and ask what they are actually trying to get done.
- **Transformation versus improvement.** Getting a caterpillar to crawl faster is improvement. Becoming a butterfly is transformation. Most corporate "transformation" projects are 5% improvements with a nice label. He was not moralistic about it – call it whatever gets it funded – but be honest with yourself.
- **The moving target analogy.** Your product is a missile launched at a target that is itself moving, while competitors are firing at the same target. So course correction is not a failure, it is the normal state.
- **A different view of Scrum.** I have used Scrum for years and never heard it described this way. He called it a negotiation with your team: for one sprint, I will not jerk you around. After that, everything can change. And you can kill a sprint, but you cannot change it midway. More volatile environment, shorter sprint. More stable, longer. That reframing is genuinely useful to me.

The pricing point at the end was the one I wrote down twice: the product builder historically captures only about 15–25% of the value created, with 20% as a sensible default. The customer keeps the rest. If you plan your economics assuming you capture 100%, you are dreaming.

**Personal note:** the part where he talked about his company shutting down the week he returned from leave, and him spending his energy placing his team rather than himself, was not a framework at all. But it was probably the most important thing said in that class.

## Class 4 – Where and When Does AI Actually Create Value (22 August 2026)

This is the class where the subject finally became specifically about AI, and the structure was a set of gates.

**Gate 0 is social legitimacy**, and it comes *before* any technical fit question. Do we even have the standing to do this? Is there consent? Are vulnerable groups involved? Is the whole thing built on surveillance, manipulation or hidden labour? Only after that do we ask about value, feasibility and learning.

Then came the point I found genuinely useful for my day job – the **AI appropriateness ladder**:
1. Deterministic rules (if-then-else)
2. Retrieval, analytics and search
3. Predictive machine learning
4. Generative or agentic

Start at the bottom. If a rule solves it, a rule solves it. Don't default to a foundation model because the word AI is fashionable. His "nuclear powered pencil sharpener" example is crude but it works – you *can* build it, but should you? And if it costs ₹5,000 per sharpening, that is many times the cost of the pencil.

The **actionable time window** idea was the strongest part of the session for me. Telling someone it will rain one minute before it rains is useless. Telling a farmer two days in advance lets him arrange labour and harvest. And the autonomous driving example – detection time, plus alert time, plus human recognition time, plus decision time, plus braking distance, plus enough margin that the pedestrian isn't traumatised even if not hit – shows that "our model is accurate" is nowhere near the same as "our product works".

Two more things I want to carry:
- **Look at tail risk, not averages.** A low-probability event with generational consequences is not made acceptable by a good average.
- **Human-in-the-loop can become a rubber stamp.** His lending example, where the human and the AI decide in parallel and only disagreements get escalated, is a smarter design than showing the AI answer first and asking the human to confirm.

The discussion on adoption was also honest. People don't resist change because they are stupid; they resist because switching has a real cost – retraining, disrupted workflow, uncertainty about support. The 100-km-per-litre car example landed well. I would be tempted, but I would also ask where the service stations are.

## Class 5 – From Opportunity to Design for Uncertainty (29 August 2026)

The first half was about finding AI opportunities and the second half about designing for the fact that AI is not certain.

On opportunity signals – look for friction, trust gaps, high frequency, decision-heavy and data-rich workflows. But the hard question is always the same one: **where is the evidence?** Sir was clear that many of us are uncomfortable being asked to prove value rather than assert it, and that we need to get comfortable running experiments and recording results.

The example about the intranet screen stuck with me. His team wanted to build a full UI to manage group/division/company structure. He asked how often that changes. Answer: once in two or three years. So a back-end update is enough – no screen, no testing, no access permissions. Not everything is worth automating. Materiality matters.

The **build vs buy vs API/subscribe** discussion is directly relevant to what I do. Betting on an external API gets you started fast but hands control to a vendor. Open-weight models run in-house are "buy" in disguise – free to license, not free to operate. And the platform bet (OpenAI-style API versus Anthropic-style API) is a real lock-in choice, not a detail.

The second half – designing for uncertainty – is where I learned the most:
- Should the AI even be visible to the user? That is a product decision, not a default.
- What happens when confidence is low? Flag it, ask a clarifying question, or honestly say "I don't have an answer for you."
- Fallback behaviour and error communication have to be designed, not bolted on.
- The real risk is a UI that speaks with **false confidence**. Users then either over-trust it or, after being burnt, stop trusting it entirely.

His sharpest observation: in traditional software, the industry eventually forced developers to write test cases and show coverage. In the AI modelling world, that habit has not arrived yet. Ask your data scientist for their test cases for a generative feature and you will often get "I tried two prompts."

I also liked the prompt structure he gave – **Role, Context, Task, Constraints** – and his puzzlement about why no chat tool gives us four boxes to force that thinking. A prompt inside a product is not a one-off; it is part of the product promise and needs versioning like anything else.

## Class 6 – The Art of Reimagining, and Listening to Customers Properly (5 September 2026)

The class opened with the GitHub and topic-submission logistics, and then went somewhere much more interesting.

**The art of the possible.** If I don't know that computer vision can catch a mislabelled product, I cannot design a next-generation product around it. Thirty years ago an audiobook was economically silly; with text-to-speech it is trivial. So the constraint is often not the technology but our own imagination. His example of a company that printed, signed, scanned and emailed a document – then the next party printed, signed, scanned and emailed again – is painfully familiar. The rules we built for yesterday's constraints outlive the constraints.

I noted his very practical PRD advice too. Requirements should be written in the **stakeholder's language** – "I, as a rider, want…" – and the whole team must use one consistent vocabulary. "The system should handle 10,000 users" is not a requirement from a rider; that belongs in the specification document, not the PRD. And once you pick a term – rider, driver, right-giver, right-taker – everyone from engineering to customer support to the manual writers uses the same word.

The **black box reframing** was useful: keep the inputs and outputs of the step you are replacing constant, then rethink what happens inside. Maybe you now need only 12 of the 20 inputs. Maybe you need three old ones plus two new ones that were too expensive to collect before.

The second half on **sentiment analysis** was excellent and not what I expected. The point was that a thumbs-up/thumbs-down or a generic positive/negative score is useless to a product manager. An opinion is really a quintuple – the object, the feature, the sentiment value, the opinion holder, and the time – and often the context too ("camera is superb in daylight, hopeless at night"). One review can carry four different feature-level sentiments from two different people.

And the detail I would have completely missed: in the example review, the line "my mother was mad with me because I didn't tell her before I bought the phone" is not about any feature at all. It tells you something about the emotional weight of the purchase. Nobody says that about an ice cream. That is the human signal behind the product, and a feature-by-feature analysis would throw it away.

**Uncomfortable but fair reminder:** data consent. We are custodians, not owners. DPDP and GDPR are not optional, and an AI product built without thinking about consent is dead on arrival.

## Class 7 – A Full Case Worked Live: The Electricity Distribution Company (12 September 2026)

This was the last class before the midterm and it was run almost entirely as a live case, which I found far more useful than a slide walkthrough.

The setup: we are a product company, not the electricity board. We want to sell a customer-service product to distribution companies. Everyone in class offered an answer, and sir kept pushing each one further. The thing he corrected repeatedly was people slipping into **generic textbook answers** – "premium pricing", "brand positioning" – in a context where none of that applies. A DISCOM is not buying a luxury handbag. Nobody takes pride in owning a call-centre solution.

The reframing that made the whole thing click for me came from a classmate, and sir called it system thinking: the distribution company **buys power in advance on forward contracts**. If a locality loses power, they have already paid for electricity they now cannot sell. So detecting and fixing an outage fast is not just a customer-service nicety – it is directly protecting their margin. Suddenly the value proposition is quantifiable.

And then the best question of all: **why should the customer have to complain at all?** If consumption in an area drops abnormally, you already know. Push a message. Tell them before they call. If they do call, you know their number, their address, their meter – or their GPS location if they are elsewhere. Handled well, the call centre partly disappears. That is reimagining rather than optimising.

On pricing, the outcome-based model (pay per ticket resolved) was discussed with its downside made explicit: if you take outcome risk, you absorb the volatility of quiet weeks and flood days. And if you price per seat, your revenue shrinks as your customer gets better – which is a strange incentive to sign up for.

The framework I want to remember is **TFIR** – Truth, Facts, Inferences, Recommendations. Truth and facts can be made almost hallucination-free with database lookups and tool calls. Inferences are largely data-driven. Recommendations are the risky layer, and the way to make them trustworthy is to show the chain of evidence underneath so a human can spot when the foundation is wrong.

Other points I noted for the exam and beyond:
- Buy vs build vs subscribe carries a supply-chain risk we rarely model. If the OTP provider goes down, internet banking stops. If an AI vendor changes or deprecates a model, a launched and stable product can start misbehaving without any change on our side.
- With traditional software, tension drops sharply after GA. With AI products it only tapers, because external dependencies keep shifting.
- Give the demo yourself. If a product manager cannot demo their own product without help, that is a warning sign.
- Sit in customer support for a while after launch.

**Closing thought for myself:** the strongest theme across all seven classes is that product management is a long sequence of judgement calls made with incomplete information, and the job is to make more good calls than bad ones. The frameworks help. The evidence helps. But the decision, and the consequence, stays with me.
