## Class 1 – What a Product Manager Really Owns (25 July 2026)

The first class went in a direction I did not expect. I came in thinking we would start with AI, and instead we spent almost the whole session arguing about what a product manager actually is.

Most of us in the class answered in the same safe way – "a bridge between business and engineering", "someone who gathers requirements", "someone who coordinates". Sir kept pushing and finally said the word we had all avoided: **ownership**. He asked us to think of ourselves as the CEO of the product. That line changed how the rest of the session landed for me.

The cricket examples made it stick. If the team wins, the captain takes the trophy even if he scored zero. If the captain scores a century and the team loses, he still lost. You cannot say "engineering failed" or "marketing didn't do their job" – all of it comes back to you. That is uncomfortable, but it is honest.

One classmate asked whether this is simply a thankless job. I liked that he asked it out loud, because I have felt the same in my own work. Sir's answer was that it depends on which lens you use – every appraisal cycle someone is unhappy, every school year children are uncomfortable, but that does not mean there is no progress. If you genuinely feel it is thankless, then it is time to move on rather than stay and be bitter.

**What I am taking away:**
- Accountability cannot be delegated; responsibility can.
- You will often have to influence people who do not report to you. That is the job, not a complaint about the job.
- Being the "postman" between teams is not product management. If I do not understand the domain, I have no business running the product.

**Where I disagree slightly:** in large organisations the product manager often genuinely does not control engineering or budget. Saying "the buck stops with you" is right in spirit, but in practice you also need the organisation to give you some room. I think the honest version is: own the outcome, and fight for the authority you need.

**How I will use this at work:** on the platform work I handle, I have been behaving like a coordinator. I want to start writing down the decisions I actually own versus the ones I have been quietly passing upward.

## Class 2 – The Product Life Cycle and the Artifacts (1 August 2026)

This class was about the full journey of a product – discover, define, strategise, plan, build, launch, grow, and then either renew or retire. The point that stayed with me is that this is not a waterfall. You go round and round, and rarely do you get it right the first time.

Two ideas hit home.

**First, the trustee idea.** We are spending somebody else's money, somebody else's time, somebody else's career. That is why we keep records and evidence – not to create paperwork, but because we are custodians. When a classmate asked about decision gates and misaligned expectations, sir's answer was very practical: defer the decisions that are cheap to defer (the paint colour of the room), but settle the structural ones up front (whether you are building a house or buying a flat). I have been guilty of the opposite – locking in technology choices early and leaving the hard stakeholder alignment for later.

**Second, "good enough".** The product will never be perfect. At some point you take a call and say it is good enough to launch. Sir shared his own story of a 1990s card processing product where the plan was development by April, beta in May, launch in June – and the CEO laughed at them. The actual launch happened the following February. His personal learning: beta is only halfway. Don't burn out your team before the second half starts.

He also introduced the difference between a **project and a product** – a project serves one customer, a product serves N unknown, unnamed customers. That distinction is going to matter a lot for our team assignment.

On artifacts – PRD, discovery notes, roadmaps, launch briefs, PR/FAQ – the message was that these are starting templates, not scripture. Adapt them. But the traceability point was strong: a decision that gets made but does not flow into design, code, testing and the user manual is not really a decision at all.

**Where I am unsure:** he wants everything recorded in GitHub as evidence. I like the discipline, but in a corporate setting a lot of my decision trail sits in email and Teams. I need to think about how to make that traceable without creating a second parallel system.

## Class 3 – Frameworks Are Dials, Not the Steering Wheel (18 August 2026)

This session started with two classmates recapping the earlier classes, and then turned into a walk through the framework toolkit – Jobs To Be Done, Double Diamond, Opportunity Solution Trees, Lean Canvas, Amazon's working-backwards and PR/FAQ, Porter's Five Forces, PESTLE, SWOT, Ansoff, QFD, RICE, Kano, MoSCoW, RACI/RAPID, one-way versus two-way doors, OODA.

That is a lot. But the framing I found most useful was the warning that came with it: **frameworks are your dials, not your steering wheel and not your brakes.** Don't stack them up. If a second framework only repeats the questions of the first and does not change the decision or the evidence, it is ceremony, not value.

Some specific things I want to remember:

- **Stop using the word "user".** Name them. A rider, a driver, a farmer, a parent. Once you name them, you can build a persona and ask what they are actually trying to get done.
- **Transformation versus improvement.** Getting a caterpillar to crawl faster is improvement. Becoming a butterfly is transformation. Most corporate "transformation" projects are 5% improvements with a nice label. He was not moralistic about it – call it whatever gets it funded – but be honest with yourself.
- **The moving target analogy.** Your product is a missile launched at a target that is itself moving, while competitors are firing at the same target. So course correction is not a failure, it is the normal state.
- **A different view of Scrum.** I have used Scrum for years and never heard it described this way. He called it a negotiation with your team: for one sprint, I will not jerk you around. After that, everything can change. And you can kill a sprint, but you cannot change it midway. More volatile environment, shorter sprint. More stable, longer. That reframing is genuinely useful to me.

The pricing point at the end was the one I wrote down twice: the product builder historically captures only about 15–25% of the value created, with 20% as a sensible default. The customer keeps the rest. If you plan your economics assuming you capture 100%, you are dreaming.

**Personal note:** the part where he talked about his company shutting down the week he returned from leave, and him spending his energy placing his team rather than himself, was not a framework at all. But it was probably the most important thing said in that class.

## Class 4 – Where and When Does AI Actually Create Value (22 August 2026)

This is the class where the subject finally became specifically about AI, and the structure was a set of gates.

**Gate 0 is social legitimacy**, and it comes *before* any technical fit question. Do we even have the standing to do this? Is there consent? Are vulnerable groups involved? Is the whole thing built on surveillance, manipulation or hidden labour? Only after that do we ask about value, feasibility and learning.

Then came the point I found genuinely useful for my day job – the **AI appropriateness ladder**:
1. Deterministic rules (if-then-else)
2. Retrieval, analytics and search
3. Predictive machine learning
4. Generative or agentic

Start at the bottom. If a rule solves it, a rule solves it. Don't default to a foundation model because the word AI is fashionable. His "nuclear powered pencil sharpener" example is crude but it works – you *can* build it, but should you? And if it costs ₹5,000 per sharpening, that is many times the cost of the pencil.

The **actionable time window** idea was the strongest part of the session for me. Telling someone it will rain one minute before it rains is useless. Telling a farmer two days in advance lets him arrange labour and harvest. And the autonomous driving example – detection time, plus alert time, plus human recognition time, plus decision time, plus braking distance, plus enough margin that the pedestrian isn't traumatised even if not hit – shows that "our model is accurate" is nowhere near the same as "our product works".

Two more things I want to carry:
- **Look at tail risk, not averages.** A low-probability event with generational consequences is not made acceptable by a good average.
- **Human-in-the-loop can become a rubber stamp.** His lending example, where the human and the AI decide in parallel and only disagreements get escalated, is a smarter design than showing the AI answer first and asking the human to confirm.

The discussion on adoption was also honest. People don't resist change because they are stupid; they resist because switching has a real cost – retraining, disrupted workflow, uncertainty about support. The 100-km-per-litre car example landed well. I would be tempted, but I would also ask where the service stations are.

## Class 5 – From Opportunity to Design for Uncertainty (29 August 2026)

The first half was about finding AI opportunities and the second half about designing for the fact that AI is not certain.

On opportunity signals – look for friction, trust gaps, high frequency, decision-heavy and data-rich workflows. But the hard question is always the same one: **where is the evidence?** Sir was clear that many of us are uncomfortable being asked to prove value rather than assert it, and that we need to get comfortable running experiments and recording results.

The example about the intranet screen stuck with me. His team wanted to build a full UI to manage group/division/company structure. He asked how often that changes. Answer: once in two or three years. So a back-end update is enough – no screen, no testing, no access permissions. Not everything is worth automating. Materiality matters.

The **build vs buy vs API/subscribe** discussion is directly relevant to what I do. Betting on an external API gets you started fast but hands control to a vendor. Open-weight models run in-house are "buy" in disguise – free to license, not free to operate. And the platform bet (OpenAI-style API versus Anthropic-style API) is a real lock-in choice, not a detail.

The second half – designing for uncertainty – is where I learned the most:
- Should the AI even be visible to the user? That is a product decision, not a default.
- What happens when confidence is low? Flag it, ask a clarifying question, or honestly say "I don't have an answer for you."
- Fallback behaviour and error communication have to be designed, not bolted on.
- The real risk is a UI that speaks with **false confidence**. Users then either over-trust it or, after being burnt, stop trusting it entirely.

His sharpest observation: in traditional software, the industry eventually forced developers to write test cases and show coverage. In the AI modelling world, that habit has not arrived yet. Ask your data scientist for their test cases for a generative feature and you will often get "I tried two prompts."

I also liked the prompt structure he gave – **Role, Context, Task, Constraints** – and his puzzlement about why no chat tool gives us four boxes to force that thinking. A prompt inside a product is not a one-off; it is part of the product promise and needs versioning like anything else.

## Class 6 – The Art of Reimagining, and Listening to Customers Properly (5 September 2026)

The class opened with the GitHub and topic-submission logistics, and then went somewhere much more interesting.

**The art of the possible.** If I don't know that computer vision can catch a mislabelled product, I cannot design a next-generation product around it. Thirty years ago an audiobook was economically silly; with text-to-speech it is trivial. So the constraint is often not the technology but our own imagination. His example of a company that printed, signed, scanned and emailed a document – then the next party printed, signed, scanned and emailed again – is painfully familiar. The rules we built for yesterday's constraints outlive the constraints.

I noted his very practical PRD advice too. Requirements should be written in the **stakeholder's language** – "I, as a rider, want…" – and the whole team must use one consistent vocabulary. "The system should handle 10,000 users" is not a requirement from a rider; that belongs in the specification document, not the PRD. And once you pick a term – rider, driver, right-giver, right-taker – everyone from engineering to customer support to the manual writers uses the same word.

The **black box reframing** was useful: keep the inputs and outputs of the step you are replacing constant, then rethink what happens inside. Maybe you now need only 12 of the 20 inputs. Maybe you need three old ones plus two new ones that were too expensive to collect before.

The second half on **sentiment analysis** was excellent and not what I expected. The point was that a thumbs-up/thumbs-down or a generic positive/negative score is useless to a product manager. An opinion is really a quintuple – the object, the feature, the sentiment value, the opinion holder, and the time – and often the context too ("camera is superb in daylight, hopeless at night"). One review can carry four different feature-level sentiments from two different people.

And the detail I would have completely missed: in the example review, the line "my mother was mad with me because I didn't tell her before I bought the phone" is not about any feature at all. It tells you something about the emotional weight of the purchase. Nobody says that about an ice cream. That is the human signal behind the product, and a feature-by-feature analysis would throw it away.

**Uncomfortable but fair reminder:** data consent. We are custodians, not owners. DPDP and GDPR are not optional, and an AI product built without thinking about consent is dead on arrival.

## Class 7 – A Full Case Worked Live: The Electricity Distribution Company (12 September 2026)

This was the last class before the midterm and it was run almost entirely as a live case, which I found far more useful than a slide walkthrough.

The setup: we are a product company, not the electricity board. We want to sell a customer-service product to distribution companies. Everyone in class offered an answer, and sir kept pushing each one further. The thing he corrected repeatedly was people slipping into **generic textbook answers** – "premium pricing", "brand positioning" – in a context where none of that applies. A DISCOM is not buying a luxury handbag. Nobody takes pride in owning a call-centre solution.

The reframing that made the whole thing click for me came from a classmate, and sir called it system thinking: the distribution company **buys power in advance on forward contracts**. If a locality loses power, they have already paid for electricity they now cannot sell. So detecting and fixing an outage fast is not just a customer-service nicety – it is directly protecting their margin. Suddenly the value proposition is quantifiable.

And then the best question of all: **why should the customer have to complain at all?** If consumption in an area drops abnormally, you already know. Push a message. Tell them before they call. If they do call, you know their number, their address, their meter – or their GPS location if they are elsewhere. Handled well, the call centre partly disappears. That is reimagining rather than optimising.

On pricing, the outcome-based model (pay per ticket resolved) was discussed with its downside made explicit: if you take outcome risk, you absorb the volatility of quiet weeks and flood days. And if you price per seat, your revenue shrinks as your customer gets better – which is a strange incentive to sign up for.

The framework I want to remember is **TFIR** – Truth, Facts, Inferences, Recommendations. Truth and facts can be made almost hallucination-free with database lookups and tool calls. Inferences are largely data-driven. Recommendations are the risky layer, and the way to make them trustworthy is to show the chain of evidence underneath so a human can spot when the foundation is wrong.

Other points I noted for the exam and beyond:
- Buy vs build vs subscribe carries a supply-chain risk we rarely model. If the OTP provider goes down, internet banking stops. If an AI vendor changes or deprecates a model, a launched and stable product can start misbehaving without any change on our side.
- With traditional software, tension drops sharply after GA. With AI products it only tapers, because external dependencies keep shifting.
- Give the demo yourself. If a product manager cannot demo their own product without help, that is a warning sign.
- Sit in customer support for a while after launch.

**Closing thought for myself:** this case showed me that the real value often sits one level above the problem you were asked to solve. Optimising the call centre was the obvious answer; questioning why the customer needs to call at all was the better one.

## Class 8 – Prototype, MVP and Earning the Right to the Next Gate (3 October 2026)

This was the first class after the midterm and it opened with something I have felt in my own work but never named properly: **provider instability**. Sir separated two things. One is the normal probabilistic behaviour of a model – if it is wrong 5% of the time, it should stay roughly 5% wrong. The other is the vendor quietly changing something behind the scenes, so the same harness and the same prompt suddenly behave very differently from one week to the next. His example of asking an agent to "work continuously towards the goal" and it still stopping after one step (the Kannada saying about the donkey that moves one step per beating) made the whole class laugh, but the point was serious. In development you can roll back. In production you don't even know which customer got the weak answer.

The second idea I liked was **assumption drift**. He told us how he asked the AI to test with three banks, and it silently decided there must always be exactly three banks. When he later tested with one bank, it failed. That is exactly the kind of thing that slips past when you are approving AI output quickly. His warning was that the product manager becomes the bottleneck, and the natural reaction is to say "fine, go ahead" to everything – which is how these wrong assumptions get baked in.

The main content was the ladder from **concept → prototype → MVP → pilot → scale**, and the rule that a team should not move to the next stage without earning it. A prototype is for learning and demos; an MVP is for a real customer to use and give feedback. I noted the different prototype types (concept, UI, data, workflow) and the warning about "Wizard of Oz" MVPs where a human is secretly doing the work. Using a human in the loop is fine; hiding it from the customer is not.

**Points I want to carry forward:**
- Keep vendors pluggable. Today there are broadly two API styles (OpenAI-style and Anthropic-style); design so you can switch.
- Write a short prototype/MVP brief with the success criteria up front, so the team doesn't feel the rules changed midway.
- Learn before you automate. Stage the automation and keep manual fallbacks while confidence is low.
- Right-size the governance. Jaydeep's comment about ISO/CMM paperwork made the point – process should help, not become theatre.
- Never build for one customer. When a classmate said the vendor must not sell the tool to anyone else, sir pushed back: make a generic core and put the customer-specific rules in an add-on.

**How this connects to my work:** I have seen the "same prompt, different behaviour" problem with our chatbot work. I now want to keep a small fixed test set and rerun it whenever a model or provider changes, instead of relying on how it "feels".

## Class 9 – Metrics That a Business Person Can Trust (10 October 2026)

This class was about evaluation and metrics, and the part that stayed with me was the **loan payoff matrix** that sir built live in Excel. A ₹1 lakh loan, 2.5% spread for the bank, and a default meaning the full principal is lost. He kept changing the numbers in the four cells and showed that the model with 90%+ accuracy could still lose money, while a model with lower accuracy could be profitable – because one bad loan wipes out the margin from about 40 good loans. Accuracy alone told us nothing about the business.

Jaydeep argued that recall is the right metric here. Sir's answer was that he deliberately avoids preaching precision or recall; he wants us to put a rupee value on each cell – who gains and who pays – and decide like a business person. I think both are right, but as an MBA student I found the economic view more useful because it cannot be "gamed" as easily and it directly tells you how much you can afford to pay for a better model.

Other points I noted:
- **Tail risk beats averages.** Jaydeep's story about the California wildfires bankrupting insurers made it real. The casino example also stuck – probability assumes you can keep playing forever, but if you run out of money, the game is over.
- **Metrics must evolve with the product.** Revenue may be growing while new customer sign-ups are falling. If your metrics haven't changed since launch, ask what you are blind to.
- **Who pays for false positives and false negatives** changes everyone's motivation. Banks invested in fraud detection seriously only after regulators put the liability on them.
- **Measure the right behaviour.** Rewarding testers by number of bugs found can make them hold back defects.
- **A/B testing with AI is harder** because the vendor may be A/B testing you at the same time. Before celebrating "customer satisfaction went from 4.5 to 4.8", check the sample size and whether the change is actually significant.

**Where I disagree slightly:** sir said keep the metric set small, start with three. I agree, but for AI products I think at least one of the three has to be a quality/safety metric, otherwise cost and speed will always win the argument.

**How I will use this:** for the finance chatbot work, I will try to express the impact of a wrong answer in money or time lost, rather than reporting only an accuracy percentage.

## Class 10 – Scaling Is About Keeping the Promise (17 October 2026)

The class started with a hard reminder about the grading – strict bell curve, average marks around 70%, no late submissions. After that, the topic was scaling AI products, and the line I wrote in capitals was: **"If your teams are talking servers and not promises, you are not doing a great job."**

Sir's view is that every product is a promise, and that promise gets rewritten the moment the feature becomes mission-critical. WhatsApp is free, yet people get angry when it is down for an hour. Terms and conditions that say "no guarantees" will not save you, because customers and courts judge you by what they have come to expect. So he suggested a **customer promise matrix** – what exactly are we promising each segment (like Uber's "a cab in three minutes"), and are we monitoring that promise rather than just server uptime.

The **latency–cost–quality trade-off** was the core. Sir was clear that it is the product manager's job to define the acceptable range for each – for example, "answer in under 45 seconds, minimum quality of X, cost between A and B" – and to communicate that window to the team. Otherwise one person shouts about latency and someone switches to a small model; someone else complains about quality and they jump to a frontier model. I also liked his own example of choosing **predictability over speed** – adding a few seconds of clean-up so the response time became steady.

Other things I want to remember:
- Version not just code, but data, models, prompts and customer segments. Rollback is not always possible – the old model may be retired by the vendor.
- **Rate limits and queues** only show up once you are successful. Then you need tiers (the Vande Bharat vs passenger train example on the same track), fair-use rules and monitoring by segment.
- Watch for noisy neighbours in multi-tenant systems.
- Use smaller models where they are enough, and routing/caching to keep margins – but check privacy before caching one user's answer for another.
- Don't depend only on heroics. AI products are a marathon; use "commandos" and "army" style people for the right jobs.

**Personal connection:** the rate-limit discussion was very close to an issue I faced recently, where a connection limit was reached and new sessions failed straight away instead of waiting in a queue. That is exactly the "promise" problem – for the user, a failure and a 30-second wait feel completely different.

**Exercise sir asked for:** list use cases where a small model is enough versus where you need a frontier model. My quick answer: classification, routing, extracting fields and fixed-format summaries can go to small models; open-ended reasoning, multi-step planning and anything customer-facing with legal or financial impact needs the larger model plus human review.

## Class 11 – Ethics, Bias, Privacy and Governance That Doesn't Become Bureaucracy (24 October 2026)

The class started with the new lab (Prayogshala) and sir's clear guidance on where it helps – mainly for traditional ML model building, not for agentic product development or embedding generative AI. Then we moved to ethics, risk and responsible AI.

His explanation of **bias** was the simplest I have heard. A small child who has only seen Gurkha security guards assumes all guards are Gurkhas. The child isn't bad; the exposure was limited. AI trained on limited data behaves the same way. Most bias is not deliberate – it comes from data, labelling, policies and workflows. Being aware is the first step. When Gautam asked whether there is one standard framework, sir's honest answer was no – follow the laws and industry guidelines as a minimum, and try to do better, because even the guidelines can be biased or outdated.

On **privacy**, the rule was simple: collect the minimum you can defend in front of a regulator. If you don't ask for age, gender or even name, the AI cannot use them. Consent and purpose must be stated. Health data and children's data are extra sensitive. Under DPDP (and more strictly GDPR) we are trustees, not owners – if the person asks for deletion, we delete. His phrase "data is a drug" is accurate; teams always want to collect more.

On **hallucination**, the practical controls were RAG grounding, limiting the choices the model can pick from, and designing the product to **answer, abstain or escalate**. Abstaining is fine – and it also makes pricing fair, because you can choose not to charge for "no answer".

On **governance**, two lines I want to keep:
- Governance must scale faster than the number of AI agents you deploy.
- Committees recommend; the product owner remains accountable.

He also warned that governance becomes theatre if people can't explain its value, and that reviewers must be given real time and credit – "free labour" reviews quickly become rubber stamps. For **risk**, beyond probability and severity, also ask whether the issue is detectable and whether it is reversible. And have an incident communication plan – his example of "if you haven't received a mail by 7 a.m., you are safe" was about not spreading fear.

**For the project:** we were asked to add a section on what data we collect, consent, retention, the regulator for our industry, and a launch gate checklist with minimum criteria and blocking criteria – all in the context of our product, not motherhood statements.

**Personal note:** sir again said "never use the word *user*". I have now started noticing how often I write "business users" in my own documents. I need to name the actual role.

## Class 12 – Project Review, Diagrams as Code, and Monetization (31 October 2026)

The first half was live feedback on one team's submission, and it was useful for everyone. The message was: **write your PRD as if it is a contract** you are handing to a vendor with your own money at stake. Any vague word – "accurate", "user-friendly", "reduction in time", "unsafe output", "high risk" – will be interpreted by the vendor in their favour. Put numbers ("30% reduction"), and add a separate "target value" column so you only change the number later, not the description. Hard-coded lists (like a fixed set of 12 medicines) are not future-proof. Workflows must be defined now, even if they are revised after the pilot, and the places where AI is used must be marked – reliability, cost, how uncertainty is shown, and where the human is in the loop.

He also suggested a self-check I am going to use: **feed your document to an AI and ask whether it can build it, and what clarifications it needs first.** If it needs a lot of clarifications, so will your vendor.

I learned about **PlantUML and Mermaid** – diagrams as code – and **swimlane diagrams** where each lane is a role (patient, doctor, system, AI). The diff-friendly nature of text diagrams in Git is a real advantage. He also introduced the **C4 model** and said a product manager mainly needs the system context and container levels. Submissions should be in Markdown (for AI to read) with PDF for humans.

The second half was **monetization**, and the key ideas were:
- **Target costing** (the Japanese approach): start from what the customer will pay, subtract your margin, and that is your maximum build cost – not cost plus profit.
- Customers value **outputs**; your costs are **inputs**. Customers prefer outcome-based pricing; vendors prefer input-based (like T&M). Whoever can manage the outcome risk can win more business.
- If customers love the product and use it more, inference cost can explode. **Stress test the pricing** for heavy users.
- Don't be greedy with low-usage customers – sometimes suggest a downgrade to keep them.
- **Architecture follows economics.** His Oracle examples – routing all DB calls through one module because licensing was per connection, consolidating databases because licensing was per CPU core – showed why he says architects should understand finance.
- Know the contribution margin by workflow path and by customer segment, and be able to explain unit economics in one minute to a VC.
- Platforms and ecosystems compete as groups; network value grows fast with every added user.

**Personal connection:** the Oracle licensing example felt very familiar. Licence and connection limits on data platforms do shape how we design access, and I have seen the cost side drive architecture decisions more than technical preference.

## Class 13 – Project Presentations, Day 1 (14 November 2026)

This was the first presentation session, and sir treated it as a **VC pitch**, not a product demo. The rule was that feedback given to any team is feedback for every team – we each raise it as a GitHub issue for our own project and close it with a comment if it is not applicable.

The common feedback points I noted, which apply to my own team's work as well:
- **Swimlane with roles**, and AI steps marked separately (AI1, AI2…) with the human-in-the-loop clearly shown, including where outputs are gated before reaching the end person.
- State whether AI is used in all three pillars – for PM artefacts, for building, and inside the product.
- **Who pays for the AI** – does the customer bring their own API key or does the vendor absorb the cost?
- **Two separate calculations:** (1) the startup's own business plan – build cost, number of customers, revenue and profit year by year, to convince a VC; (2) the customer's value calculation – what they pay versus what they save. Many teams, including the ones with good products, were mixing these up.
- A customer typically shares about 18–25% of the value they gain, so they expect roughly 3–4 times the price back in savings. If you don't know your industry's number, use this as a starting point.
- **Stress test the numbers** – for example, what if the same work is outsourced to a lower-cost country? Is the customer still better off buying from you?
- Plan for a **conditional go-ahead** after the pilot, not just complete success or complete failure.
- **A product is not a project.** If it is built for one organisation, it is not a product. This was said very firmly.
- Think about the four pillars: domain, technology feasibility, regulator, and consumer behaviour (positive and negative motivation to change).

The most interesting moment was when sir decided to use the Idea Hub team's product to evaluate all the GitHub submissions – "dog eat dog food". He allowed them to tune it for the course but not for individual teams, because then it becomes a project again. It was a neat live lesson on how generic a product really is.

**Reflection on myself:** watching the pricing discussions, I realised I am comfortable with the technical side of cost (tokens, infrastructure) but less practised at the customer-side ROI story. That is the part I need to strengthen.

## Class 14 – Project Presentations, Day 2 (21 November 2026)

The second presentation session was long and ran partly while sir was on the road, which itself said something about his commitment. The feedback became sharper and more personal, and I found it more useful than a lecture.

Themes that came through again and again:
- **Be ambitious and transform, not just optimise.** For the school admissions product, his question was why a human should answer simple questions like fees or term dates at all – let AI answer reliably and only escalate the rest.
- **Check the AI economics properly.** When a team claimed less than ₹1 per call, sir pushed back – RAG, re-ranking, thinking tokens and retries all add cost. Build a prototype and benchmark cost, latency and accuracy before promising a price.
- **Think about the multiplier.** For the wardrobe assistant, he said the real target is the value created for clothing brands and exporters, not one family's convenience.
- **Your hidden competitor is self-build.** A customer can ask "why not just use a Claude skill or a subscription?" If the product is a thin wrapper on a prompt, a model provider can add the feature and wipe you out.
- **Finding issues won't sell anymore – fixing or preventing them will.** Like the red underline in an IDE, people want the mistake stopped before it happens.
- **Pick a narrow segment and win it first.** For the digital twin team: don't promise a twin for everyone; make one role, one course or one function work very well. Hidden organisational knowledge – short forms, local terms – is not in any document.
- **"Less for a lot less."** Sometimes you strip features out to serve a segment the big player over-serves.
- **Partner, don't fight.** For the screen-security product, positioning it as an add-on to existing VDI tools is easier than competing with them – while being aware of the risk that the partner builds the same feature.
- **Check third-party licensing early.** Open models or libraries can be free for open-source use but paid for commercial products; until that cost is known, you cannot price your product.
- **Validate value with real people.** For the sales document generator, sir asked for salespeople willing to accept a higher target in exchange for the tool – that is real proof of value.
- **Show conviction.** Never introduce your idea as "something I just thought of". And anyone in the team must be able to present any part – a VC may invite only one person into the room.

**Final reflection for myself:** across these two days, the gap I saw most often – and I include myself – was not the technology. Most teams could build something. The gap was positioning, unit economics and proof of value. That matches the whole thread of this course: AI is the means; the promise, the customer and the money are the product.

## Overall Closing Thought

The strongest theme across all fourteen classes is that product management is a long sequence of judgement calls made with incomplete information, and the job is to make more good calls than bad ones. The frameworks help. The evidence helps. The metrics help, as long as they are in rupees and not only percentages. But the decision, and the consequence, stays with me.
