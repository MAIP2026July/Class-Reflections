# Class Reflection — 24 October 2026

One part of this class that made me think was the professor's explanation that bias is not always deliberate. Sometimes we make a decision based on the limited examples we have seen, without realising what is missing. An AI model can do the same when the data used to train or evaluate it does not represent the people or situations where it will actually be used.

I found this relevant to my role as a team lead. When we assess engineering candidates, there can be a temptation to rely heavily on familiar qualifications, previous job titles or particular keywords. If an AI-based screening tool were used in that process, I would want to know whether it was overlooking people who have genuine practical ability but a different background. A convenient automated shortlist is not necessarily a fair or good shortlist.

The discussion also reminded me that responsible AI is not completed by writing 'privacy' or 'fairness' in a checklist. We have to define what those words mean in the actual product. Who can be harmed by a wrong result? What personal information is genuinely required? How can somebody challenge or correct an automated decision? These questions are more useful than a general statement that our system is ethical.

My practical takeaway is to ask about missing data, unintended impact and the option for human review whenever AI is proposed for an important decision. **I cannot assume that a model is fair just because nobody intentionally designed it to be unfair.**
