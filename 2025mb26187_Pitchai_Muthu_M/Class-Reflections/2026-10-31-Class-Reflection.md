# Class Reflection — 31 October 2026

The most practical learning for me from this session was the importance of removing ambiguity from product requirements. The professor pointed out how words such as 'accurate', 'user-friendly' or 'faster' can mean different things to different people. This sounds simple, but I can relate it directly to the discussions that happen between development, QA, support and customers.

For example, if I say an embedded software release should be 'stable', the development team may understand that it boots and passes the main tests. QA may expect repeated stress testing, while a customer may expect it to work continuously under their operating conditions. Unless we define the test conditions and acceptance criteria, everyone can sincerely believe they have met the requirement and still disagree at delivery time.

I also liked the demonstration of swimlane diagrams using PlantUML or Mermaid. A workflow diagram is not only for presentation. It can show who is responsible for each action, where the system makes an automatic decision, where the outcome is uncertain and when a person needs to step in. That is especially useful when several teams are involved in one product or release.

One change I want to make is to replace vague expectations with measurable acceptance criteria and a simple workflow before work begins. Even a first version of the workflow is better than leaving important decisions until later. **A clear requirement may take extra effort at the beginning, but it can save much more time during implementation, testing and customer discussions.**
