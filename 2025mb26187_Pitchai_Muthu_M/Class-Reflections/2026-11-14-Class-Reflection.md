# Class Reflection — 14 November 2026

The team presentations in this session made me think about the difference between building a working solution and building a product that someone is willing to pay for. The professor repeatedly asked simple questions: Who is the customer? Who is the user? What value are they getting? And why should an investor support this product? I felt these questions were more difficult than explaining technical features.

I could relate this to my embedded software work. When we complete an Android BSP release or improve a tool such as BSP Launcher, we usually discuss build stability, validation results, supported features and technical issues. These are important, but they don't fully explain the business value. For example, if a tool reduces the time a customer spends setting up an environment, the benefit is not only a working tool. It is the engineering hours saved, fewer repeated support requests and a faster start for the customer's development. I need to learn to express that benefit with numbers, not only technical terms.

Another point that stayed with me was the difference between **the customer's economics and the product owner's economics**. A customer may save time and money, but the company building the product must also recover development, maintenance and AI service costs. A product may look attractive from one side and still fail on the other. The discussion about testing different price and cost assumptions reminded me that a business plan should also describe what happens when the first pilot is only partly successful.

I also found the GitHub feedback approach useful. The professor asked teams to record even the feedback given to other teams and explicitly close an issue if it is not applicable. This is similar to how I would like to manage review comments, release risks and customer observations within my team. I liked the idea of making the decisions traceable, although I would keep the process lightweight for a small engineering team.

My main takeaway is that I should think beyond **"Can we build it?"** and ask **"Can we repeat it for other customers, can we prove the value, and can both the customer and the product business benefit?"** I want to apply this thinking whenever we propose a reusable software capability rather than treating each customer request as a one-time development activity.
