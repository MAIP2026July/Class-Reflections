## **MANAGEMENT OF AI PRODUCTS** 

# **Session 2 – Product Lifecycle, Discovery and Strategy** 

The second session deepened my understanding of product management as a continuous learning process. The product lifecycle discussed in class—discover, define, strategise, plan, build, launch, grow and eventually renew or retire—showed me that a product is not developed through a one-time, linear process. It evolves through feedback, iterations and decisions taken at different stages. A product manager must be willing to revisit assumptions and make course corrections. 

A key distinction I found valuable was the difference between a project and a product. A project may begin with the requirement of one known customer, but a product needs to address a problem that can be relevant to a wider set of potential customers. This means that discovery should go beyond one conversation. We need to understand the problem, alternatives, market, customer segments and the people who use and purchase the product. The buyer and the end user may not always be the same person, so understanding both is important. 

The discussion on strategy also highlighted that choosing where to play is as important as deciding what to build. A product manager must define the target segment, geography and scope, including what the product will not address. Since time, money and resources are limited, prioritisation is unavoidable. Some requirements may be mandatory because of regulation or business needs, while others can be prioritised based on expected value. 

Another important learning was accountability. A product manager is a creator, but also a trustee of other people's money, time and resources. Decisions therefore need evidence and a clear rationale. I also took away that an AI product manager does not necessarily need to be a technical expert, but should have sufficient AI fluency to understand discussions, ask relevant questions and connect technical possibilities with business needs. 

Personally, this session reminded me to look at product decisions from both customer-value and business-accountability perspectives. In my professional role, I often work with customer requirements and renewal commitments. The session encouraged me to think more broadly about the underlying problem, the larger market opportunity and the evidence behind a decision. The most important takeaway for me is that product management is about learning continuously, prioritising responsibly and creating value for a clearly understood group of stakeholders.
