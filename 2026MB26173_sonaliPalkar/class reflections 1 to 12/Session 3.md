## **MANAGEMENT OF AI PRODUCTS** 

# **Session 3 – Product Management Frameworks, Value and Launch** 

The third session helped me connect the earlier discussions on product ownership and lifecycle with the practical use of product-management frameworks. During the recap, the importance of traceability, evidence-based decisions and focusing on progress rather than simply adding features stood out. A traceability matrix is not just documentation; it helps explain why a decision was taken, what evidence supported it and what can be learned from the outcome. It also reinforces accountability for the resources entrusted to a product team. 

I found the discussion on understanding users particularly useful. Terms such as “user” or “customer” can be too broad unless we specify who we mean. Product managers should identify the relevant user groups and understand their context, needs and constraints. A feature that is desirable for one group may be essential for another, and expectations can change over time. This reinforces the need to understand people rather than assume that one solution or feature set will work for everyone. 

The session also introduced the idea that frameworks are decision-support tools, not decisions by themselves. Frameworks such as Jobs to Be Done, Double Diamond, opportunity trees, RICE, Kano and MoSCoW can support different stages or decisions. However, using too many frameworks can create repetition without adding value. The product manager must choose the framework that best fits the question and available evidence. 

Another strong takeaway was the distinction between incremental improvement and transformation. Transformation should create a significantly different level of value rather than simply being presented as a small improvement with a new label. The discussion on launch readiness was equally relevant: a product does not need to be perfect, but deciding that it is “good enough” to launch must be a deliberate, strategic decision. Limited availability can help a team learn with selected customers, but general availability is an important step towards making the product broadly available. Teams must also preserve their energy for the work that continues after beta and launch. 

My personal reflection is that good product management requires disciplined thinking without becoming overly processdriven. I should be able to explain the reason behind a decision, understand whose needs are being addressed, and measure progress through outcomes rather than feature count. The session reinforced that frameworks, documentation and launch plans are valuable only when they help the team create and deliver meaningful stakeholder value.
