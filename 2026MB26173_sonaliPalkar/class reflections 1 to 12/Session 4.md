## **MANAGEMENT OF AI PRODUCTS** 

# **Session 4 – Identifying and Evaluating AI Product Value** 

The fourth session helped me understand that AI should not be adopted simply because the technology is available or appears innovative. The starting point should always be the problem we are trying to solve and the value created for a clearly identified customer segment. A solution may be technically impressive but still fail if it does not address a real need or fit the market. The discussion on product-market fit reminded me that assumptions about customers must be validated rather than based on our own preferences. 

One important learning was to evaluate opportunities through a structured and consistent framework. When both AI systems and subject-matter experts assess ideas, they may have different biases and priorities. A common evaluation rubric, clearly defined scoring criteria and a transparent method for combining scores can make comparisons more consistent. However, a score should support decision-making, not replace judgement. The criteria and assumptions also need to be reviewed, and checks should be used to confirm that the framework is being followed. 

I also found the discussion on adoption costs valuable. Customers may resist a new product if it disrupts existing workflows, requires retraining or makes switching difficult. Therefore, product value must be strong enough to justify the effort and cost of adoption. The product manager should also consider the human and social impact of automation. Saving time is not always valuable if the task itself is meaningful to the user, and employees may resist automation if they believe it is intended mainly to reduce jobs. 

Another key takeaway was the importance of comparing an AI solution with suitable non-AI or existing-process baselines. This helps establish whether AI is genuinely improving outcomes. In regulated or audit-sensitive environments, explainability and evidence may be essential parts of the customer’s workflow. A tool that delivers a result but cannot provide the required justification may not meet the customer’s needs. 

My personal reflection is that product decisions require a balance of desirability, feasibility, viability, trust and responsible governance. As a product manager, I should remain focused on the user’s actual problem instead of becoming attached to a particular technology. Overall, the session reinforced that AI creates product value only when it solves a meaningful problem, fits the target market and delivers measurable benefits responsibly.
