## **MANAGEMENT OF AI PRODUCTS** 

# **Session 5 – AI Product Architecture, Portfolio Management and Human Oversight** 

The fifth session connected product strategy with the practical decisions involved in building and managing AI-enabled solutions. I learned to view an AI product as a complete system rather than focusing only on the model. The discussion highlighted important layers such as data, models and the application or workflow through which users receive value. Product managers may rely on technical specialists for detailed estimates, but they remain accountable for understanding the product economics, including usage costs, pricing and the value delivered to customers. 

The session also reinforced the importance of product-market fit. A product must suit a clearly defined customer segment, and assumptions about users, usage and value need to be validated. Reliability and trust are equally important because even a small failure can damage confidence. I found the discussion on product portfolio management useful. A portfolio manager oversees multiple products and should provide direction without micromanaging individual product owners. A regular operating cadence creates stability while still allowing priorities to change as market needs evolve. 

The discussion on build, buy and partner decisions was particularly relevant to organisations that depend on external vendors. When several vendor products are inputs to an organisation’s final offering or internal process, the organisation must manage those relationships carefully. It should be clear whether a product is being procured for internal use or sold as an independent offering, because the responsibilities and commercial expectations are different. 

My strongest learning from this session was about human-in-the-loop design. AI should operate independently only when its reliability and confidence justify it. In uncertain or high-impact situations, human review and escalation may be necessary. However, human involvement should add real value rather than become a routine approval step. The system should provide the reviewer with the reason for escalation, relevant evidence and context, and should record approvals, corrections and decisions. The session also highlighted human fatigue and the risk of people accepting AI recommendations without applying their judgement. Clear accountability and appropriate performance measures are therefore essential. 

Finally, I learned a practical approach to prompt design: define the role, context, task and constraints (RCTC). Prompts should be tested, refined and version-controlled rather than treated as one-time instructions. 

My overall takeaway is that successful AI product management combines sound economics, disciplined portfolio decisions, reliable workflows and meaningful human oversight. Technology creates value only when the complete experience is designed around the needs and responsibilities of its users.
