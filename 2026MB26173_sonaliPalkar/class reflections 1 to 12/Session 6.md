## **MANAGEMENT OF AI PRODUCTS** 

# **Session 6 – Product Strategy, Frameworks and Adaptive Product Leadership** 

The sixth session brought together several important aspects of product management and helped me see how a product manager must continuously balance customer value, business priorities, technology and people. One of the strongest messages was that product progress should be measured against outcomes agreed with stakeholders, not simply by the number of features delivered. A feature has value only when the intended customer uses it and it helps achieve the desired outcome. 

I found the discussion on building a product for multiple customers particularly useful. A solution created for one organisation may be a good starting point, but it becomes a product only when its core can serve a wider market. Customisations for a particular customer should be layered carefully without compromising the core product. This requires understanding the target market, knowing when to say no to highly specific requests, and periodically checking whether the product is still aligned with its original vision. Since customer expectations, competitors and technology keep changing, product managers must make timely course corrections. 

The session also clarified that frameworks are decision-support tools, not substitutes for judgement. Jobs to Be Done, customer journey mapping, Double Diamond, Opportunity Solution Trees, Lean Canvas, PESTLE, SWOT, Porter’s Five Forces, QFD, MoSCoW and other tools can help answer different questions. The important thing is to select only the frameworks that add value rather than creating unnecessary process. I also learned that prioritisation involves balancing customer impact, reach, confidence and effort, while roadmaps communicate the product’s longer-term direction. 

Another key learning was how to manage uncertainty while protecting the team’s ability to deliver. Shorter Scrum sprints may suit highly dynamic environments, while more stable work can allow longer cycles. During an agreed sprint, the team needs stability; changes can be considered at the next planning point, or a sprint may be stopped when continuing would create greater waste. Decision tools such as RACI/RASCI, reversible versus irreversible decisions, blast-radius assessment, pre-mortems and decision journals help clarify ownership and manage risk. The product manager may delegate responsibility, but accountability cannot be delegated. 

I also took away that product leadership is not only about making decisions. It involves creating an environment where people can explore, learn and contribute, while protecting the team from unrealistic demands. Pricing should reflect the value delivered to customers and leave a fair incentive for both the customer and the product provider. Finally, AI increases the pace of change, so continuous learning and experimentation are essential. 

My personal takeaway is that effective product management requires a clear vision but a flexible approach to execution. I should remain focused on measurable customer outcomes, question assumptions, document the reasoning behind important decisions and adapt when evidence or market conditions change. The session reinforced that successful products are built not just through features and frameworks, but through sound judgement, responsible leadership and sustained customer value.
