## **MANAGEMENT OF AI PRODUCTS** 

# **Session 7 – Finding AI Opportunities, Defining Value and Product Specifications** 

The seventh session helped me connect AI product ideas with the discipline of defining measurable value. The key message I took away is that AI should be introduced only when it creates a meaningful improvement for the intended stakeholders. Adding AI as a label is not enough; customers will pay for the value they receive, not simply for the technology used. This makes it important to identify the actual problem, understand the people affected and define what success should look like. 

I found the discussion on measurable requirements particularly useful. Statements such as “improve accuracy” or “reduce effort” are too broad unless the product team specifies how accuracy or effort will be measured, the baseline, the target and the conditions under which the result will be assessed. Clear measures reduce ambiguity and make it easier for teams to evaluate whether the product is meeting expectations. I also understood the importance of writing requirements in a way that can be checked rather than leaving interpretation to individual team members. 

The session connected product value with commercial viability. Pricing decisions should consider what the customer is willing to pay, the cost of building and operating the solution, expected volumes and a fair margin. The product manager must understand the customer’s context rather than apply generic pricing logic. A solution for an essential, cost-sensitive service provider, for example, may need a different value proposition from a premium consumer product. 

I also learned the practical value of workflow visualisation. Swimlane diagrams and diagramming-as-code tools such as PlantUML or Mermaid can make responsibilities, hand-offs and parallel activities easier to understand. Keeping diagrams in a text-based format also makes changes easier to track and maintain. The discussion on using AI to review product documents reminded me that AI can help identify gaps and ambiguities, but the product team remains responsible for the quality and completeness of the specification. 

My personal takeaway is to move from an interesting AI idea to a clear, testable and commercially sensible product proposal. I should be able to explain whose problem is being solved, what measurable outcome will improve, how the workflow will change and why the economics make sense. This session reinforced that strong product management depends on clarity, evidence and disciplined communication.
